**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Supreme Court; Article 142; civil liberties; right to protest; police accountability
- GS Paper II → Governance → National Testing Agency; public examination reforms; NEET-UG

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Supreme Court invoked Article 142 of the Constitution to quash all FIRs registered against Gen Z students who had organised demonstrations against alleged irregularities in NEET-UG 2026. RTI activist Saurav Das had approached the court; Delhi Police, which had initially resisted, executed a complete reversal. The Chief Justice of India (CJP) consequently withdrew the planned September 5 protest march. The court retained the option for fresh FIRs against 2,873 protesters with criminal antecedents. The Centre was directed to frame a pan-India policy for welfare of families of students who died due to NEET paper leaks. This development is significant for UPSC as it tests the interplay between the right to protest, the Supreme Court's plenary constitutional powers, police accountability, and the systemic failures of India's high-stakes examination ecosystem under the National Testing Agency.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 142 — Complete Justice:</strong> Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order necessary for "doing complete justice" in any pending matter. It is a plenary constitutional power that can override statutory provisions and is broader than the inherent power to quash FIRs under Section 528 BNSS. Landmark uses include the Bhopal gas tragedy settlement (1989) and the Ram Janmabhoomi judgment (2019).</li>
<li><strong>NEET-UG and NTA Crisis:</strong> NEET-UG is the single national entrance examination for undergraduate medical admissions, conducted by the National Testing Agency (NTA) under the Ministry of Education. The 2024 paper leak controversy and grace-marks irregularities triggered massive protests. A High-Level Review Committee under former ISRO chairman K. Radhakrishnan was constituted to recommend NTA reforms and examine structural vulnerabilities in the examination ecosystem.</li>
<li><strong>Right to Protest under the Constitution:</strong> The right to peaceful protest is a fundamental right under Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech and expression) and Article 19(1)(b) (right to assemble peaceably without arms), subject to reasonable restrictions under Articles 19(2) and 19(3). The Supreme Court affirmed in Ramlila Maidan Incident (2012) that police cannot disperse peaceful assemblies without lawful justification.</li>
<li><strong>FIR Framework under BNSS 2023:</strong> First Information Reports are governed by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. Section 173 BNSS mandates compulsory registration of FIRs for cognisable offences. Courts can quash FIRs under Section 528 BNSS (inherent power) or under Articles 226/32, apart from the Supreme Court's broader Article 142 power.</li>
<li><strong>Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024:</strong> Enacted specifically to criminalise malpractice in public examinations, this law provides for imprisonment of up to 10 years and fines up to ₹1 crore. It covers exam paper leaks, impersonation, and organised cheating networks. The direction to frame a welfare policy for affected families signals judicial recognition that systemic failures also create victim-class obligations on the state.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → SCO; India-Pakistan relations; India-Central Asia; multilateral diplomacy; counter-terrorism
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Cross-border terrorism; state-sponsored terror

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Prime Minister Narendra Modi addressed the 26th Summit of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) in Bishkek, Kyrgyz Republic, calling on member states to dismantle the entire ecosystem of terrorism — not merely its visible manifestations. Pakistan, which assumed the SCO Chair for 2026-27, was the implicit target of India's counter-terror messaging. The summit adopted the Bishkek Declaration, which addressed the Taliban regime in Afghanistan, the West Asia conflict, and reaffirmed respect for Iran's territorial sovereignty. The SCO setting underscores a structural tension: India and Pakistan — nuclear-armed adversaries — must cooperate on regional security and trade within the same multilateral framework. India consistently uses SCO platforms to advocate for a universal, non-selective definition of terrorism, countering Pakistan's alleged use of terror as a tool of state policy and pushing back on any distinction between "good" and "bad" terrorist organisations.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO):</strong> Founded in 2001, the SCO is a Eurasian political, economic, and security bloc. Permanent members include China, Russia, India, Pakistan, Iran, and four Central Asian states. India became a full member in 2017 at the Astana summit. The SCO Charter recognises the "Shanghai Spirit" of mutual trust, non-interference, and respect for civilisational diversity, and the organisation operates by consensus.</li>
<li><strong>Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS):</strong> RATS is the SCO's permanent body headquartered in Tashkent, Uzbekistan, tasked with coordinating counter-terrorism, counter-separatism, and counter-extremism efforts — the "three evils" in SCO parlance. India has used RATS to push for a comprehensive definition of terrorism that covers state-sponsored actors, directly targeting Pakistan's dual policy of cooperating on some terror while allegedly supporting others.</li>
<li><strong>Bishkek Declaration and Afghanistan:</strong> The declaration's reference to the Taliban-governed Afghanistan is significant: the SCO has not formally recognised the Taliban, yet Afghanistan borders several SCO states and its instability directly threatens regional security. The SCO's Tashkent Process and Contact Group on Afghanistan represent the organisation's attempts to engage without legitimising the Taliban regime.</li>
<li><strong>India-Pakistan Coexistence at SCO:</strong> Despite frozen bilateral ties, India and Pakistan participate in the same SCO structures. India has used SCO summits to signal isolation of Pakistan on terrorism — PM Modi notably did not hold bilateral meetings with Pakistan at SCO sidelines. The SCO thus functions as a limited multilateral channel even when direct diplomacy is suspended, an important dimension for UPSC questions on India's neighbourhood policy.</li>
<li><strong>India-Central Asia Connectivity via SCO:</strong> India uses SCO to advance the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC), Chabahar Port (Iran), and the Ashgabat Agreement as alternative trade routes to Central Asia bypassing Pakistan. The SCO framework also supports India's push for the Connect Central Asia policy and energy cooperation with Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">NCLT Special Bench stays relief to Subhash Chandra amid ₹22,006 crore insolvency claims</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code; NCLT; corporate governance; banking sector NPAs; creditor rights

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The NCLT's five-member Special Bench stayed relief previously granted to media baron Subhash Chandra (Essel Group) in an insolvency proceeding. Admitted claims against Chandra totalled ₹22,006.57 crore, against which a settlement of only ₹6.25 crore had been proposed — representing a haircut exceeding 99%. Dissenting financial creditors LIC Housing Finance and Union Bank of India challenged the resolution plan, arguing it inadequately protected their interests. The matter has also been challenged before the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT). Indiabulls Housing Finance had initiated the original proceedings. This case tests the IBC framework's robustness in ensuring equitable treatment of financial creditors and the appellate oversight capacity of NCLT's Special Bench mechanism in complex multi-creditor insolvency proceedings involving high-profile corporate debtors.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016:</strong> The IBC created a unified, time-bound framework for resolving corporate insolvencies, replacing earlier BIFR and SICA regimes. Resolution must be completed within 330 days including litigation. The Code prioritises financial creditors over operational creditors and equity holders in the distribution waterfall. It is administered through NCLT, NCLAT, and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI).</li>
<li><strong>National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT):</strong> Established under the Companies Act, 2013 (effective 2016), NCLT is a quasi-judicial body adjudicating corporate insolvency, mergers, and company law disputes. Special Benches with five members are constituted for complex multi-creditor matters. NCLT replaced the Company Law Board and Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction (BIFR). Orders are appealable to NCLAT.</li>
<li><strong>Committee of Creditors (CoC) and Haircuts:</strong> Under IBC, the Committee of Creditors — comprising financial creditors weighted by their admitted claims — approves resolution plans by a 66% supermajority. The Supreme Court in Essar Steel (2019) held that the CoC has commercial wisdom over haircut decisions, but plans must not discriminate arbitrarily between similarly-situated creditors. Average IBC haircuts have ranged from 40% to 80% across resolved cases; a 99% haircut is extreme.</li>
<li><strong>NCLAT and Appellate Architecture:</strong> The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal hears appeals from NCLT under the IBC and the Companies Act. Further appeals on questions of law lie to the Supreme Court. The two-tier structure is intended for expeditious resolution, but high-profile cases with dissenting creditors and challenged resolutions frequently traverse all three levels over multiple years.</li>
<li><strong>Essel Group and Media Sector Insolvency:</strong> The Subhash Chandra-led Essel Group includes Zee Entertainment, Dish TV, and other media and infrastructure assets. The group's debt crisis, which peaked around 2019-2020, led to promoter stake sales and creditor settlements across multiple entities. IBC proceedings against promoter entities — as distinct from operating companies — test whether the Code can reach personal guarantees and holding company structures effectively.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<li><strong>Varsen Aghabekian Shahin:</strong> Varsen Aghabekian Shahin is a Palestinian-Armenian politician who serves as Foreign Minister of the State of Palestine under the Palestinian Authority. <h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">No metro, costly cab rides: the long road to Jewar's Noida International Airport</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Infrastructure</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Infrastructure → Airport development; urban connectivity; last-mile transport; PPP models

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Noida International Airport at Jewar, Uttar Pradesh, began commercial operations from June 15, but passengers face severe last-mile connectivity gaps. Metro connectivity to the airport does not yet exist, and cab fares range from ₹1,200 to ₹3,500 from central Delhi-NCR. UPSRTC buses provide limited public transport but remain inadequate. The article exposes a recurring issue in Indian infrastructure planning: airports, highways, and ports inaugurated before complementary connectivity infrastructure is ready. This planning gap increases user costs, reduces utilisation of expensive public investments, and reflects coordination failures among NHAI, metro authorities, airport concessionaires, and state transport agencies.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Noida International Airport (Jewar):</strong> Developed under a PPP concession by Zurich Airport International AG, Jewar airport is meant to decongest IGI Delhi and serve western UP and the Yamuna Expressway industrial corridor. It was approved under India's Greenfield Airports Policy, 2008, which requires NOC from MoCA and state government support agreements.</li>
<li><strong>Greenfield Airports Policy, 2008:</strong> Provides the regulatory framework for new airports developed through PPP. Key conditions include Viability Gap Funding (VGF) eligibility for commercially unviable routes, open access to airport services, and mandatory coordination with state governments on connectivity. India now has 20+ operational greenfield airports under this policy.</li>
<li><strong>Last-Mile Connectivity Gap:</strong> The Ministry of Civil Aviation's UDAN scheme targets regional air connectivity but does not directly fund urban last-mile links. The Economic Survey has noted that airport-to-city connectivity — metro, bus rapid transit, expressway — is critical for airport utilisation yet chronically underfunded relative to terminal construction.</li>
<li><strong>UPSRTC:</strong> The Uttar Pradesh State Road Transport Corporation is the state-owned bus operator. State RTCs across India run aging fleets with low frequency, making them structurally inadequate for time-sensitive airport connectivity compared to metros and private taxis.</li>
<li><strong>YEIDA and Coordination Challenges:</strong> The Yamuna Expressway Industrial Development Authority oversees Jewar airport's zone. Effective last-mile connectivity requires coordination across YEIDA, NMRC (Noida Metro), NHAI, the airport concessionaire, and UPSRTC — a classic multi-agency coordination problem in Indian infrastructure governance.</li>
</ul>
</div>

West Bengal BJP leader and Leader of Opposition Suvendu Adhikari, at a Kolkata Press Club event, cautioned journalists against "compromising on patriotism." Simultaneously, the West Bengal government announced journalist welfare measures: health insurance of ₹5 lakh combining Ayushman Bharat and the state's Mukhyamantri Swasthya Bima Yojana, and a monthly pension of ₹5,000 for retired journalists. The Press Club welcomed the welfare announcement. The article reflects two overlapping debates: the social security deficit for journalists working informally without employer benefits, and the political pressure on editorial independence when political leaders frame patriotism as a journalistic duty. Both dimensions are relevant to UPSC questions on press freedom, civil society, and social protection for informal workers.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY:</strong> Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana provides ₹5 lakh per family per year for hospitalisation to economically vulnerable households, covering ~55 crore beneficiaries. States supplement it with their own schemes; West Bengal's Mukhyamantri Swasthya Bima Yojana extends coverage to groups not on the central list.</li>
<li><strong>Working Journalists Act, 1955:</strong> The Working Journalists (Conditions of Service) and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1955, and the Working Journalists (Fixation of Rates of Wages) Act, 1958, govern conditions for journalists in newspaper establishments. These laws do not cover digital or freelance journalists, leaving a large coverage gap in an era when most new journalism happens online.</li>
<li><strong>Press Freedom in India:</strong> India ranks in the lower bracket of Reporters Without Borders' World Press Freedom Index. Article 19(1)(a) guarantees press freedom as a facet of freedom of speech; the Supreme Court has held that this right cannot be diluted by extra-legal pressure. Political leaders framing "patriotism" as a journalistic standard has been critiqued as soft censorship.</li>
<li><strong>State Journalist Welfare Boards:</strong> Several states — Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Kerala — maintain journalist welfare boards providing accident insurance, medical aid, and scholarships for journalists' children. These boards are funded through state budgets and administered by press councils or welfare trusts. West Bengal's announced pension scheme would be among the more generous in this category.</li>
<li><strong>Suvendu Adhikari — Leader of Opposition, WB:</strong> Suvendu Adhikari is the BJP MLA from Nandigram and Leader of Opposition in the West Bengal Legislative Assembly. He defected from TMC in 2020 and defeated Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee in the 2021 assembly election in Nandigram (though the CM won a by-election subsequently). His statements on media patriotism are part of BJP's broader political messaging in WB ahead of the 2026 assembly elections.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
UP Minister Manoj Pandey led BJP's objection to a Samajwadi Party hoarding outside SP's Lucknow office featuring an image of Lord Ram and alleging theft of donations to the Ram Temple Trust. BJP characterised the hoarding as an insult to the Brahmin community and demanded its removal. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">

Aravind, found that well water use was a statistically significant risk factor for Amoebic Meningoencephalitis (AME), a rare but almost universally fatal brain infection caused by the Naegleria fowleri amoeba. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Naegleria fowleri and AME:</strong> Naegleria fowleri is a free-living amoeba found in warm freshwater bodies — lakes, rivers, poorly maintained pools, and open wells. It causes Primary Amoebic Meningoencephalitis (PAM/AME) when water enters the nose, usually during head bathing or swimming. There is currently no effective treatment protocol, though miltefosine has shown limited promise.</li>
<li><strong>ICMR (Indian Council of Medical Research):</strong> ICMR is India's apex body for biomedical and health research, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. It funds, coordinates, and conducts research across its network of 26 institutes and 6 regional centres. ICMR-state government joint studies represent a key mechanism for translating national research capacity into state-level public health policy.</li>
<li><strong>Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM):</strong> Launched in 2019, JJM aims to provide functional household tap connections to all rural households by 2024, supplying safe and adequate drinking water (55 litres per capita per day). As of 2024, over 14 crore rural households have been covered. Transition from open wells to piped treated water supply is a direct public health intervention that would reduce AME risk — connecting this study's findings to ongoing national infrastructure policy.</li>
<li><strong>Population Attributable Fraction (PAF):</strong> PAF is an epidemiological measure indicating the proportion of disease cases in a population that can be attributed to a specific risk factor (here, well water use). <li><strong>Kerala's AME Burden:</strong> Kerala has had a disproportionately high number of AME deaths in India relative to its population, prompting heightened surveillance. The state has been a pioneer in disease surveillance through its Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) network.</li>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Passerine Birds and Vocal Learning:</strong> Passerines (order Passeriformes) constitute the largest order of birds — over 6,000 of ~10,000 known bird species. They are defined by their foot structure (three toes forward, one back) and are virtually all capable of vocal learning — acquiring and modifying songs through neural mechanisms in the forebrain analogous to human language learning. Oscines (true songbirds) within passerines have the most complex vocal repertoires.</li>
<li><strong>Modulation Power Spectrum (MPS):</strong> MPS is an acoustic analysis technique that characterises how sound's amplitude and frequency modulate over time. Applied to birdsong, it reveals acoustic structures invisible to simple spectrograms. By decomposing thousands of songs into MPS components and applying machine learning clustering, the researchers could identify common acoustic patterns (the eight motifs) that recur across phylogenetically distant species — suggesting evolutionary convergence in acoustic signalling.</li>
<li><strong>Machine Learning in Biodiversity Research:</strong> ML techniques — clustering algorithms, neural networks, convolutional networks for spectrogram analysis — are transforming bioacoustics research. Platforms like Xeno-canto (crowdsourced bird sound recordings) and eBird (Cornell Lab) provide the large datasets necessary. India Ecoacoustics Network is developing similar capacity for Indian bird and ecosystem monitoring — relevant to India's biodiversity targets under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF).</li>
<li><strong>Tropical vs. Temperate Bird Songs:</strong> Tropical species like many Indian forest birds tend to have more complex, longer songs due to year-round breeding seasons (no need for rapid mate attraction in short breeding windows). Temperate species often have simpler, louder songs optimised for penetrating dense foliage and long-distance signalling. The study's finding that the same eight motifs appear in both, but in different proportions, suggests a shared acoustics framework with ecological adaptations.</li>
<li><strong>Ecoacoustics and Environmental Monitoring:</strong> The field of ecoacoustics uses soundscape monitoring to assess ecosystem health — biodiversity indices from sound recordings can track habitat quality, species richness, and responses to environmental disturbance. India's Forest Survey of India and Wildlife Institute of India have begun incorporating acoustic monitoring in biodiversity assessments, with applications for the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 and National Biodiversity Authority mandates.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Wakefield accelerator: table-top particle accelerator using plasma waves explained</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Physics</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Particle physics; plasma physics; accelerator technology; India's participation in CERN

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Science page explains Wakefield accelerators — compact particle accelerators that use plasma waves to accelerate charged particles to high energies. A laser pulse fired through plasma creates a wake of alternating electric fields; electrons "surf" these fields and gain enormous energy over short distances. A Wakefield accelerator can fit atop a table, unlike the 27-kilometre Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN. However, Wakefield accelerators currently cannot exceed energies of a few tens of GeV, while the LHC reaches multiple TeV — making them complementary rather than replacement technologies. For UPSC, the article is relevant to understanding India's scientific collaboration with CERN (India is an associate member state) and the distinction between different particle accelerator technologies that appear in prelims science questions.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN:</strong> The LHC is the world's largest and highest-energy particle collider, a 27-km ring at the France-Switzerland border operated by CERN (European Organisation for Nuclear Research). It accelerates protons to 6.5-7 TeV and collides them to study fundamental particles. Its discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012 was a landmark in particle physics. India became a CERN Associate Member State in 2017, gaining participation rights in governance and research.</li>
<li><strong>Plasma and Wakefield Acceleration:</strong> Plasma is the fourth state of matter — a gas of ionised particles. When a laser pulse or a relativistic particle beam traverses plasma, it displaces electrons, creating a region of excess positive charge (ions) that draws electrons back, generating a travelling electric field "wake" behind the driver. This wake can accelerate "witness" electrons to energies of tens of GeV over distances of a few centimetres — the key advantage over conventional radiofrequency accelerators that need kilometres of infrastructure.</li>
<li><strong>Limitations of Wakefield Accelerators:</strong> Current Wakefield accelerators achieve energies of a few tens of GeV — useful for specific physics experiments, medical applications (compact cancer treatment accelerators), and materials science, but far below the TeV energies of the LHC. Beam quality (emittance) and energy spread are also inferior to conventional accelerators. Research to overcome these limitations — through staging multiple plasma stages — is ongoing globally.</li>
<li><strong>India's Particle Physics Facilities:</strong> India's main particle physics infrastructure includes the Variable Energy Cyclotron Centre (VECC) in Kolkata, the Superconducting Cyclotron at VECC, TIFR's pelletron accelerator, and RRCAT (Raja Ramanna Centre for Advanced Technology) in Indore, which operates electron beam accelerators and synchrotron radiation sources. India's participation in CERN and FAIR (Facility for Antiproton and Ion Research, Germany) connects these to global frontier research.</li>
<li><strong>Applications of Compact Accelerators:</strong> Beyond fundamental physics, compact accelerators (including Wakefield types) have applications in medical isotope production, cancer radiotherapy (proton/carbon ion therapy), industrial X-ray imaging, sterilisation of medical equipment, and food irradiation. India's DAE (Department of Atomic Energy) has programmes to develop domestic accelerator-based applications, making this technology directly relevant to national science policy goals.</li>
</ul>
</div>

The portal was mandated to facilitate delivery of welfare schemes but poor financial incentive to register and awareness gaps have led to low gig worker uptake relative to total numbers.</li>
<li><strong>Gig Economy in India — Scale:</strong> India's gig/platform economy has grown dramatically with digital platform adoption. Zomato, Swiggy, Ola, Uber, Porter, Urban Company, and Dunzo employ millions of delivery, ride-hailing, and home services workers. NITI Aayog's 2022 estimate of 77 lakh workers in FY21 (growing to 2.35 crore by 2029-30) is widely cited. These workers typically lack PF, ESI, paid leave, or severance — making them among the most socially insecure segments of India's labour force.</li>
<li><strong>Rajasthan Gig Worker Protection Act, 2023:</strong> Rajasthan became the first state to legislate specifically for gig workers, passing the Rajasthan Platform Based Gig Workers (Registration and Welfare) Act, 2023. It mandated registration of gig workers, established a welfare board, and required platform companies to contribute a fee per transaction to the welfare fund. Karnataka has also considered similar legislation. These state-level initiatives have emerged partly because the Central Labour Code has not been fully implemented.</li>
<li><strong>Platform Companies' Resistance:</strong> Major platform aggregators have resisted being classified as "employers" (which would trigger full ESI/PF obligations) and prefer the "aggregator" label under the Code on Social Security, which carries lighter contribution obligations. The gig worker welfare debate globally — UK Supreme Court's Uber ruling (2021), EU Platform Work Directive — provides international precedents that Indian courts and legislators are watching closely.</li>
</ul>

Tikender Singh Panwar analyses the NEET-UG protests at Jantar Mantar, Delhi, as evidence that Gen Z is not apolitical — but is politically organised differently from older generations. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Bluetooth Mesh Networks in Protest Coordination:</strong> Bluetooth mesh networks allow devices to communicate with each other directly (device-to-device) without Wi-Fi or cellular infrastructure, using Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) protocols. Apps like Meshtastic and Briar have been used in Hong Kong protests (2019) and Bangladesh student protests (2024) during internet shutdowns. Despite this ruling, shutdown orders under the Temporary Suspension of Telecom Services (Public Emergency or Public Safety) Rules, 2017 continue to be issued, often without adequate judicial oversight.</li>
<li><strong>Students' Unions and Campus Democracy:</strong> Students' unions are recognised by the Lyngdoh Committee (2006) recommendations as essential for campus democracy and civic education. The Committee recommended elections on every campus following a code of conduct. Tamil Nadu and West Bengal have effectively banned student union elections (citing violence), while states like Kerala, Karnataka, and Delhi maintain vibrant students' union ecosystems. The Lyngdoh recommendations are advisory, not legally binding.</li>
<li><strong>Neoliberalism and Education Privatisation:</strong> Since the 1990s, India's higher education has seen rapid privatisation — private engineering, medical, and management colleges outnumber government institutions. This market model has created fee pressure, increased the stakes of competitive exams (NEET, JEE, UPSC), and shifted the student experience from campus community to individual competitive anxiety. Critics argue this has atomised students and depoliticised campuses, making organised collective action less natural.</li>
<li><strong>Gen Z Political Participation — Global Context:</strong> Globally, Gen Z (born mid-1990s to early 2010s) has shown both lower rates of conventional political participation (party membership, voting in younger cohorts) and higher rates of non-conventional activism (climate protests, social media campaigns, issue-specific movements). The NEET protests represent this non-conventional activation — driven by direct economic and educational grievance rather than partisan politics.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>

- GS Paper II → Governance → Animal rights; judicial activism; Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Authors from ALPN Research Foundation argue that India's legal framework treats animals inconsistently — wild animals get some protection under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, but domestic animals (including captive elephants and street dogs) face arbitrary treatment. They cite two recent cases: Jayakrishna Menon vs. Krishnankutty & Ors — involving elephant Raman's welfare in Kerala — and Re: City Hounded by Strays (street dog removal by municipal bodies). The article applies philosopher Peter Singer's "equal consideration of interests" principle — that capacity to suffer, not species membership, should determine moral consideration — and points to the Swiss Constitution's recognition of animal dignity as a model. India's Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 is criticised as outdated and inadequate for modern animal welfare standards.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 (PCA Act):</strong> India's primary animal welfare legislation, the PCA Act prohibits causing unnecessary suffering to animals and established the Animal Welfare Board of India (AWBI). However, its penalties are minimal (maximum ₹50 fine for first offence) and enforcement is patchy. The Animal Cruelty Prevention and Animal Welfare Act has been drafted as a replacement with higher penalties, but has not been passed. The Act's anthropocentric framing — protecting animals from human cruelty, not recognising animals as rights-holders — is the philosophical gap the authors address.</li>
<li><strong>Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 (WPA):</strong> WPA provides legal protection to wildlife through schedules of protected species (Schedule I affords highest protection). It prohibits hunting, trapping, and trade in protected species, and establishes national parks and wildlife sanctuaries. The Act is enforced by state forest departments and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau. The Supreme Court has used WPA as a basis for animal welfare judgments but its scope is limited to wild animals — domestic and captive animals fall outside its main provisions.</li>
<li><strong>Peter Singer's Equal Consideration Principle:</strong> In "Animal Liberation" (1975), philosopher Peter Singer argued that the capacity to suffer — not species membership, intelligence, or personhood — is the morally relevant criterion for equal consideration. Speciesism (discriminating based on species alone) is analogous to racism and sexism. Singer's utilitarian framework has influenced animal rights jurisprudence globally, including the Swiss Constitution's animal dignity clause (Article 120) and EU treaty provisions on animal sentience.</li>
<li><strong>India's Supreme Court on Animal Rights:</strong> The Supreme Court has progressively expanded animal rights protections through judicial activism. In Nagaraja v. State of Tamil Nadu (2014), the Court recognised that animals have the right to live with dignity and banned jallikattu (later overturned by a Constitutional amendment). In Jallikattu Amendment case (2023), the SC upheld Tamil Nadu's amendment allowing jallikattu under regulations. The tension between cultural traditions and animal welfare is a live constitutional question.</li>
<li><strong>Street Dogs — ABC Rules and Municipal Conflict:</strong> The Animal Birth Control (Dogs) Rules, 2001 (under PCA Act) prohibit municipalities from killing stray dogs; they must use Animal Birth Control (ABC) — catch, sterilise, vaccinate, release. Municipal corporations have frequently challenged this, citing public safety after dog bite incidents. The Supreme Court has upheld ABC Rules against municipal killing orders in multiple cases, balancing animal welfare with public health concerns.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Indian Society → Caste system; untouchability; Dalit rights; social exclusion
- GS Paper II → Polity → Article 17; SC/ST Atrocities Act; Protection of Civil Rights Act; judicial interpretation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Sahil Hussain Chaudhury examines a "shuddhikaran" (purification) ritual performed at a Ramlila ground in Haldwani on August 10, 2026 — two days after Congress president Mallikarjun Kharge had addressed a rally at the same venue. Upper-caste groups performed the ritual citing "pollution" by a Dalit man's presence, invoking ancient caste purity norms that Article 17 of the Constitution explicitly abolishes. The article traces the legal framework: Article 17 abolishes untouchability; the Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955 (renamed Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955) criminalises it; the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 adds further teeth. Key cases cited include Sukanya Shantha v Union of India (2024) — on caste-based prison treatment; Surya Narayan Choudhary v State of Rajasthan (1988) — on temple access; Hitesh Verma v State of Uttarakhand (2020); and a 2026 Delhi HC case.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 17 — Abolition of Untouchability:</strong> Article 17 declares that untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. Enforcement of any disability arising from untouchability is an offence punishable by law. The provision is enforceable against private individuals (not just the state), making it one of the few horizontal fundamental rights in the Constitution. The "shuddhikaran" ritual explicitly enacts the caste "purity/pollution" distinction that Article 17 is designed to eradicate.</li>
<li><strong>Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955:</strong> Originally enacted as the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, renamed in 1976, the PCRA criminalises the enforcement of social disabilities based on untouchability — denial of access to temples, public places, shops, water sources, and schools. Section 7(1)(d) specifically punishes insult or injury to a person on the ground of untouchability. However, penalties under PCRA are relatively minor (maximum 6 months imprisonment), which is why the more stringent SC/ST Act is usually invoked.</li>
<li><strong>SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989:</strong> The PoA Act provides for stringent punishment for specific atrocities against SC/ST members — including forced consumption of offensive substances, intimidation, sexual assault, and social boycott. Section 3(1)(r) criminalises intentional insult or intimidation by a member of a non-SC/ST community against an SC/ST person in any public place. The PoA Act has its own Special Courts for speedy trial and mandates that police officers must register FIRs without delay when complaints are filed.</li>
<li><strong>Sukanya Shantha v. Union of India (2024):</strong> In this landmark judgment, the Supreme Court struck down caste-based labour segregation in Indian prisons — where "lower" castes were assigned cleaning and sanitation duties while "upper" castes received cooking duties — holding it a violation of Article 17 and Articles 14, 15, and 21. The Court ordered state prison manuals to be revised. This case established that caste-based "purity and pollution" frameworks embedded in institutional rules are constitutionally impermissible.</li>
<li><strong>Public Manifestations of Caste Purity:</strong> The Haldwani incident is not isolated. Similar "shuddhi" or purification rituals at sites visited by Dalit leaders (including Congress gatherings) have been documented in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and UP. Such acts directly violate both Article 17 and the PoA Act. The political dimension — that the ritual was performed after Congress president Kharge (a Dalit) had spoken there — gives the incident an additional layer of targeted political and caste intimidation.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Vighnesh P. Venkitesh explains Thailand's Peaceful Society Promotion Act, passed in July 2026 and effective from August 24. The Act grants amnesty to participants in political protests between January 1, 2005 and July 16, 2025 — covering the turbulent era spanning the 2006 coup against Thaksin Shinawatra, the Red Shirt/Yellow Shirt conflicts, and the 2020-21 pro-democracy youth protests. However, the Act notably excludes lese majeste (Article 112 of Thai criminal code — insulting the monarchy, punishable by 3-15 years per count), murder, and corruption charges. Over 5,000 people have been prosecuted since 2006; approximately 54 remain in prison for political activities. Young activists who faced Article 112 charges in the 2020-21 protests are excluded from the amnesty. This illustrates the limits of transitional amnesty in politically complex democracies.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Thailand's Political History (2006-2026):</strong> Thailand has experienced serial political instability: the 2006 coup against PM Thaksin Shinawatra, the 2008 protests leading to PM change, the 2010 Red Shirt crackdown (91 killed), the 2014 coup by General Prayuth Chan-ocha, the 2020-21 youth pro-democracy protests demanding monarchy reform, the 2023 election won by Move Forward Party (later dissolved by courts), and the 2024 formation of government under Pheu Thai (Thaksin's party). The amnesty covers protests from all these episodes.</li>
<li><strong>Lese Majeste (Article 112):</strong> Thailand's lese majeste law criminalises insults to the King, Queen, Heir Apparent, or Regent with 3-15 years imprisonment per count. It has been used extensively against pro-democracy activists since the 2020-21 protests. Human rights organisations have documented cases where individuals face decades of cumulative prison time for social media posts. The Act's exclusion of Article 112 cases is its most contested aspect — effectively preventing full political reconciliation.</li>
<li><strong>Transitional Justice and Amnesty:</strong> Transitional justice refers to processes by which societies emerge from periods of political violence or systematic human rights violations — including truth commissions, reparations, prosecutions, and amnesties. Selective amnesties (as in Thailand, excluding serious crimes) are a middle path between blanket forgiveness (which enables impunity) and full prosecution (which can prevent political reconciliation). South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996-2003) is the benchmark for comparative analysis.</li>
<li><strong>Thaksin Shinawatra and Thai Politics:</strong> Thaksin, a telecom billionaire turned PM (2001-06), was ousted by a military coup in 2006 while abroad. His populist welfare policies (30-baht healthcare, village fund) built a strong rural base. His exile, the alternation of pro-Thaksin and anti-Thaksin governments, and his return to Thailand in 2023 (followed by immediate parole and medical leave) represent the central fault line of Thai politics. The amnesty benefits many of his supporters prosecuted in the 2006-2025 period.</li>
<li><strong>India-Thailand Relations in Context:</strong> India's Act East Policy prioritises ASEAN connectivity, and Thailand is a key partner for the India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway and the Mekong-Ganga Cooperation framework. Understanding Thailand's political dynamics — particularly the influence of military and monarchy on democratic institutions — is relevant for UPSC analysis of democratic transitions and India's engagement with Southeast Asian partners at different levels of democratic consolidation.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Soumya Sarkar analyses India's data centre capacity explosion — from 520 MW in 2020 to 1.5 GW currently — and projections of 6.5 GW by 2030. Electricity consumption will surge from 13 TWh (2024) to 57 TWh (2030); AI systems alone will add 26.3 GW of new demand by 2031-32. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India's Data Centre Policy Framework:</strong> The Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY) released Data Centre Policy guidelines in 2021 granting infrastructure status to data centres — enabling them to access priority power connections, bank loans at infrastructure rates, and land acquisition benefits. India's Personal Data Protection Act (now the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023) and data localisation requirements have incentivised companies to set up Indian data centres for processing data of Indian users.</li>
<li><strong>Energy Intensity of AI and Cloud Computing:</strong> Generative AI models require massive computational power — training a large language model (like GPT-4) consumes roughly 500-1,000 MWh, and inference (running queries) adds continuous load. Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Indian tech companies are rapidly expanding data centres in Hyderabad, Pune, Mumbai, and Chennai. AI's energy demand is projected to grow 10x over 2023-2030 levels, creating a structural challenge for clean energy commitments.</li>
<li><strong>Renewable Energy Curtailment in India:</strong> Curtailment occurs when renewable generation exceeds grid absorption capacity — grid operators must ramp down wind or solar plants rather than waste electricity. India curtailed 300 GW-hours in Q1 2026 due to inadequate transmission infrastructure and grid balancing challenges. Paradoxically, data centres' 24/7 baseload power demand could help absorb curtailed renewables if they could be powered by real-time renewable surplus rather than contracted coal power.</li>
<li><strong>Water Stress and Data Centre Cooling:</strong> Data centres use water-cooled chiller systems or evaporative cooling towers. A 100 MW facility consumes approximately 2 million litres per day — equivalent to the daily water needs of thousands of households. India's 21 river basins are classified as "water stressed" (NITI Aayog 2019), and data centre water demand in already water-stressed metropolitan areas (Bengaluru, Hyderabad) compounds urban water scarcity. Some data centres are shifting to air cooling or heat reuse technologies.</li>
<li><strong>Heat Island Effect and Urban Temperature:</strong> The urban heat island effect refers to the phenomenon where cities are significantly warmer than surrounding rural areas due to heat-absorbing infrastructure, reduced vegetation, and waste heat from human activities. The Cambridge study finding that data centres raise surface temperatures by 2°C within 10 km is significant — concentrated data centre zones (Navi Mumbai, Whitefield Bengaluru) risk creating micro-heat islands that increase cooling demand, in a vicious cycle. This intersects with India's National Action Plan on Climate Change and heat action plans.</li>
</ul>

Kerala's agriculture — rice, rubber, coconut, spices — is heavily rain-fed. The deficit also increases wildfire risk in Western Ghats biodiversity zones.</li>
<li><strong>Flash Floods in Jharkhand:</strong> Jharkhand's hilly terrain and high rainfall in the Chota Nagpur Plateau make it vulnerable to flash floods, particularly in August-September. Districts in the Subarnarekha, Damodar, and Koel-Karo river basins are the most affected. The state has significant Adivasi communities in flood-prone areas with limited disaster preparedness infrastructure. NDRF and SDRF deployments are the primary response mechanism, coordinated by NDMA guidelines.</li>

The 94.1% denial rate in June 2026 reflects these policy changes. For India, this affects Indian nationals seeking asylum in the US (a smaller but growing category), and India-US relations on migration and border management, increasingly a bilateral agenda item.</li>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 11 · Text &amp; Context</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Modern History → India's independence movement; interim government; Cabinet Mission Plan; Jawaharlal Nehru
- Prelims Paper I → Modern History → 1946 interim government

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
On this day in 1946 — September 2 — India's interim government headed by Jawaharlal Nehru formally began functioning, marking a critical milestone on the road to independence. The interim government was formed under the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946, which sought to keep India united under a federal structure. Nehru served as Vice President of the Executive Council (the Viceroy, Lord Wavell, remained as head). The Muslim League initially refused to join; it joined the interim government in October 1946. The interim government functioned from September 2, 1946 to August 14, 1947. This episode is a standard fact for UPSC Prelims and is frequently tested in the context of the Cabinet Mission Plan, the Mountbatten Plan, and the sequencing of events leading to India's partition and independence.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Cabinet Mission Plan (1946):</strong> The Cabinet Mission — comprising British Cabinet ministers Pethick-Lawrence, Stafford Cripps, and A.V. Alexander — arrived in India in March 1946. Its plan (May 16, 1946) rejected Pakistan as a separate nation but proposed a three-tier federal structure: an All-India Union (defence, foreign affairs, communications), Group level (provinces grouped into A-B-C groups), and individual provinces. Both Congress and the Muslim League initially accepted it but subsequently disagreed on interpretation.</li>
<li><strong>Interim Government (September 1946 — August 1947):</strong> Jawaharlal Nehru became Vice President of the Executive Council on September 2, 1946, effectively Prime Minister, with 14 members including Vallabhbhai Patel (Home), C. Rajagopalachari (Education), Liaquat Ali Khan (Finance — representing Muslim League when it joined in October). The interim government negotiated India's independence, participated in the Constituent Assembly, and managed the transition until August 14-15, 1947.</li>
<li><strong>Muslim League's Participation:</strong> The Muslim League initially boycotted the interim government and the Constituent Assembly, demanding Pakistan. After negotiations (and to prevent total exclusion from the transition process), the League joined the interim government in October 1946, with Liaquat Ali Khan as Finance Minister. The League used the Finance Ministry to obstruct Congress initiatives — an episode cited in arguments about the futility of coalition governance between the two parties.</li>
<li><strong>Lord Mountbatten and Partition Plan:</strong> Lord Wavell as Viceroy was replaced by Lord Mountbatten in March 1947. Mountbatten's plan — announced June 3, 1947 (Mountbatten Plan) — accepted partition, created the two dominions of India and Pakistan, and set August 15, 1947 as the date of independence. The interim government under Nehru transitioned into the government of independent India on that date.</li>
<li><strong>Constituent Assembly:</strong> The Constituent Assembly of India — elected indirectly by provincial assemblies in July 1946 — began its deliberations on December 9, 1946, chaired by Dr. Rajendra Prasad. It drafted the Constitution of India over 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days. The Muslim League initially boycotted it; the Assembly later became exclusively India's after partition, with Pakistan's provinces exiting.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">GST revenue grows 14.8% in August to nearly ₹2 lakh crore; imports component surges 29%</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Trade</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 12 · Business</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → GST; indirect tax revenues; fiscal policy; GST Council; inverted duty structure

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India's GST collections for August 2026 reached ₹1,99,853 crore — just shy of the ₹2 lakh crore milestone, representing a 14.8% year-on-year gross growth. Net collections grew 8.3%, domestic GST grew 9.3%, and import-related GST surged 29% — signalling strong import activity and trade volume. However, domestic refunds grew 72.6%, largely attributed to inverted duty structures where input goods are taxed higher than output goods, leading to excess input tax credit claims. The GST Council was scheduled to meet on September 12 to discuss rate rationalisation and address the inverted duty issue. For UPSC, this article tests understanding of GST's compositional structure (CGST, SGST, IGST, Compensation Cess), the inverted duty problem, and India's fiscal health through indirect tax receipts.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>GST Architecture (CGST, SGST, IGST, Cess):</strong> GST is a dual tax — CGST (Centre) and SGST (state) are levied simultaneously on intra-state supplies; IGST (Centre, proceeds shared with states) on inter-state supplies. The Compensation Cess (above 28% slab) was levied to compensate states for revenue loss during 2017-2022. India has five GST slabs: 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%, with essential goods at 0-5% and luxury/demerit goods at 28%+ cess.</li>
<li><strong>Inverted Duty Structure:</strong> An inverted duty structure occurs when the GST rate on inputs (raw materials) is higher than on outputs (finished goods), making input tax credit (ITC) claims exceed output tax liability. This results in accumulated ITC that businesses claim as refunds — inflating gross collections while net collections are lower. It is most pronounced in sectors like textiles (cotton inputs taxed at 5% but finished garments also at 5%), fertilisers, and some pharmaceuticals.</li>
<li><strong>GST Council:</strong> The GST Council is a constitutional body under Article 279A (inserted by 101st Constitutional Amendment, 2016) that makes recommendations on GST rates, exemptions, and administrative matters. It is chaired by the Union Finance Minister; state finance ministers are members. Decisions require a 3/4 weighted majority (Centre has 1/3 vote weight; states collectively have 2/3). All GST rate changes are Council recommendations, implemented through CGST/IGST/SGST Acts amendments.</li>
<li><strong>₹2 Lakh Crore Threshold Significance:</strong> Monthly GST collections crossed ₹1 lakh crore for the first time in April 2022 and have been consistently above that since. The ₹2 lakh crore threshold represents a further doubling of the tax base — reflecting formalisation of the economy, increased compliance, expanded taxpayer base, and economic growth. Sustained ₹2 lakh crore monthly collections would mean annual GST of ~₹24 lakh crore, significantly exceeding the revised estimate targets.</li>
<li><strong>GST Rate Rationalisation:</strong> The 13th Finance Commission and multiple expert committees have recommended rationalising GST from five slabs to three (5%, 15%, 28%), eliminating the inverted duty structure across sectors, and widening the tax base by reducing exemptions. The September 12 GST Council meeting was expected to discuss these structural reforms, which could increase net collections while reducing compliance burden.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Trade</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 12 · Business</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Balance of payments; current account deficit; trade balance; remittances; FPI flows; forex reserves

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India's current account deficit (CAD) widened to $4.2 billion (0.5% of GDP) in Q1 FY27 (April-June 2026), from 0.4% of GDP in the corresponding quarter of the previous year. The merchandise trade gap widened sharply to $86.1 billion from $68.9 billion — driven by higher gold and oil imports amid the West Asia conflict. Net services receipts stood at $51.6 billion (software exports remain India's largest services category). Remittances surged to $42.9 billion from $33.2 billion — a positive swing in the current account. FPI outflows of $9.6 billion pressured the capital account, and foreign exchange reserves declined by $8.1 billion. India's CAD at 0.5% of GDP remains manageable by historical standards (the 2013 "taper tantrum" crisis saw CAD at 4.8% of GDP).
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Current Account and Balance of Payments:</strong> The Balance of Payments (BoP) has two major components: Current Account (trade in goods and services, remittances, primary income) and Capital/Financial Account (FPI, FDI, external borrowings, ECBs, banking capital). A current account deficit (CAD) means India imports more than it exports on the current account. It is financed by surpluses in the capital account — FDI, FPI, NRI deposits, ECBs. Unsustainable CAD (above 2-3% of GDP) can cause currency depreciation and balance of payments crises.</li>
<li><strong>Remittances in India's BoP:</strong> India consistently receives the world's largest inward remittances — $120+ billion annually. Remittances (money sent by Indian workers abroad, primarily from the Gulf, US, UK, Canada, Australia) are classified as secondary income in the current account, partially offsetting the merchandise trade deficit. The surge from $33.2 bn to $42.9 bn in a single quarter is unusually high and may reflect calendar effects or base-year revisions.</li>
<li><strong>FPI Flows and CAD Financing:</strong> Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) hold equity, debt, and hybrid instruments in Indian markets. FPI outflows of $9.6 billion in Q1 FY27 would have pressured the INR and reduced the financial account surplus available to finance CAD. India's RBI intervenes in currency markets by selling forex reserves to stabilise the rupee — the $8.1 billion decline in forex reserves reflects such intervention. India's forex reserves were approximately $650+ billion before this decline.</li>
<li><strong>Merchandise Trade Deficit Drivers:</strong> India's structural merchandise trade deficit reflects its dependence on crude oil imports (India imports ~85% of its crude), electronic goods (smartphones, semiconductors), and gold (India is the world's second-largest gold consumer). The West Asia conflict-driven oil price spike in 2026 is a key factor behind the widening merchandise deficit from $68.9 bn to $86.1 bn in a single quarter.</li>
<li><strong>Services Export — India's Comparative Advantage:</strong> India's services exports — software and IT services (NASSCOM data: ~$245 bn in FY26), business process management, consulting, financial services — generate the services surplus that partially offsets the merchandise deficit. Net services receipts of $51.6 billion in a single quarter annualise to ~$200+ billion, making India a major surplus country in the invisible trade account. This is why India's CAD remains manageable despite a large merchandise trade deficit.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
LPG (liquefied petroleum gas) consumption declined 16% in August 2026 to 2.424 million metric tonnes (MMT), driven by a structural shift from LPG cylinders to piped natural gas (PNG) connections. PNG connections grew 36% in April-June 2026 to 4.61 lakh new connections, as city gas distribution (CGD) networks expand under the City Gas Distribution Policy. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY):</strong> PMUY (2016, extended as PMUY 2.0 in 2021) provided free LPG connections to BPL households — primarily women — to shift them from biomass cooking (firewood, dung cakes) to clean cooking fuel. Over 10 crore connections have been distributed. The scheme's success in LPG penetration now creates a paradox: as PNG expands in urban areas and LPG prices rise, some PMUY households (urban BPL) are switching back to biomass, while wealthier households switch to PNG.</li>
<li><strong>City Gas Distribution (CGD) and PNGRB:</strong> The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB) regulates city gas distribution (CNG for vehicles, PNG for households and industry) through geographical area licences awarded to CGD entities (like Indraprastha Gas, Mahanagar Gas, Adani Gas). The government has awarded CGD licences for 295+ Geographical Areas covering 90% of India's population. The 36% PNG connection growth reflects this accelerating rollout.</li>
<li><strong>LPG Subsidy Architecture:</strong> LPG subsidies are channelled through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) — the government transfers the subsidy amount directly to consumers' bank accounts rather than subsidising the cylinder price. The PAHAL scheme (world's largest DBT programme by beneficiary count) implemented this. As PNG displaces LPG for upper-income consumers, the LPG subsidy bill is increasingly targeted at actual BPL users, improving fiscal efficiency.</li>
<li><strong>Petrol and Diesel Demand as Economic Indicators:</strong> Petrol demand growth (7.9% YoY) reflects rising personal vehicle usage — consistent with PV wholesale growth. Diesel demand growth (6.5%) tracks freight and agricultural activity. India is the world's third-largest oil consumer; domestic petroleum product demand data is published monthly by the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas (MoPNG) and serves as a real-time economic activity indicator.</li>
<li><strong>Natural Gas Pipeline Network (GAIL):</strong> GAIL (Gas Authority of India Ltd.) operates India's approximately 16,000+ km natural gas pipeline network (Pradhan Mantri Urja Ganga, HVJ pipeline, GREP pipeline). The network connects gas sources (LNG terminals at Dahej, Hazira, Kochi, Dhamra) to CGD networks and industrial consumers. Expansion of the pipeline network is critical for the government's target of increasing natural gas's share in India's primary energy mix from 6% to 15% by 2030.</li>
</ul>

**UPSC Mains Classification**

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
UPI (Unified Payments Interface) transaction volumes set a new record of 24.51 billion in August 2026, surpassing the previous record of 23.66 billion. Transaction value reached ₹29.8 lakh crore. Year-on-year growth was 22% in volume and 20% in value. The Raksha Bandhan festival period boosted volumes through gifting payments. UPI is now accepted in 11 countries — with Uzbekistan as the latest addition — reflecting India's push for cross-border UPI interoperability as a fintech diplomacy tool. NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India) manages UPI. For UPSC, UPI's growth story embodies India's Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) model, which India has been actively promoting internationally through G20 forums and bilateral agreements as a template for other developing nations.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>UPI Architecture:</strong> UPI (launched 2016) is a real-time payment protocol built on IMPS (Immediate Payment Service) infrastructure. It enables inter-bank transactions via Virtual Payment Addresses (VPA/UPI ID), mobile numbers, or QR codes. UPI works on a four-party model: payer PSP (Payment Service Provider), payer bank, payee bank, payee PSP. NPCI is the operator; RBI is the regulator. UPI is interoperable across all bank accounts and wallets — a key design principle that democratised digital payments.</li>
<li><strong>NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India):</strong> NPCI is an umbrella organisation for retail payments infrastructure in India, set up in 2008 under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, as a not-for-profit entity. It owns and operates UPI, IMPS, RuPay cards, NACH, AePS (Aadhaar-Enabled Payment System), Bharat QR, and other payment systems. NPCI International Pvt. Ltd. (NIPL) handles cross-border UPI expansion.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-Border UPI — Diplomatic Tool:</strong> India has operationalised cross-border UPI acceptance in Singapore (PayNow linkage), UAE, France, UK, Mauritius, Nepal, Bhutan, Malaysia, Fiji, and Uzbekistan. These are implemented through bilateral central bank agreements and NIPL partnerships. India has been promoting UPI as part of its G20 presidency's Digital Public Infrastructure agenda and as an alternative to card network-dominated (Visa/Mastercard) cross-border payment systems.</li>
<li><strong>India Stack — DPI Model:</strong> India Stack refers to the layered Digital Public Infrastructure — Aadhaar (identity), UPI (payments), DigiLocker (documents), Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (health records). India has packaged this as the "DPI for the World" initiative through the G20, offering the architecture to developing nations. The World Bank, UNDP, and G20 countries have endorsed India's DPI model as a template for inclusive digital financial infrastructure in the Global South.</li>
<li><strong>Zero-MDR Policy and UPI Economics:</strong> Since January 2020, the government mandated zero Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) on UPI and RuPay transactions — meaning merchants pay nothing for UPI payments, unlike credit card transactions (MDR: 1.5-2%). While this drove massive adoption, it creates a revenue challenge for banks and PSPs. The government provides ₹1,500 crore annually in incentive payments to banks for UPI infrastructure maintenance. The long-term sustainability of the zero-MDR model as volumes scale is a policy debate.</li>
