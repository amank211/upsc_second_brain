  Delhi HC calls for hostel audit; building collapse toll reaches 7

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Urban local bodies; accountability of MCD; judicial oversight
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Student welfare; right to safe accommodation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Delhi High Court, taking suo motu cognisance of a building collapse in a PG (paying-guest) accommodation colony that killed seven people, directed the Municipal Corporation of Delhi (MCD) to conduct a comprehensive audit of all registered and unregistered hostels and PG facilities in the capital. The court expressed dismay at the continued proliferation of unauthorised structures and demanded an explanation from MCD on its inspection regime. The incident underscores a structural gap in urban governance: rapid growth in student and migrant populations has far outpaced the regulatory capacity of local bodies to enforce building bye-laws and fire-safety norms. For UPSC, this story links urban governance failures, the role of High Courts in enforcing fundamental rights (Article 21 — right to life), accountability of local self-government bodies under the 74th Constitutional Amendment, and the tension between informal housing supply and formal regulation in India's cities.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Municipal Corporation of Delhi (MCD):</strong> Reconstituted as a unified body in May 2022 after the trifurcation of 2012 was reversed by the DMC (Amendment) Act, 2022. MCD is responsible for building plan approvals, demolition of unauthorised structures, and enforcement of the Delhi Building Bye-Laws. Its failure to act on unsafe structures is periodically subjected to High Court oversight.</li>
<li><strong>74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992:</strong> Gave constitutional status to Urban Local Bodies (ULBs) and provided for a 12th Schedule listing 18 functions that may be devolved to them, including regulation of land use and construction. However, actual devolution varies by state; Delhi's ULB functions overlap with the Delhi Development Authority (DDA) and the state government, creating accountability gaps.</li>
<li><strong>Article 21 and Right to Safe Shelter:</strong> The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to live with dignity and access to basic amenities. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) the court recognised that the right to livelihood is part of Article 21, a precedent often cited in housing-related PILs.</li>
<li><strong>Paying Guest (PG) Regulation:</strong> Delhi does not have a dedicated PG regulation Act; PG accommodations fall loosely under the Delhi Rent Act or bye-laws but enforcement is weak. The National Building Code of India prescribes fire safety and structural norms but its adoption and enforcement by ULBs is inconsistent.</li>
<li><strong>Suo Motu Cognisance:</strong> Courts can take up cases on their own initiative (suo motu) without a formal petition being filed, typically in response to media reports or letters from affected citizens. The Delhi HC has used this power in matters relating to pollution, COVID management, and urban safety — making it a key tool for judicial oversight of executive inaction.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Arunachal and Aksai Chin between 'unspecified' claim lines on UN-endorsed map
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Diplomacy</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-China bilateral relations; boundary dispute; cartographic aggression
- GS Paper I → Geography → India's territorial boundaries; Line of Actual Control

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A map endorsed by the United Nations General Assembly as part of a "Correct the Map" resolution depicts both Arunachal Pradesh and Aksai Chin as lying between unspecified competing claim lines, effectively refusing to affirm Indian sovereignty over either territory. India's Ministry of External Affairs lodged a formal protest, reiterating that Arunachal Pradesh is an integral and inalienable part of India and that the depiction in the UN-sanctioned cartography amounts to institutional endorsement of China's maximalist territorial claims. The dispute is part of a broader pattern of what India terms "cartographic aggression" — a strategy of incrementally normalising contested boundaries through repeated publication of maps showing Indian territory as disputed or Chinese. For UPSC, the article is significant for its links to the India-China boundary dispute, the 1914 McMahon Line, the Shimla Convention, India's position at the UN, and the broader question of how international institutions handle contested sovereignty claims.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>McMahon Line (1914):</strong> The boundary between British India and Tibet agreed at the Shimla Convention between Britain, Tibet, and China (which ultimately did not ratify it). India regards this line — which places Arunachal Pradesh firmly within Indian territory — as the legal international boundary. China contests this, claiming the line was drawn without its consent and that Arunachal Pradesh (which it calls "South Tibet") belongs to China.</li>
<li><strong>Aksai Chin:</strong> A high-altitude plateau administered by China since the 1950s but claimed by India as part of Ladakh (and historically of Jammu & Kashmir). China built a strategic highway through it (National Highway 219) linking Xinjiang to Tibet without India's knowledge in the 1950s. India's discovery of this road was a major trigger for the 1962 war.</li>
<li><strong>Line of Actual Control (LAC):</strong> The de facto boundary between Indian and Chinese-controlled territories, established in the aftermath of the 1962 war. Unlike a formal international boundary, the LAC is not delineated in any mutually signed agreement and its precise alignment is contested in several sectors — Western (Ladakh), Middle (Himachal/Uttarakhand), and Eastern (Arunachal).</li>
<li><strong>Cartographic Aggression:</strong> A term used by Indian officials to describe China's practice of publishing official maps that show disputed or Indian territories as Chinese. China's 2023 "Standard Map" included Arunachal Pradesh and Aksai Chin as Chinese territory, prompting protests from India and several other countries. The strategy is seen as an attempt to normalise territorial claims through repeated visual assertion.</li>
<li><strong>India's UN Position:</strong> India is a founding member of the UN and a non-permanent member of the Security Council (served multiple terms). While it actively participates in UNGA resolutions, it reserves the right to reject the depiction of its territory on UN-endorsed maps and has consistently protested such representations through diplomatic channels, though the UN Secretariat's cartographic standards are a matter of internal UN procedure.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  LPG booking interval in rural areas cut to 25 days
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Energy policy; subsidies; welfare scheme delivery
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Welfare schemes for vulnerable sections; Ujjwala Yojana

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The government reduced the minimum interval for LPG cylinder booking in rural areas from 30 days to 25 days, citing supply-chain improvements and the need to prevent households from reverting to biomass fuels during the ongoing West Asian conflict, which has driven up crude oil prices and tightened global energy markets. The decision is particularly relevant for beneficiaries under the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY), where refill rates have historically lagged behind cylinder distribution due to affordability constraints. Cutting the booking interval effectively increases the maximum number of subsidised refills a household can claim in a year. For UPSC, the story connects energy security, welfare scheme implementation gaps, rural consumption patterns, under-recovery in oil marketing companies (OMCs), and India's dependence on West Asian crude amid geopolitical volatility.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY, 2016):</strong> Launched by the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas to provide free LPG connections to women from BPL (Below Poverty Line) households, particularly in rural areas. By 2024, over 10 crore connections had been distributed. The scheme aims to eliminate indoor air pollution from biomass cooking and improve women's health outcomes. Phase 2 (Ujjwala 2.0) expanded coverage to migrants and those without proof of address.</li>
<li><strong>LPG Subsidy Mechanism:</strong> LPG is sold at market-linked prices but the government provides a direct benefit transfer (DBT) subsidy into beneficiaries' bank accounts. The amount of subsidy has fluctuated — it was removed for most urban consumers in 2020 but retained for PMUY beneficiaries. Oil marketing companies (BPCL, HPCL, IOC) absorb under-recoveries when LPG is sold below cost, affecting their financials.</li>
<li><strong>Under-Recovery vs. Subsidy:</strong> Under-recovery refers to the gap between the cost of supplying LPG and the price charged to consumers. While the government may partially compensate OMCs, large under-recoveries affect public sector oil company profits and government fiscal arithmetic. The current West Asia crisis has widened this gap by raising crude input costs.</li>
<li><strong>West Asia Conflict and Energy Prices:</strong> India imports roughly 85% of its crude oil requirements. West Asian suppliers (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iraq) account for a significant share. Geopolitical tensions — including Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping and U.S.–Iran tensions — have kept crude prices elevated, affecting the cost of LPG (which is a byproduct of crude refining or natural gas processing).</li>
<li><strong>Biomass Reversal Risk:</strong> Studies by the Energy Policy journal and CEEW have documented that PMUY beneficiaries often use LPG as a supplementary fuel rather than a complete substitute for biomass, due to cost. When cylinder prices rise or booking intervals lengthen, households revert to wood, dung cakes, and crop residue — reversing the scheme's health and environmental objectives.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Maharashtra Chief Minister Eknath Shinde suspended a leader of his Shiv Sena faction after the individual was arrested for leading an assault on hospital staff — doctors and nurses — at a government medical facility. The attack occurred in the context of rising frustrations over a patient's death, an increasingly common trigger for mob violence against healthcare workers. The incident follows the national controversy over the rape and murder of a trainee doctor at R.G. Kar Medical College, Kolkata, which had sparked nationwide doctor strikes. The Supreme Court had warned that those who attack doctors "do not deserve to roam free" (covered separately in News, p.5). Shinde's swift action — suspending the party leader — reflects political damage-control but also the broader legislative conversation about enacting a Central law to protect healthcare workers, beyond state-level acts already in place in many states.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Violence Against Healthcare Workers:</strong> India does not yet have a Central law specifically criminalising attacks on healthcare workers, though the Healthcare Service Personnel and Clinical Establishments (Prohibition of Violence and Damage to Property) Bill was introduced in Parliament. Many states have their own acts (e.g., Maharashtra Medicare Service Persons and Hospitals Act, 2010) providing for imprisonment of up to 3 years and fines.</li>
<li><strong>R.G. Kar Medical College Case (2024):</strong> The rape and murder of a postgraduate medical trainee inside a Kolkata government hospital triggered nationwide protests by doctors demanding better safety infrastructure (secure rest rooms, CCTV, security personnel) and a Central law. The Supreme Court constituted a National Task Force to address doctor safety.</li>
<li><strong>Shiv Sena Split and Eknath Shinde Faction:</strong> Following the 2022 Maharashtra political crisis, the original Shiv Sena split into two factions — one led by Eknath Shinde (recognised by the Election Commission as the "real" Shiv Sena in February 2023) and the other by Uddhav Thackeray. The split followed Shinde's rebellion against the Maha Vikas Aghadi coalition government. The Shinde faction is part of the Mahayuti coalition with BJP and NCP (Ajit Pawar group).</li>
<li><strong>Mob Justice and Due Process:</strong> Attacks on hospitals by mobs after patient deaths are a recurrent phenomenon in India, partly reflecting weak trust in medical institutions and partly exploited by political actors. Such violence is criminalised under the IPC (now BNS) provisions on rioting, causing hurt, and criminal intimidation, but prosecutions are infrequent.</li>
<li><strong>Chief Minister's Disciplinary Power over Party:</strong> Suspending a party member is an internal party action, not a state government order; CMs acting as party presidents or through their party's disciplinary committee can impose suspension. It does not carry legal force but signals political distancing and can affect candidacy for party-distributed posts.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  'Conduct worthy of derision': HC pulls up District Magistrate over NSA detention
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Preventive detention; National Security Act; fundamental rights; habeas corpus
- GS Paper II → Governance → Accountability of district administration; judicial review

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Allahabad High Court used sharp language to censure a District Magistrate (DM) whose order detaining a student (Akriti Chaudhary) under the National Security Act (NSA), 1980 was found to be legally infirm and motivated by improper grounds. The court, hearing a habeas corpus petition, described the DM's conduct as "worthy of derision," quashed the detention order, and directed the student's release. NSA detentions are administrative — meaning no formal criminal charge is needed — but they are subject to judicial review to ensure the grounds of detention are reasonable, the detainee is informed of the grounds, and the Advisory Board's procedure is followed. Courts in India have periodically criticised executive overreach in NSA cases, particularly where the grounds appear vague or politically motivated. For UPSC, the case illustrates the tension between state security powers and individual liberty, the role of the judiciary as a check on executive action, and the procedural safeguards around preventive detention.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Security Act (NSA), 1980:</strong> Empowers Central and State governments to detain a person without trial for up to 12 months (extendable) if satisfied that detention is necessary to prevent acts prejudicial to national security, public order, or the maintenance of essential services. The detaining authority must be the District Magistrate, Commissioner of Police, or the Central/State government.</li>
<li><strong>Preventive Detention vs. Punitive Detention:</strong> Preventive detention does not require a criminal trial; it is a precautionary measure. Article 22 of the Constitution provides safeguards: the detainee must be informed of the grounds "as soon as may be" (but not if disclosure is against public interest), has the right to make a representation, and must be produced before an Advisory Board within 3 weeks. The Advisory Board — consisting of persons qualified to be High Court judges — must report whether detention is justified.</li>
<li><strong>Habeas Corpus:</strong> A writ (Article 32/226) literally meaning "produce the body," directing an authority to bring a detained person before the court and show legal justification for the detention. It is a fundamental safeguard of personal liberty. Courts can quash NSA detention orders if grounds are vague, irrelevant, stale, or if procedural requirements were not met.</li>
<li><strong>Judicial Review of Administrative Detention:</strong> Although courts cannot question the subjective satisfaction of the detaining authority (i.e., whether the DM genuinely believed detention was necessary), they can review: (a) whether the grounds are legally relevant, (b) whether the facts stated could reasonably lead to such satisfaction, and (c) whether procedure mandated by Article 22 and the NSA was followed. The Allahabad HC has been particularly active in scrutinising NSA orders in UP.</li>
<li><strong>District Magistrate's Powers:</strong> The DM is the apex civil authority at district level, combining executive, magisterial, and revenue functions. Their powers under NSA are administrative and must be exercised responsibly; courts expect DMs to apply independent judicial-minded scrutiny, not act as rubber stamps for police or political pressure. Censure by a High Court of a DM is a serious finding with implications for their service record.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  HC warns Bengal govt. of blanket order protecting Abhishek Banerjee
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Parliamentary privileges; separation of powers; judicial review of executive orders

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Calcutta High Court warned the West Bengal government against issuing a broad, blanket protective order in favour of Abhishek Banerjee — the Trinamool Congress (TMC) MP and nephew of Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee — in connection with ongoing ED and CBI investigations. The court indicated that such an order would be legally unsustainable, as courts cannot provide blanket anticipatory protection from the normal operation of the law unless specific legal grounds are established. Abhishek Banerjee has faced scrutiny in multiple cases including alleged irregularities in the Bengal coal scam and cattle smuggling cases. The case raises fundamental questions about the limits of state government power to shield politically connected individuals from central investigative agencies, the doctrine of separation of powers, and the appropriate scope of judicial interim orders.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Enforcement Directorate (ED) and CBI Jurisdiction:</strong> Both are central agencies. The ED operates under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002, and the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), while the CBI operates under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. State governments cannot direct these agencies to drop investigations; they can, however, refuse consent for CBI investigation of state-list offences (though this restriction was diluted by SC rulings).</li>
<li><strong>Blanket Anticipatory Protection:</strong> Indian law provides for anticipatory bail under Section 438 CrPC (now Section 482 BNSS), which protects a person from arrest in a specific case for a defined period. Courts have held that blanket or open-ended orders protecting a person from arrest in any and all cases are contrary to law — such an order would effectively nullify the investigative mandate of law-enforcement agencies.</li>
<li><strong>Abhishek Banerjee and Bengal Scams:</strong> Abhishek Banerjee is a two-time TMC MP from Diamond Harbour and has been named in investigations related to the Bengal coal scam (illegal coal mining from ECL mines), cattle smuggling (across the India-Bangladesh border), and teacher recruitment irregularities. He has challenged several ED summons in court.</li>
<li><strong>Centre-State Relations and Investigative Agencies:</strong> The use of central agencies (ED, CBI, IT) against Opposition-ruled state leaders has been a contentious federal issue. Some states have filed cases in the Supreme Court challenging CBI's right to operate without state consent. The SC has upheld the constitutional validity of PMLA's stringent bail conditions in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India (2022).</li>
<li><strong>Parliamentary Privilege vs. Criminal Investigation:</strong> MPs enjoy certain privileges under Articles 105 and 194, but these do not extend to criminal cases unrelated to their parliamentary duties. The Supreme Court in the JMM bribery case (1998) and subsequent rulings has held that parliamentary privilege does not shield legislators from criminal prosecution for acts committed outside the House.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  77 days and counting: Haryana teachers seek job regularisation
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Labour</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Education</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Contractual employment in public services; education sector governance
- GS Paper III → Economy → Labour market; regularisation of contract workers

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Guest and contractual teachers in Haryana who have been on strike for 77 days demanding regularisation of their services renewed their protests outside government offices in Chandigarh. The teachers, appointed on a contractual basis, argue they have rendered continuous service for years without the job security, pay scale, or benefits of regular government employees. The Haryana government has not accepted their demands, citing budgetary constraints and pending court cases. The issue reflects a nationwide trend of governments expanding the contractual and temporary workforce in public services — particularly education — as a cost-saving measure, with significant implications for service delivery, teaching quality, and the rights of workers under labour law.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Contractualisation in Public Services:</strong> The 5th and 6th Pay Commission recommendations and fiscal pressure led states to increasingly appoint para-teachers, shiksha mitras, and contractual staff on lower fixed pay rather than regular scales. In education, this reduces cost but may compromise quality and motivation. The SC in Daya Shankar Yadav v. State of UP (2013) held that shiksha mitras have legitimate expectations of regularisation after prolonged service.</li>
<li><strong>Uma Devi Judgment (2006):</strong> The Supreme Court's five-judge bench ruling in Secretary, State of Karnataka v. Umadevi held that irregular appointments (not made through proper process) cannot be regularised as a matter of right. However, those in continuous service for 10+ years should be regularised as a one-time measure. This landmark ruling has been the basis for both government refusals and employee claims across India.</li>
<li><strong>Right to Education (RTE) Act, 2009:</strong> Requires that teachers in government schools must be professionally qualified (B.Ed or equivalent). This has put contractual teachers without requisite qualifications at risk, while also creating pressure to ensure every school has adequate teaching staff — a contradiction that drives the contractual appointment cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Haryana Labour Context:</strong> Haryana has a significant public sector workforce and has periodically faced agitations from contractual workers across departments. The current BJP state government has maintained that regularisation must follow due process of recruitment, while contractual teachers argue that continuous service itself constitutes a legitimate basis for absorption.</li>

<li><strong>India's AML/CFT Framework:</strong> PMLA 2002 (primary AML statute) + FEMA + UAPA (terror financing) + FCRA (foreign funding). FIU-IND (Financial Intelligence Unit, under Finance Ministry) receives Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs) from financial institutions.</li>

Fourteen people were arrested as the death toll from a spurious liquor tragedy in Madhya Pradesh reached 13. Spurious liquor typically contains methanol (industrial alcohol) instead of ethanol; even small quantities cause blindness, organ failure, and death. Such tragedies recur in India — especially where illicit liquor flourishes due to prohibition or high legal prices — revealing systemic failures in excise regulation and police vigilance. Victims are overwhelmingly from marginalised communities: daily wage workers, Dalits, and Adivasis who cannot afford legal liquor. The story illustrates the intersection of poverty, regulatory failure, and public health governance.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Methanol Poisoning:</strong> Methanol (wood alcohol) metabolises to formaldehyde and formic acid — as little as 10ml causes permanent blindness; 30ml can be fatal. Treatment with ethanol or fomepizole works if administered early. Spurious liquor tragedies in India involve deliberate or accidental substitution of ethanol with methanol.</li>
<li><strong>Excise Administration:</strong> Liquor regulation is a state subject (State List, Seventh Schedule). States earn major revenue from excise duty. Illicit brewing flourishes where legal liquor is expensive or prohibition creates supply gaps — Bihar, Gujarat, Manipur, Nagaland, Mizoram maintain complete prohibition.</li>
<li><strong>Recurrence of Hooch Tragedies:</strong> Notable cases include Aligarh (1991, 76 deaths), Andhra Pradesh (2005), and multiple Gujarat tragedies despite total prohibition. The pattern reveals corruption in excise-police chains, weak monitoring of industrial alcohol supply, and demand for affordable intoxicants among the poor.</li>
<li><strong>Legal Framework:</strong> Producing and selling spurious liquor is prosecuted under BNS (culpable homicide by negligence, or murder if deliberate), state excise acts, and the Food Safety and Standards Act (FSSA), 2006. Some states invoke NSA against habitual bootleggers.</li>
<li><strong>Social Dimension:</strong> Public health advocates argue for affordable ethanol access combined with strong enforcement preventing methanol diversion from industrial to beverage channels — rather than prohibition that drives consumption underground and into fatal territory.</li>
</ul>

<li><strong>DGFT Import Restrictions:</strong> The Directorate General of Foreign Trade, under the Ministry of Commerce, controls import licencing. <p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Energy pricing; subsidies; oil marketing companies; under-recoveries
- GS Paper II → Governance → Welfare scheme delivery; Ujjwala; DBT

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The explainer analyses why commercial LPG prices have been hiked (non-domestic cylinders used in hotels, restaurants, and small businesses), tracing the cause to rising crude oil import costs driven by the ongoing West Asian conflict and tightening global LPG supply. The piece explains the distinction between domestic (subsidised/DBT) and commercial LPG pricing, how oil marketing companies (BPCL, HPCL, IOC) calculate under-recoveries, and why the government periodically passes through price increases on commercial cylinders while protecting domestic consumers. It also links back to the Front Page story on rural LPG booking interval reduction — part of the government's simultaneous effort to ensure domestic supply access even as prices rise commercially.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>LPG Price Determination:</strong> Domestic LPG (14.2 kg cylinder) prices are revised periodically by oil marketing companies based on import parity price (IPP) — benchmark price at which India could import LPG internationally. The government either provides direct subsidy or allows pass-through depending on policy stance. Commercial LPG (19 kg cylinder) prices are market-linked and revised monthly.</li>
<li><strong>Under-Recovery vs. Subsidy:</strong> When LPG is sold below its production/import cost, the gap is the "under-recovery" borne by OMCs. The government compensates OMCs through budgetary subsidies or allows them to absorb losses (impacting their profits and credit ratings). Large sustained under-recoveries affect OMC balance sheets and their ability to invest in refinery expansion.</li>
<li><strong>Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) in LPG:</strong> PAHAL scheme (launched 2014) transfers LPG subsidy directly to beneficiaries' bank accounts — they first pay market price, then receive the subsidy as cash. <li><strong>West Asia Conflict and Crude Prices:</strong> LPG (liquefied petroleum gas) is a byproduct of crude oil refining and natural gas processing. <div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The explainer examines the thermal management challenge posed by the new generation of hyperscale data centres — including Google's planned 1-GW facility near Visakhapatnam and TCS's HyperVault initiative — where AI workloads generate vastly more heat per rack than traditional computing. Conventional air-cooling (CRAC/CRAH units) is reaching its physical limits for modern AI-accelerator-dense racks; liquid cooling (direct-to-chip, immersion cooling) is becoming necessary. The piece discusses the water consumption implications of liquid cooling in a water-stressed country like India, the energy efficiency metric (PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness), and the regulatory and infrastructure challenges for building hyperscale AI data centres in India.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Data Centre Growth in India:</strong> India's data centre capacity has been growing rapidly, driven by cloud adoption, digital public infrastructure (UPI, Aadhaar), OTT streaming, and now AI workloads. India had ~950 MW of installed data centre capacity in 2024, expected to exceed 2,000 MW by 2027. The government's Digital India push and PLI-adjacent incentives for data centres are key drivers.</li>
<li><strong>Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE):</strong> The industry metric for data centre energy efficiency — total facility power divided by IT equipment power. A PUE of 1.0 is perfect (all power goes to computing); global average is ~1.5. Modern hyperscale data centres achieve PUE of 1.1–1.2. Higher PUE means more power wasted on cooling, lighting, and distribution — a key sustainability metric.</li>
<li><strong>Liquid Cooling Technologies:</strong> As AI GPU clusters (e.g., Nvidia H100, B200 GPUs) dissipate 600–1,000+ watts per chip, conventional air-cooling cannot maintain safe temperatures. Alternatives include: (a) Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) — pipes bring coolant to chip-level heat sinks; (b) Rear-door heat exchangers; (c) Immersion cooling — servers submerged in dielectric fluid (mineral oil or engineered fluids like 3M Novec). Immersion cooling is most efficient but capital-intensive.</li>
<li><strong>Water Consumption and India's Climate:</strong> Liquid-cooled data centres that use evaporative cooling (cooling towers) consume large quantities of fresh water — a hyperscale facility can use millions of litres per day. In a water-stressed region like Andhra Pradesh (Visakhapatnam), this raises environmental and community concerns about industrial water allocation competing with agriculture and domestic use.</li>
<li><strong>India's Data Centre Policy:</strong> India does not yet have a comprehensive data centre policy at the Central level, though the DPDP Act, 2023 establishes data localisation requirements for certain sensitive data. States like Maharashtra (Navi Mumbai hub), Telangana, and Tamil Nadu have data centre policies offering land, power, and connectivity incentives. A National Data Centre Policy is under development by MeitY.</li>
</ul>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Monetary policy; RBI liquidity management; VRRR; interest rate corridor

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The RBI conducted two Variable Rate Reverse Repo (VRRR) auctions absorbing ₹6 lakh crore of surplus liquidity from the banking system. VRRR is used to drain excess liquidity — when banks have more money than they can profitably deploy, they park it with the RBI. The RBI uses VRRR (variable rate, auction-based) rather than fixed-rate reverse repo to better price liquidity absorption based on market demand. High VRRR volumes indicate a banking system in surplus liquidity mode — which supports lower lending rates. For UPSC, VRRR is part of the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) framework and connects to monetary policy transmission, inflation management, and the interest rate corridor.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF):</strong> RBI's primary daily liquidity management tool (introduced 2000). Banks borrow from RBI at the repo rate (injection) or park surplus funds at the reverse repo / SDF rate (absorption). The interest rate corridor is: Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) rate (floor) → policy repo rate (benchmark) → Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) rate (ceiling).</li>
<li><strong>VRRR (Variable Rate Reverse Repo):</strong> An auction where RBI absorbs liquidity from banks at a rate determined by competitive bidding — not a fixed rate. Gives RBI flexibility to drain more or less liquidity depending on market conditions; allows market to price the absorption rate. Has replaced fixed-rate reverse repo as the primary large-scale absorption tool.</li>
<li><strong>Surplus Liquidity Implications:</strong> Surplus banking system liquidity pushes short-term money market rates (MIBOR, T-bill yields) toward the SDF floor. This can be accommodative (supporting credit growth) or inflationary (if excess liquidity feeds asset price inflation). RBI uses VRRR to moderate surplus without formally changing the policy stance.</li>
<li><strong>Monetary Policy Transmission:</strong> The effectiveness of repo rate changes in influencing bank lending rates depends on liquidity conditions. In surplus conditions, banks are less incentivised to pass on rate changes to borrowers, weakening transmission. VRRR operations help maintain the policy rate's signalling effectiveness.</li>
<li><strong>RBI's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC):</strong> Six-member body (3 RBI + 3 external government-appointed) that sets the policy repo rate targeting CPI inflation at 4% (±2% band). Rate decisions interact with the RBI's daily liquidity operations through the LAF framework to influence credit and money supply conditions.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<li><strong>Strait of Hormuz:</strong> A 39-km-wide waterway between Iran and Oman connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman. Closure would cause an immediate global oil price spike, affecting all oil-importing nations including India.</li>

<li><strong>U.S. Shale Revolution:</strong> The U.S. shale oil breakthrough (2008–2015, sustained since) made the U.S. the world's largest crude producer (~13 million barrels/day). <li><strong>OPEC+ and Global Oil Architecture:</strong> Iran's ability to threaten Hormuz is also constrained by the fact that alternative suppliers (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iraq) — all transiting Hormuz — could theoretically route oil overland or via different pipelines if Hormuz were genuinely closed. Global marine fuel (bunker fuel) supply is under strain as refineries processing redirected Russian crude face capacity constraints, while Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping have forced vessels to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope — increasing voyage distances, fuel consumption, and demand for bunkering services at alternative ports. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>IMO 2020 (VLSFO Mandate):</strong> The International Maritime Organization's regulation effective January 1, 2020 requires ships to use fuel with sulphur content below 0.5% (Very Low Sulphur Fuel Oil) or fit scrubbers to clean exhaust gases. This cut sulphur emissions from shipping dramatically but required refineries to produce more desulphurised fuel — adding refinery complexity and cost.</li>
<li><strong>Red Sea Crisis and Rerouting:</strong> Houthi attacks on commercial ships in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden since late 2023 have caused major shipping companies (Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM) to reroute around Africa's Cape of Good Hope. This adds 7–14 days to voyages from Asia to Europe, increases fuel consumption per voyage by 20–30%, and has spiked freight rates on key trade lanes.</li>
<li><strong>Bunkering Hubs:</strong> Marine fuel is supplied at major bunkering ports — Singapore (world's largest bunkering port), Fujairah (UAE, critical for Gulf-bound traffic), Rotterdam, and others. Supply constraints at these hubs directly limit shipping operations globally. India's Mundra and JNPT are minor bunkering locations.</li>
<li><strong>Russia-Ukraine War and Refinery Redirection:</strong> Post-2022 Western sanctions caused Russian crude to be redirected to India, China, and Turkey. Indian refineries processing Russian Urals crude (now trading at a significant discount) have expanded capacity, but global refinery configurations optimised for Russian crude grades face strain when production or logistics shift.</li>
<li><strong>India's Shipping Industry:</strong> India handles ~95% of its trade by volume and ~70% by value through sea routes. India's own merchant shipping fleet is small relative to trade volumes — it relies heavily on foreign-flagged vessels. Rising freight rates directly increase India's import costs and reduce price competitiveness of Indian exports. India has a Sagarmala programme and a Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Policy aimed at developing domestic shipping capacity.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Jamaica submitted a formal petition to King Charles III through the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council — the UK's final court of appeal for several Commonwealth nations — seeking recognition and reparations for Britain's role in the transatlantic slave trade and the economic harms of plantation slavery in Jamaica. The petition is a landmark in the global reparations movement and challenges the UK government's position that reparations are not appropriate or legally required. The case is directly relevant to India's own colonial experience — debates about reparations for British colonial extraction from India (estimated by economist Utsa Patnaik at ~$45 trillion) are periodically raised in Indian academic and political discourse.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Transatlantic Slave Trade:</strong> Britain transported approximately 3.1 million enslaved Africans to the Americas between the 17th and early 19th centuries — one of the largest forced migrations in human history. The British Caribbean colonies (Jamaica, Barbados, Trinidad) were primary destinations. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in 1833, but compensated the slaveholders (not the enslaved) £20 million (~£300 billion in today's value).</li>
<li><strong>CARICOM Reparations Commission:</strong> The Caribbean Community (CARICOM) established a Reparations Commission in 2013, demanding that Britain, France, the Netherlands, and other former colonial powers engage in reparations discussions. CARICOM has outlined a 10-point plan including a formal apology, repatriation, indigenous people's development programme, and debt cancellation. Jamaica and Barbados have been most active in pursuing this.</li>
<li><strong>Judicial Committee of the Privy Council:</strong> The JCPC is the final court of appeal for many Commonwealth countries that have retained the link — including Jamaica, Barbados (now a republic but retained JCPC until 2023), and several Eastern Caribbean states. It sits in London and its judgments are binding in those countries. Using it as a vehicle for a reparations claim against the British Crown is legally novel and faces jurisdictional challenges.</li>
<li><strong>India's Colonial Reparations Debate:</strong> Economist Utsa Patnaik (JNU) estimated that Britain extracted $45 trillion from India between 1765–1938. The late Shashi Tharoor's Oxford Union speech (2015) popularised the reparations argument in India. The Indian government has not formally pursued a reparations claim, balancing historical grievance with contemporary diplomatic and economic relations with the UK (FTA negotiations).</li>
<li><strong>Global Reparations Movement:</strong> Following George Floyd's death (2020) and Black Lives Matter protests, reparations discussions gained momentum globally — from U.S. state-level reparations commissions (California) to Caribbean-UK negotiations and African Union demands. The movement raises legal questions about intergenerational liability, the measure of harm, and the form of redress (monetary, developmental, symbolic).</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Bayeux Tapestry — a 70-metre embroidered cloth depicting the Norman Conquest of England (1066 CE), housed in Bayeux, France — is on display at the British Museum for the first time in its approximately 950-year history, as part of a UK-France cultural exchange deal. The loan is a landmark event in cultural diplomacy, requiring special conservation measures and negotiated for years. For UPSC, the story is relevant in the context of cultural artefact repatriation debates — a globally contested issue where India has strongly advocated for the return of artefacts removed during colonial rule (Koh-i-Noor diamond, sculptures from Nalanda, etc.) from Britain and other Western countries.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Bayeux Tapestry:</strong> An 11th-century embroidered cloth (~70 metres long) depicting events leading to the Norman Conquest of England, culminating in the Battle of Hastings (1066 CE) where William the Conqueror defeated King Harold. Produced in England (likely Canterbury) but commissioned by Norman patrons; held in Bayeux, Normandy, France since the 14th century. It is a UNESCO World Documentary Heritage item.</li>
<li><strong>Cultural Property and Repatriation:</strong> The debate over ownership and repatriation of cultural artefacts in Western museums is globally live. Several European countries have returned artefacts; UK has largely resisted under the British Museum Act, which prohibits permanent transfer of objects.</li>
<li><strong>India's Repatriation Efforts:</strong> India has repatriated (or received repatriations of) hundreds of antiquities from the US, UK, Australia, and other countries. PM Modi has personally received returned artefacts as part of bilateral summit symbolism — including 157 artefacts from the US (2023), 29 from Australia (2023), and earlier returns from the UK. India's Antiquities and Art Treasures Act, 1972 governs domestic regulation of cultural property.</li>
<li><strong>British Museum and the 1963 Act:</strong> The British Museum Act, 1963 prohibits the trustees from permanently disposing of collection objects (except in limited circumstances). This has been the legal basis for refusing repatriation demands. Critics argue the Act needs reform to allow voluntary repatriations; supporters argue the universal museum model benefits all humanity by concentrating artefacts for broad public access.</li>
<li><strong>Cultural Diplomacy:</strong> The lending of the Bayeux Tapestry by France to the UK demonstrates how cultural property can be a tool of diplomatic goodwill — improving bilateral relations (post-Brexit friction between UK and France) through a shared historical narrative. India similarly uses cultural diplomacy — lending artefacts, hosting cultural festivals, promoting yoga globally — as a soft power instrument.</li>
</ul>
