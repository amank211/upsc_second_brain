<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  India, China push for border delimitation at 25th SR talks in Beijing
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Diplomacy</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Security</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-China bilateral relations; Line of Actual Control management; border delimitation and demarcation
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Border area management; role of external state actors

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
National Security Adviser Ajit Doval and Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi held the 25th round of Special Representatives (SR) talks in Beijing on 25 August 2026 — the first such meeting in five years and only the second since the Galwan clash of June 2020. The talks centred on translating the military-level disengagement, completed at all friction points by April 2025, into a durable diplomatic framework. Both sides discussed moving toward formal delimitation and eventual demarcation of the Line of Actual Control, a step that would require agreeing on precise geographical coordinates for the first time. The meeting builds on the "Kazan consensus" of October 2024, where Prime Ministers Modi and Xi agreed to restore normalcy in border areas as a precondition for broader normalisation. The SR mechanism, created in 2003, is the highest political-level channel for boundary talks, operating above the military Corps Commander meetings. India is said to have insisted on a time-bound roadmap for buffer-zone removal and restoration of pre-April 2020 patrolling rights at Depsang and Demchok as preconditions for full normalisation of diplomatic and trade ties.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Representatives (SR) Mechanism (2003):</strong> Established by the India-China Joint Declaration of 2003 to resolve the boundary question at a political level. The SR mechanism operates at the NSA/senior diplomat level and is guided by the 2005 Agreement on Political Parameters and Guiding Principles for the Settlement of the India-China Boundary Question and the 2012 Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination. India's SR is currently NSA Ajit Doval; China's is Foreign Minister Wang Yi.</li>
<li><strong>Line of Actual Control (LAC):</strong> The de facto border between India and China, approximately 3,488 km long, dividing three sectors — Western (Ladakh), Middle (Himachal Pradesh/Uttarakhand), and Eastern (Arunachal Pradesh/Sikkim). Unlike the Line of Control with Pakistan, the LAC has never been formally demarcated; both sides hold differing perceptions of its alignment in several areas, making delimitation legally and politically complex.</li>
<li><strong>Kazan Consensus (October 2024):</strong> On the sidelines of the BRICS summit in Kazan, Russia, PM Modi and President Xi agreed to restore "peace and tranquility" at the LAC as the necessary precondition for normalising bilateral relations. The Kazan meeting marked the first formal bilateral summit since 2019 and resulted in India and China concluding the disengagement deal at Depsang Plains and Demchok in Ladakh.</li>
<li><strong>Delimitation vs Demarcation:</strong> Delimitation is the process of defining a boundary line on a map using agreed coordinates and descriptions; demarcation is the physical marking of that line on the ground with pillars, markers, or fencing. India and China have neither delimitated nor demarcated the LAC, which is a primary source of friction. The 1993 Agreement on Maintenance of Peace and Tranquility called for an eventual exchange of maps to clarify the LAC, but this has only partially occurred in the middle sector.</li>
<li><strong>Galwan Valley Clash (June 2020):</strong> A violent hand-to-hand confrontation in the Galwan Valley, Ladakh, on 15–16 June 2020 resulted in 20 Indian soldiers and an undisclosed number of Chinese PLA personnel killed — the first fatalities on the LAC since 1975. The clash triggered a sharp diplomatic and military standoff, suspension of most bilateral exchanges, and a prolonged disengagement process at multiple friction points including Gogra-Hot Springs, Pangong Tso, PP-15, Depsang Plains, and Demchok.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Bihar student protests over BPSC irregularities turn violent; police use force
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Public service commissions; transparency and accountability in recruitment; student grievance redressal
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Youth unemployment; access to public employment for marginalised groups

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Student agitation in Patna escalated into violence on 25 August 2026 as protesters demanding a re-examination of the Bihar Public Service Commission (BPSC) integrated examination alleged paper leaks and irregularities in the conduct of the test held earlier this year. Police used water cannons and lathi charges to disperse crowds near the BPSC headquarters; several students and police personnel were injured. Protesters alleged that question papers circulated on social media hours before the exam began and demanded immediate cancellation of results, a fresh examination under CCTV surveillance, and an independent inquiry by a sitting High Court judge. The BPSC and state government denied systemic irregularities but acknowledged isolated incidents at individual centres. Bihar has seen repeated examination controversy over the past decade — the BPSC 67th Combined Competitive Examination in 2023 was similarly mired in paper-leak allegations. The protests drew opposition demands for the dismissal of the BPSC chairman and highlight the persistent governance deficit in state public service recruitment, a recurring theme in UPSC case studies on transparency in administration.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Bihar Public Service Commission (BPSC):</strong> A constitutional body under Article 315 of the Constitution established to recruit officers to state civil services. The BPSC conducts the Combined Competitive Examination (CCE) for entry to the Bihar Administrative Service and allied posts. It is accountable to the Governor and submits an annual report to the state legislature, but oversight mechanisms have been criticized as inadequate.</li>
<li><strong>Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024:</strong> A central law enacted to address the epidemic of examination fraud in competitive public recruitment. It provides for up to 10 years' imprisonment and fines up to ₹1 crore for paper leaks, impersonation, and organized cheating. The law applies to exams conducted by UPSC, SSC, NTA, and other central bodies but does not automatically cover state PSC examinations — a key gap.</li>
<li><strong>National Recruitment Agency (NRA):</strong> Proposed in the Union Budget 2020–21 to conduct a Common Eligibility Test (CET) for non-gazetted posts under central government, replacing multiple separate exams by SSC, RRBs, and IBPS. The intent was to reduce duplication and examination stress on aspirants. The NRA has faced delays in implementation; a similar model for state recruitment remains absent in most states including Bihar.</li>
<li><strong>Article 315–323 (Part XIV of the Constitution):</strong> These articles establish the Union Public Service Commission and State Public Service Commissions as constitutional bodies. They guarantee independence through security of tenure — a UPSC/SPSC chairman can only be removed by the President/Governor on grounds specified in Article 317, and removal requires a Supreme Court inquiry.</li>
<li><strong>Youth Unemployment and Examination Economy:</strong> India's state-level competitive examinations attract millions of aspirants for a limited number of posts — Bihar alone sees 5–10 lakh applications for a few thousand vacancies in each BPSC cycle. This creates intense social pressure, making irregularities or perceived unfairness a flash point for protests. The UPSC has noted the link between examination fraud, youth frustration, and social unrest as a governance concern in its annual reports.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  SC mulls special Bench on OBC 'creamy layer' — may revisit Indra Sawhney criteria
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Reservation policy; OBC classification; Supreme Court jurisprudence on Articles 15 and 16
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Creamy layer exclusion; backward class welfare; affirmative action reform

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Supreme Court is considering constituting a larger special Bench to revisit the "creamy layer" income threshold for OBC (Other Backward Classes) reservation, currently fixed at ₹8 lakh per annum — a limit that has not been revised since 2017 despite inflation. A bench of Chief Justice and two judges noted that the criteria set by the nine-judge bench in Indra Sawhney v Union of India (1992) may need reinterpretation in light of subsequent constitutional amendments, changing socio-economic conditions, and the Supreme Court's 2024 ruling in Pankaj Kumar Yadav v State of Bihar allowing sub-classification within OBCs. The creamy layer doctrine, designed to exclude the more affluent among OBCs from reservation benefits so as to direct them toward the truly backward, has been criticized as inadequate in capturing relative deprivation. The bench also noted the inconsistency between states, some of which apply no creamy layer at all to OBC quotas in state jobs, and the central government's ₹8 lakh ceiling. A reference to a seven-judge or nine-judge constitution bench could significantly reshape the architecture of OBC reservations across India.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Indra Sawhney v Union of India (1992):</strong> The landmark nine-judge Supreme Court verdict that upheld 27% OBC reservation in central government jobs under Article 16(4) but imposed two critical limits: (a) total reservation cannot exceed 50% (the "50% ceiling rule"), and (b) the "creamy layer" — the more socially and economically advanced among OBCs — must be excluded from reservation benefits. The judgment also struck down the Narasimha Rao government's attempt to give 10% reservation to the economically backward among upper castes.</li>
<li><strong>Creamy Layer Threshold:</strong> The National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) and the government periodically revise the income ceiling below which an OBC family is considered eligible for reservation. It currently stands at ₹8 lakh per annum (revised in 2017); the NCBC has repeatedly recommended raising it to ₹15 lakh to account for inflation, but the central government has not acted. States have wide discretion to set their own thresholds for state-level reservations.</li>
<li><strong>Sub-classification within OBCs — Pankaj Kumar Yadav (2024):</strong> A seven-judge SC bench ruled in 2024 that states may sub-classify OBCs into different tiers (e.g., "Most Backward" vs "Backward") and allocate separate quotas within the 27% OBC ceiling, overruling E.V. Chinnaiah v State of AP (2004). This opened the door to targeted reservation for the most deprived OBC communities and intensified debate on whether the creamy layer criteria also needs re-stratification.</li>
<li><strong>Articles 15(4) and 16(4):</strong> Article 15(4) enables the state to make special provisions for "socially and educationally backward classes" in education; Article 16(4) enables reservation in public employment for "backward classes of citizens." Both were inserted by the First Constitutional Amendment (1951). Article 16(4A) and 16(4B) were added by the 77th and 81st Amendments to enable carry-forward and backlog reservation for SC/STs.</li>
<li><strong>National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC):</strong> A constitutional body under Article 338B (inserted by the 102nd Amendment, 2018) with powers to examine complaints, advise the government on OBC-related matters, and recommend inclusions/exclusions from the OBC list. Before the 102nd Amendment, the NCBC was a statutory body under the NCBC Act, 1993; its constitutionalization strengthened its independence. The NCBC's creamy-layer revision recommendations, however, are advisory and not binding on the government.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Civil society opposes Maharashtra Freedom of Religion Act; fears targeting of minorities
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Fundamental Rights; Article 25 (freedom of religion); anti-conversion legislation; federalism
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Minority rights; communalism; secularism

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Maharashtra's newly enacted Freedom of Religion Act, which mandates prior government permission before religious conversions and imposes up to three years' imprisonment for "fraudulent" or "allurement-based" conversions, drew sharp opposition from civil society groups, Christian organisations, and legal scholars at a public hearing in Mumbai on 25 August 2026. Critics argued the law violates Articles 25 and 26 of the Constitution by requiring state permission for a constitutionally guaranteed right, and that the vague definition of "allurement" (which the law extends to include acts of charity, scholarship, or medical assistance) gives police virtually unlimited discretion to harass missionaries and social workers. The law mirrors similar legislation passed in Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, and Himachal Pradesh over the past two decades. Proponents argue it prevents coercive conversions of tribals and the economically vulnerable. However, opponents noted that conviction rates under similar state laws have been negligible, while instances of harassment of minority communities by police have multiplied — a pattern documented by the National Human Rights Commission. The Maharashtra law is expected to face a constitutional challenge before the Bombay High Court.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 25 and 26 of the Constitution:</strong> Article 25 guarantees to every person the freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, and health and other Fundamental Rights. Article 26 guarantees religious denominations the right to manage their own affairs in matters of religion. The Supreme Court in Rev. Stainislaus v State of MP (1977) held that the right to "propagate" does not include the right to convert another person.</li>
<li><strong>State Anti-Conversion Laws:</strong> At least 10 states have enacted "Freedom of Religion" acts, starting with Orissa (1967) and Madhya Pradesh (1968). These laws typically require converts to notify the District Magistrate or collector and place the burden of proving a voluntary conversion on the convert. States like UP (Uttar Pradesh Prohibition of Unlawful Religious Conversion Act, 2021) have expanded the scope to include marriage-related conversions ("love jihad") — provisions that remain under challenge before respective High Courts.</li>
<li><strong>Rev. Stainislaus v State of MP (1977):</strong> The Supreme Court upheld Orissa and MP anti-conversion laws, ruling that the right to "propagate" religion in Article 25 does not imply a right to convert another person. This precedent is routinely cited by states defending anti-conversion legislation. However, legal scholars argue Stainislaus did not address the current generation of laws that require prior state permission (as opposed to mere post-facto notification).</li>
<li><strong>Scheduled Tribes and Conversion Concerns:</strong> A significant proportion of anti-conversion law enforcement targets Scheduled Tribe (ST) regions, ostensibly to protect tribal customs and identity. Critics counter that many tribal communities have adopted Christianity voluntarily and that forced reconversion (ghar wapsi) by Hindu nationalist groups is a parallel phenomenon not addressed by these laws. The UN Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Religion has repeatedly flagged India's anti-conversion laws as disproportionate.</li>
<li><strong>National Human Rights Commission (NHRC):</strong> A statutory body under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, the NHRC has documented cases where anti-conversion laws in states like UP and Gujarat have been misused to file false cases against religious minorities. Its recommendations are non-binding but carry moral authority; the NHRC can direct state governments to pay compensation to victims of human rights violations.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  CWMA orders Karnataka to release 9,000 cusecs to Tamil Nadu for 15 days amid Cauvery row
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Federalism</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Agriculture</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Federalism → Inter-state water disputes; role of quasi-judicial bodies; Article 262
- GS Paper III → Agriculture → Irrigation; water resource management; food security

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA) on 25 August 2026 directed Karnataka to release 9,000 cusecs of Cauvery water per day to Tamil Nadu for the next 15 days, citing the critical sowing season for the kuruvai (short-term paddy) crop in the delta districts of Thanjavur, Tiruvarur, and Nagapattinam. Karnataka protested the order, citing below-normal monsoon inflows into the Krishnaraja Sagar (KRS) and Kabini reservoirs and the water needs of the Bengaluru metropolitan region and Cauvery basin farmers in Mandya district. The CWMA was constituted in 2018 by the Supreme Court following the final award of the Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT), which allocated 177.25 TMC of water to Tamil Nadu, 284.75 TMC to Karnataka, and shares to Kerala and Puducherry. The dispute recurs almost every year during the southwest monsoon season when reservoir storage determines how much Karnataka can spare. The CWMA's directive is binding, but compliance has historically been contested, with Karnataka repeatedly challenging CWMA orders before the Supreme Court on grounds of inadequate inflows.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT):</strong> Constituted in 1990 under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, the CWDT delivered its final award in 2007 (published in the Gazette in 2013 after Karnataka's challenge). The award allocated 419 TMC of Cauvery waters among the four riparian states and union territory: Karnataka (284.75 TMC), Tamil Nadu (177.25 TMC), Kerala (30 TMC), and Puducherry (7 TMC) — the figures add up after accounting for environmental flows and sea loss of about 10 TMC.</li>
<li><strong>Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA):</strong> A statutory body constituted by the Union government in June 2018 under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, pursuant to a Supreme Court direction in February 2018. The CWMA monitors implementation of the CWDT award, issues monthly release schedules based on actual inflows, and can summon state officials. Its decisions are enforceable but states can approach the Supreme Court for modification.</li>
<li><strong>Article 262 of the Constitution:</strong> Empowers Parliament to provide by law for the adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution, or control of inter-state rivers or river valleys, and may bar the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court or any other court. Under this provision, Parliament enacted the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, to set up ad hoc tribunals for river-water disputes.</li>
<li><strong>Krishnaraja Sagar (KRS) Dam:</strong> Built across the Cauvery in Mandya district of Karnataka (1932), it is the principal storage reservoir in the Cauvery basin in Karnataka with a gross storage capacity of 49.45 TMC. KRS water supplies drinking water to Bengaluru and irrigation to Mandya and Mysuru districts. The reservoir's storage level is the key metric in inter-state release disputes during the southwest monsoon.</li>
<li><strong>Kuruvai Paddy Season:</strong> The short-duration (90–100 day) paddy cultivation season in the Cauvery delta of Tamil Nadu, sown in June–July and harvested in October. It is critically dependent on timely Cauvery releases and fails when Karnataka withholds water during below-normal monsoon years. The kuruvai season supports the livelihoods of over 10 lakh farmers in the delta and is a perennial flashpoint in the inter-state dispute.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Karnataka CM urges EC to extend Special Intensive Revision claims period amid 19.5% deletion rate
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 3 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Electoral rolls; Election Commission of India; Representation of People's Act; voter rights
- GS Paper II → Governance → Transparency and accountability; e-governance in election management

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Karnataka Chief Minister D.K. Shivakumar wrote to the Chief Election Commissioner on 25 August 2026 demanding an extension of the claims and objections period under the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, arguing that the current exercise is deleting an alarming number of genuine voters from the rolls. Karnataka has reported an overall deletion rate of 19.5% across constituencies, with five constituencies seeing more than 50% of registered voters struck off — figures that opposition parties, civil society groups, and independent legal observers have flagged as indicative of a flawed methodology. The Election Commission of India (ECI) had launched the SIR in July 2026 as a roll-cleaning exercise to weed out dead, duplicate, and migrated voters. The CM alleged that many deletions were made without proper field verification, leaving genuine voters — particularly migrant workers and daily-wage laborers who may not receive local enumerator visits — disenfranchised. The SIR process requires voters to submit affidavits affirming their continued residence, and critics say the documentation burden is disproportionate for marginalized communities.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of Electoral Rolls:</strong> A process conducted by the Election Commission of India under Section 21 of the Representation of People Act, 1950, which empowers the ECI to revise electoral rolls at any time. The SIR involves door-to-door surveys by Booth Level Officers (BLOs) to verify continued residence of registered voters and to add eligible new voters. Unlike the Summary Revision (annual), the SIR is more comprehensive and has historically been used before elections in states with large-scale migration or population change.</li>
<li><strong>Election Commission of India (ECI):</strong> A constitutional body under Article 324, which vests the superintendence, direction, and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections in the ECI. <li><strong>Representation of the People Act, 1950 and 1951:</strong> The RPA 1950 deals with the preparation of electoral rolls and delimitation of constituencies; the RPA 1951 deals with the actual conduct of elections, election offences, disqualification of members, and election disputes. <li><strong>Booth Level Officer (BLO):</strong> A government official (usually a school teacher or lower-rung government employee) assigned to each polling booth, responsible for maintaining the accuracy of the electoral roll for that booth, enrolling new voters, and processing deletion requests. BLOs are the ground-level interface of the ECI; complaints of inadequate BLO verification frequently accompany large-scale roll revisions.</li>

A migrant voter enrolled at their place of origin who has not filed a transfer-of-enrollment request loses their effective vote if they cannot return to their constituency. The ECI's Electronically Transmitted Postal Ballot System (ETPBS) and remote voting proposals are intended to address this, but implementation remains partial.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Researchers at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru have identified a class of enzymes from the bovine (cow) gut microbiome that are highly effective at degrading the extracellular polysaccharide matrix of bacterial biofilms — the protective slime-like layer that makes many hospital-acquired infections resistant to conventional antibiotics. The study, published in a peer-reviewed journal, demonstrated that these enzymes (glycoside hydrolases) break down the biofilm of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus — two of the most clinically significant antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) pathogens — by more than 80% in laboratory conditions. Biofilm-associated infections account for 60–80% of all microbial infections and are notoriously difficult to treat because conventional antibiotics cannot penetrate the biofilm matrix. The IISc team isolated the enzymes by screening thousands of microbial communities found in the rumen (stomach) of cows, exploiting the fact that ruminant gut bacteria routinely degrade complex plant polysaccharides structurally similar to biofilm matrices. The research has potential applications in treating chronic wounds, catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs), and ventilator-associated pneumonia — conditions that drive significant morbidity in Indian hospitals.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR):</strong> AMR occurs when bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites evolve to resist the drugs designed to kill them. The WHO has declared AMR one of the top ten global public health threats. India is one of the world's largest consumers of antibiotics (both human and veterinary) and has high rates of resistance — the 2023 India AMR Surveillance Network report showed over 60% resistance to third-generation cephalosporins in E. coli isolates from hospital settings. India's National Action Plan on AMR (2017–2021) is being revised for 2025–2030.</li>
<li><strong>Biofilms:</strong> Structured communities of bacteria encased in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, typically composed of polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and extracellular DNA. Biofilms adhere to surfaces — including medical implants, catheters, and wound surfaces — and provide 1,000 times higher resistance to antibiotics than planktonic (free-floating) bacteria. Biofilm-related infections include endocarditis, cystic fibrosis lung infections, and chronic diabetic foot ulcers.</li>
<li><strong>Indian Institute of Science (IISc):</strong> Established in 1909 in Bengaluru under a partnership between Jamsetji Tata and the Government of India, IISc is India's premier research university and is ranked among the top 200 globally. It is an Institute of National Importance and receives funding from the Department of Science and Technology (DST) and the Department of Biotechnology (DBT). IISc's Centre for Biosystems Science and Engineering (BSSE) has been a hub for synthetic biology and AMR research.</li>
<li><strong>Department of Biotechnology (DBT) and BIRAC:</strong> The DBT under the Ministry of Science and Technology funds biomedical research in India; the Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council (BIRAC), a public-sector enterprise under DBT, provides grants and equity funding for biotech startups. The DBT's National Biopharma Mission and Mission COVID Suraksha have accelerated the pipeline from academic discovery to clinical application — a model relevant to AMR enzyme development.</li>
<li><strong>One Health Approach to AMR:</strong> The One Health framework recognizes that human health, animal health, and environmental health are interconnected. AMR in livestock — driven by prophylactic antibiotic use in poultry and cattle — contributes to human AMR through food, water, and environmental routes. The IISc research, paradoxically, derives a therapeutic tool from the bovine gut microbiome, illustrating how the animal-human microbiome interface can be a source of novel therapeutics even as it is a route of resistance transfer.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Allahabad High Court ruled on 25 August 2026 that the wearing of a hijab (headscarf) is not an "essential religious practice" in Islam and therefore cannot override an institutional dress code prescribed by an educational institution. The case arose from a petition by a nursing student at a government medical college in Uttar Pradesh who was barred from clinical duty for wearing a hijab that did not conform to the prescribed uniform. The court applied the "essential religious practices" (ERP) test — a judicial doctrine developed by the Supreme Court — to hold that while the hijab is a religious practice observed by many Muslim women, it is not so fundamental to Islam that its prohibition would constitute an attack on the religion itself. The court further held that the institutional dress code (which mandated wearing a specific uniform, including headgear, but did not prohibit a headscarf of the same color beneath the uniform cap) was a reasonable restriction under Article 25(2)(a). The ruling follows the Karnataka High Court's 2022 hijab judgment (upheld in part by the Supreme Court in 2023) and is expected to be challenged before the Supreme Court.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Essential Religious Practices (ERP) Doctrine:</strong> A judicial test developed by the Supreme Court in the Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt (1954) — commonly called the Shirur Mutt case — to determine which religious practices are protected under Article 25. Under this test, only practices that are "essential" or "integral" to a religion are constitutionally protected; peripheral or merely customary practices are not. The doctrine has been widely criticised by constitutional scholars for requiring courts to determine theology.</li>
<li><strong>Karnataka Hijab Case (2022):</strong> The Karnataka High Court in Resham v State of Karnataka (2022) ruled that wearing a hijab is not an essential religious practice of Islam and that the state's order prescribing uniforms in pre-university colleges was a valid restriction under Article 25(2)(a). The Supreme Court heard the appeal and delivered a split verdict in October 2023 — Justice Hemant Gupta upheld the HC ruling; Justice Sudhanshu Dhulia set it aside. The case has been referred to a larger bench; it remains sub judice.</li>
<li><strong>Article 25(2)(a):</strong> Permits the state to regulate or restrict any economic, financial, political, or other secular activity associated with religious practice. Courts have used this clause to uphold dress code regulations as "secular" acts of institutional management even when they incidentally affect religious practice.</li>
<li><strong>Reasonable Restriction under Part III:</strong> Fundamental Rights under Articles 19–25 are not absolute. Article 25 is subject to "public order, morality, and health" and to other provisions of Part III. Courts apply the proportionality and reasonableness standard to determine whether a restriction on religious practice is justified — whether the restriction is non-discriminatory, serves a legitimate aim, and is the least restrictive means available.</li>
<li><strong>CEDAW and Women's Religious Dress:</strong> India ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW, 1979) in 1993. The UN Committee on CEDAW has noted that compulsory dress codes — whether requiring or prohibiting religious dress — may constitute discrimination against women when they remove women's agency over their own bodies. This international law lens is often invoked in academic critiques of both hijab bans and compulsory veiling laws globally.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

The Russia-Ukraine war demonstrated the transformative tactical value of loitering munitions at scale.</li>
<li><strong>Make in India in Defence:</strong> A policy initiative launched in 2014 to increase the share of domestic production in defence procurement. Key milestones include the Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020, which created priority categories (Indigenously Designed Developed and Manufactured — IDDM; Make-I; Make-II), and a positive indigenization list barring import of hundreds of defence items. India's defence exports have grown from ₹1,941 crore (2014–15) to over ₹21,083 crore (2023–24), with a target of ₹50,000 crore by 2028.</li>
<li><strong>iDEX (Innovations for Defence Excellence):</strong> A Defence Ministry initiative launched in 2018 that provides grants and contracts to startups and MSMEs for developing defence technologies. iDEX has funded over 350 startups across drones, AI, quantum communication, and materials science. The DRDO ToT mechanism and iDEX together form the two pillars of India's effort to create a domestic defence-industrial ecosystem beyond the traditional DPSUs (Defence Public Sector Undertakings).</li>
<li><strong>Anti-Tank Guided Missiles (ATGM) and MPATGM:</strong> The Man-Portable Anti-Tank Guided Missile (MPATGM) is a third-generation fire-and-forget ATGM developed by DRDO with a range of 2.5 km. It uses an imaging infrared (IIR) seeker and can destroy tanks with explosive reactive armour. MPATGM is designed to replace the ageing Milan missile (a Franco-German system inducted in 1977) in Indian Army service. Its induction and private-sector production will reduce India's dependence on French missile manufacturer MBDA.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<li><strong>Intelligence Bureau (IB):</strong> India's domestic intelligence agency, operating under the Ministry of Home Affairs. Unlike RAW (which handles foreign intelligence) and the military intelligence directorates, the IB's mandate is internal security — counter-terrorism, counter-espionage, monitoring extremist organisations, and VIP security intelligence. The IB was established in 1887 during British rule and is the world's oldest surviving intelligence agency. It has no statutory basis and operates under executive order, a lacuna identified by multiple review committees.</li>
<li><strong>NATGRID (National Intelligence Grid):</strong> A project to create a nationwide database linking 21 sets of databases from immigration, banks, tax authorities, airlines, railways, and telecom companies for real-time intelligence analysis. It was conceived after the 26/11 attacks to enable rapid detection of terror suspects through their digital footprints. Implementation has been partial and slow due to privacy concerns, technical challenges, and inter-agency turf issues. NATGRID works under the Ministry of Home Affairs.</li>

In India, the Punjab-Pakistan border is the most acute flashpoint: drones from Pakistan have been used to drop heroin and weapons simultaneously. The Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB), BSF, and Punjab Police operate a coordinated response. The NDPS Act (Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985) and UAPA (Unlawful Activities Prevention Act, 1967) are the primary legal instruments used to prosecute narco-terror cases.</li>
<li><strong>26/11 Mumbai Attacks and Intelligence Reform:</strong> The November 2008 terrorist attack on Mumbai (10 coordinated attacks, 166 killed) exposed critical failures in intelligence coordination, coastal security, and inter-agency communication. The subsequent reforms included creation of the National Investigation Agency (NIA) in 2009, the Multi-Agency Centre (MAC) for intelligence fusion, the establishment of the National Security Guard (NSG) hubs in four cities, and the NATGRID project. The 2026 conference's focus on an integrated intelligence platform reflects that implementation of 26/11 recommendations is still ongoing 18 years later.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law):</strong> Added to the Constitution by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985, the Tenth Schedule disqualifies a member of Parliament or state legislature who voluntarily gives up party membership or votes/abstains against the party whip without prior permission. An exception exists for mergers: a member is not disqualified if at least two-thirds of the legislative party merges with another party. The Schedule designates the Speaker/Chairman as the sole authority to decide disqualification petitions.</li>
<li><strong>Speaker as Adjudicator — Constitutional Problem:</strong> The Tenth Schedule vests disqualification decisions in the Speaker, but Speakers are typically elected members of the ruling party and face a conflict of interest when adjudicating intra-ruling-party disputes. The Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu (1992) upheld the Tenth Schedule but held that Speaker's decisions are subject to judicial review for procedural irregularities and violation of natural justice. Multiple Law Commission and Constitution Review Commission reports have recommended transferring this power to an independent tribunal.</li>
<li><strong>Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Speaker, Manipur (2020):</strong> The Supreme Court held that unreasonable delay by Speakers in deciding disqualification petitions itself becomes a subject of judicial intervention. The court directed the Manipur Speaker to decide a pending defection petition within four weeks. This precedent has been cited repeatedly in petitions across states (Maharashtra, Goa, Arunachal Pradesh) where Speakers have kept defection petitions pending for months or years to protect rebel legislators aligned with the ruling party.</li>
<li><strong>Whip and Party Directions:</strong> Under parliamentary convention, a political party issues a "whip" directing its legislative members to vote in a specified way. Violation of a three-line whip (the most binding form) can trigger disqualification under the Tenth Schedule. A member may vote differently from the whip only if the party has granted prior permission or if the conscience vote has been permitted by the party for a particular issue. Not all votes are covered — votes on private member bills or some procedural matters may not attract the whip.</li>
<li><strong>91st Constitutional Amendment (2003):</strong> Amended the Tenth Schedule to tighten the anti-defection law by eliminating the "one-third split" provision (previously, if one-third of a legislative party broke away, they were not disqualified). Now only a merger of at least two-thirds is exempt. The amendment also capped the size of the Council of Ministers at 15% of the legislative strength (or a minimum of 12), limiting the use of cabinet posts as an incentive for defection.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<li><strong>Caste Census History in India:</strong> The British-era Census enumerated caste from 1881 to 1931. **Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (UNCERD) released its periodic review of India on 25 August 2026, flagging multiple concerns: continuing caste-based discrimination and atrocities against Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes despite legal prohibitions; inadequate implementation of the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act; high levels of manual scavenging; displacement of tribal communities without free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) in the name of development projects; and the treatment of Rohingya refugees in India as "illegal immigrants" subject to arbitrary detention and deportation without individual refugee status determination. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>UNCERD (UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination):</strong> A treaty body that monitors compliance with the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD, 1965). India ratified ICERD in 1968. States must submit periodic reports; UNCERD reviews them and issues "concluding observations" — which are not legally binding but carry significant normative weight. India has consistently contested UNCERD's jurisdiction over caste discrimination, arguing that caste is a social phenomenon distinct from race.</li>
<li><strong>SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989:</strong> Also known as the Atrocities Act or POA Act, it criminalizes specific acts of humiliation, violence, and discrimination against SC and ST members committed by non-SC/ST persons. Offences include forcing untouchability practices, sexual exploitation, forcible land occupation, and preventing access to water sources. The 2015 Amendment strengthened penalties and added new offences. The National Crime Records Bureau data consistently shows high rates of atrocity crimes, with conviction rates remaining low (below 30% in most states).</li>
<li><strong>Rohingya Refugees in India:</strong> Approximately 40,000–55,000 Rohingya from Myanmar have sought refuge in India, primarily in Jammu, Hyderabad, Delhi, and Nuh (Haryana). The Supreme Court in 2017 refused to stay their deportation, allowing the government to proceed with deportation of those lacking valid documents. UNHCR has registered many Rohingya and issued refugee certificates, but these have no legal standing under Indian law.</li>
<li><strong>Caste-as-Race Debate in International Law:</strong> UNCERD in 2002 issued a General Recommendation (No. 29) on descent-based discrimination, explicitly stating that caste discrimination falls within the purview of ICERD as "discrimination based on descent." India, Japan, Nepal, and other countries with caste systems have challenged this interpretation, arguing that descent and race are distinct categories and that ICERD was not intended to address caste. This remains one of the live debates in international human rights law.</li>
<li><strong>Manual Scavenging:</strong> The practice of manually cleaning, carrying, disposing of, or handling human excreta from dry latrines and open defecation sites. It is prohibited by the Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act, 2013, which replaced the 1993 law. The 2013 Act also mandates rehabilitation (livelihoods, housing, education for children) of those released from the practice. Despite the legal prohibition, the 2018 National Survey and 2023 socio-economic data identified over 66,000 manual scavengers nationally — most of them from Dalit communities, particularly the Valmiki and Balmiki sub-castes.</li>
</ul>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Shipping Board (NSB):</strong> A statutory body constituted under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, to advise the government on matters relating to Indian shipping. It consists of representatives of ship owners, seafarers, port users, and government nominees. The NSB has historically called for greater policy support for Indian shipping, which has declined sharply relative to global fleet growth since the 1980s when India had a more prominent merchant marine.</li>
<li><strong>Tonnage Tax Regime:</strong> A special tax regime for shipping companies introduced in India in 2004 (Finance Act 2004), under which a company can opt to pay tax based on the "notional income" derived from the tonnage of its fleet rather than actual profits. This regime, common in maritime nations (UK, Netherlands, Greece), provides tax certainty and encourages fleet investment. India's tonnage tax has been criticized for its high floor rates compared to European regimes, reducing its attractiveness for Indian shipping companies.</li>
<li><strong>Sagarmala Programme:</strong> Launched by the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways in 2015, Sagarmala aims to modernize ports, develop port-led industrialization, and improve coastal shipping and inland waterways. It is India's most comprehensive maritime development programme. Projects include port development, rail and road connectivity to hinterlands, and development of fisheries and cruise tourism. The programme targets reducing India's logistics costs from about 14% of GDP to 8% by 2035.</li>
<li><strong>India's Logistics Cost Problem:</strong> India's logistics cost as a share of GDP (approximately 13–14%) is significantly higher than developed economies (7–9% in the US, 9–11% in China). High logistics costs reduce export competitiveness, inflate inflation, and squeeze manufacturing margins. The government's National Logistics Policy (2022) targets reducing logistics costs to 8% of GDP through multi-modal infrastructure, technology adoption (e-waybill, FASTag), and regulatory rationalization.</li>
<li><strong>Deadweight Tonnage (DWT) and Merchant Fleet Classification:</strong> DWT is the total weight a ship can safely carry, including cargo, fuel, and crew supplies. Merchant ships are classified by type: bulk carriers (iron ore, coal, grain), tankers (crude oil, LNG, chemicals), and container ships (manufactured goods). India's major exports by sea include petroleum products, iron ore, and engineering goods; imports include crude oil, coal, fertilizers, and electronic goods. Owning Indian-flagged ships for these trades would reduce foreign exchange outflows significantly.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  2026 monsoon forecast failures: El Niño, La Niña, and the limits of AI weather models
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 7 · Science</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → AI; weather forecasting; satellite-based meteorology; climate modeling
- GS Paper I → Geography → Indian monsoon system; El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO); climate variability
- GS Paper III → Environment → Climate change impacts; disaster management; agricultural risk

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A Text & Context feature examined why the 2026 southwest monsoon was so difficult to forecast: the India Meteorological Department (IMD), the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), and AI-based models including Google DeepMind's GraphCast all issued initial June forecasts of normal-to-above-normal rainfall, yet August saw a severe rainfall deficit in the Gangetic plains (-42%) alongside flooding in northeast India. The article explained that the transition from El Niño conditions (which suppress the Indian monsoon) to neutral or La Niña conditions (which typically enhance it) during the June–August window created a "transition year" uncertainty that even the most sophisticated ensemble models could not capture reliably. The piece noted that AI weather models are trained primarily on ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis data (1940–present) and therefore inherit the biases and data gaps of that dataset — including poor representation of the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) dynamics and the Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO), both of which critically modulate the Indian monsoon. The article called for an Indian-developed AI foundation model for monsoon forecasting trained on IMD's own dense observational network.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO):</strong> A coupled ocean-atmosphere phenomenon in the tropical Pacific that oscillates between El Niño (warm phase — suppresses Indian monsoon), neutral, and La Niña (cool phase — often enhances Indian monsoon) conditions. ENSO is the single most important predictor of Indian monsoon variability; an El Niño year typically reduces all-India rainfall by 10–15%. The 2023 El Niño was one of the strongest on record and contributed to a weak 2023 Indian monsoon. The 2024–25 transition to La Niña conditions was delayed, creating forecast uncertainty for 2026.</li>
<li><strong>Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD):</strong> An ENSO-like climate mode in the Indian Ocean characterized by anomalous warming or cooling of the western Indian Ocean relative to the eastern Indian Ocean. A positive IOD (warmer west) is associated with above-normal Indian monsoon rainfall; a negative IOD suppresses monsoon rains. The IOD can either reinforce or partially offset ENSO effects on the Indian monsoon, making combined ENSO-IOD years particularly difficult to forecast. The IOD was first identified in 1999 and is now a standard predictor in IMD seasonal forecast models.</li>
<li><strong>Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO):</strong> The largest element of intra-seasonal (30–60 day) variability in the tropical atmosphere, the MJO is a slow-moving pulse of enhanced and suppressed rainfall that moves eastward around the tropics. During its active phase over the Indian Ocean and Bay of Bengal, it can significantly boost monsoon rainfall for 10–20 day periods ("active spells"); during its suppressed phase, it causes "break spells" — prolonged dry periods even during the monsoon season. MJO is now a key input for extended-range monsoon forecasts (weeks 3–4).</li>
<li><strong>AI-based Weather Forecasting (GraphCast, Pangu-Weather):</strong> Machine learning models trained on historical weather reanalysis data that can produce global 10-day forecasts in seconds rather than the hours required by numerical weather prediction (NWP) models. Google DeepMind's GraphCast (2023) and Huawei's Pangu-Weather have matched or exceeded ECMWF NWP accuracy on standard metrics. However, they are "data-driven" — they interpolate patterns seen in training data and struggle with conditions outside their training distribution, such as unprecedented climate extremes or novel ENSO-IOD interaction regimes.</li>
<li><strong>IMD's Seasonal Forecast Model:</strong> IMD uses a Statistical Ensemble Forecast System (SEFS) and dynamical models (including a coupled ocean-atmosphere model) to issue its annual April and June long-range forecasts. IMD's 2026 April forecast predicted "above normal" rainfall (≥106% of Long Period Average); the revised June forecast maintained this. The article noted that IMD's track record on qualitative (above/below/normal) forecasts is reasonable (correct ~70% of the time) but spatial and temporal distribution forecasting within a season remains a major challenge — a limitation with direct consequences for agricultural planning and water resource management.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

The editorial argued that the collegium system, though judicially sanctioned through the Three Judges Cases, operates with zero public accountability: recommendations are made without published criteria, no demographic data on the pool of candidates considered is released, conflict-of-interest declarations by collegium members are absent, and rejected names are returned without written reasons. It called for: publication of a shortlist of candidates considered; mandatory recusal of collegium members with a direct professional relationship with a candidate; a secretariat to manage and audit the recommendations process; and mandatory annual diversity reporting on appointments by gender, community, and geographic origin of judges.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>The Collegium System (Three Judges Cases):</strong> Judicial appointments to the Supreme Court and High Courts are governed by Articles 124(2) and 217(1) of the Constitution, which require consultation with the Chief Justice of India (CJI). Through three Supreme Court judgments — the First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta v Union of India, 1981), Second Judges Case (1993), and Third Judges Case (1998) — the court progressively reinterpreted "consultation" to mean "concurrence," effectively transferring appointment power from the executive to the CJI-led collegium (a panel of the 5 senior-most SC judges for SC appointments; 3 senior judges for HC appointments). The NJAC (99th Amendment, 2014) attempted to replace the collegium with an independent commission but was struck down in 2015.</li>
<li><strong>National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) — 99th Amendment (2014):</strong> Parliament unanimously passed the Constitutional Amendment creating a 6-member NJAC to replace the collegium, comprising the CJI, two senior SC judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court struck it down in Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India (2015), holding that it violated judicial independence (a basic structure doctrine element) by giving the executive a veto and including lay members in what it characterised as a judicial function.</li>
<li><strong>Judicial Diversity in India:</strong> India's higher judiciary suffers from significant underrepresentation of women, religious minorities, and lower castes. As of 2026, women constitute approximately 12% of SC judges and 11% of HC judges — up from 3% in 2000. There is no SC or ST judge in the Supreme Court as of 2026. No official data is published on caste or religious identity of judges, making diversity monitoring virtually impossible. The Bar Council of India and several academic studies have called for a diversity commission within the judiciary.</li>
<li><strong>Government's Role in Judicial Appointments:</strong> Under the collegium system, the government retains the power to return recommendations for reconsideration (raising "intelligence inputs" or "administrative concerns") but must accept a reiterated recommendation. However, the government has increasingly used delays — keeping names pending for months or years without formal rejection — as a form of informal veto. The Supreme Court has noted this practice with displeasure; in 2021 it observed that "procrastination in clearance" of collegium recommendations is "a matter of concern."</li>
<li><strong>RTI and the Judiciary:</strong> The Supreme Court and High Courts are partially covered by the Right to Information Act, 2005 — administrative matters are subject to RTI disclosure but judicial decisions and collegium deliberations are excluded under the exception for "deliberative processes." The Central Information Commission has held that collegium recommendations, once finalized, are disclosable under RTI. The Supreme Court's Registry has been inconsistent in complying with RTI requests on collegium processes, and the matter has been litigated before the SC itself.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Drawing lessons from the May 2025 India-Pakistan confrontation, the editorial argued that India's Air Force performed effectively in precision strike roles but that the absence of integrated theatre commands meant that joint operations coordination between the IAF, Army, and Navy depended on ad hoc workarounds rather than institutionalized processes. The editorial noted that theatre-isation — the restructuring of India's military into joint geographic commands (currently proposed: Air Defence Command, Maritime Theatre Command, and two continental theatre commands for China and Pakistan fronts) — has been delayed for six years due to inter-service turf disputes, particularly the IAF's resistance to placing air assets under a joint theatre commander. It cited the Kargil Review Committee (2000) and the Naresh Chandra Task Force (2012) as having both recommended jointness reforms that remain substantially unimplemented. The editorial argued that future multi-domain conflicts (cyber + space + conventional) will require institutionalized jointness, and that further delay increases strategic risk.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Theatre Commands and Integrated Theatre Commands (ITCs):</strong> Theatre commands integrate army, navy, and air force assets under a single joint commander responsible for a geographic area or functional domain. Currently India has 17 single-service commands (Army — 7, Navy — 3, IAF — 7) plus two functional joint commands (Strategic Forces Command for nuclear weapons, Andaman and Nicobar Command). The proposed ITC restructuring would reduce this to 5–6 commands. Theatre-isation was a flagship agenda of CDS General Bipin Rawat (2020–2021) and has been in various stages of planning since his death.</li>
<li><strong>Kargil Review Committee (2000):</strong> Constituted after the 1999 Kargil War, the committee headed by K. Subrahmanyam identified fundamental failures in intelligence, strategic surprise prevention, and inter-service coordination. Its recommendations included creation of the CDS post (implemented 20 years later in 2020), a National Security Council Secretariat, and joint planning mechanisms. The committee's findings remain the foundational document for Indian defence reform.</li>
<li><strong>IAF's Resistance to Theatre-isation:</strong> The Indian Air Force has historically resisted theatre-isation on the grounds that air power is inherently indivisible — air assets must be centrally commanded to be optimally deployed across theatres. The IAF's position is that placing its aircraft under theatre commanders would fragment air power and reduce strategic flexibility. This contrasts with the US model where theatre combatant commanders have operational control over air assets from day-1 of a conflict. The debate reflects a fundamental doctrinal divide about the nature of modern air warfare.</li>
<li><strong>Multi-Domain Operations (MDO):</strong> A US Army doctrinal concept adopted widely in military thinking worldwide, MDO describes conflict that occurs simultaneously across land, sea, air, space, and cyberspace domains, with information as an overarching domain. Effective MDO requires unified command, shared situational awareness, and rapid cross-domain decision-making — all of which depend on institutionalized joint structures that India currently lacks. The May 2025 conflict reportedly involved significant cyber and space components (satellite imagery, GPS spoofing, electronic warfare) that highlighted MDO gaps.</li>
<li><strong>Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020 and Indigenization:</strong> Joint military operations also require integrated logistics and common platforms — an area where India's procurement silos (each service buys its own equipment with little interoperability planning) create friction. The DAP 2020 introduced a Joint Requirements process for inter-service procurement but implementation remains early-stage. Theatre-isation without accompanying procurement jointness and logistics integration would be incomplete.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Tamil Nadu Revenue Augmentation Committee: a model for state fiscal reform India should emulate
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 9 · Opinion</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Government budgeting; state finances; tax administration; fiscal federalism
- GS Paper II → Governance → E-governance; financial accountability; state-level reform

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
An opinion piece praised Tamil Nadu's Revenue Augmentation Committee (RAC), constituted in 2021 under former Finance Secretary K. Gnanadesikan, for its systematic approach to improving own-tax revenue without increasing tax rates. The RAC's recommendations — which Tamil Nadu has been steadily implementing — include: closing GST filing loopholes for intra-state trade, expanding the stamp duty base to include digital property transactions, leveraging GIS data to identify unregistered commercial properties for property tax net expansion, and automating vehicle registration revenue through integration with the VAHAN database. The piece noted that Tamil Nadu's state own-tax revenue has grown 18% year-on-year since 2022, significantly exceeding the national average and allowing the state to fund its welfare schemes without the same level of borrowing stress seen in states like Punjab and Andhra Pradesh. The author argued that other states — particularly those facing fiscal stress — should establish similar standing committees staffed by technocrats rather than politicians, with a specific mandate to identify revenue leakage without legislative tax increases.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>State Own-Tax Revenue (SOTR):</strong> State governments in India have their own revenue sources including State GST (SGST), state excise (on alcohol), stamp duty and registration fees, motor vehicle tax, and electricity duty. SOTR is a key metric of a state's fiscal health and self-reliance. States with low SOTR are more dependent on transfers from the Union (devolution of central taxes and grants-in-aid), making them vulnerable to central policy changes. Tamil Nadu has one of the highest SOTR as a percentage of state GDP among major Indian states.</li>
<li><strong>Fiscal Federalism and the 15th Finance Commission:</strong> The Finance Commission, constituted under Article 280 of the Constitution, recommends the vertical (Union-State) and horizontal (inter-state) sharing of central taxes. The 15th Finance Commission (2020–2025) recommended 41% devolution of the divisible pool to states. However, states increasingly complain that the vertical devolution is insufficient as the Centre raises non-divisible cess and surcharges (which do not enter the divisible pool) — a structural fiscal federalism concern.</li>
<li><strong>GST and State Revenue Autonomy:</strong> The Goods and Services Tax (GST), implemented from 1 July 2017 under the 101st Constitutional Amendment, subsumed most state indirect taxes into a unified SGST + CGST structure. States lost direct control over tax rates (which require GST Council consensus) in exchange for a revenue-neutral transition. States were compensated for GST shortfalls for five years (2017–2022) but the compensation period has ended, leaving states that were revenue-neutral on pre-GST taxes now exposed to GST buoyancy variability.</li>
<li><strong>VAHAN and Parivahan — Digital Vehicle Registry:</strong> VAHAN is the National Register of Motor Vehicles maintained by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, containing data on over 350 million registered vehicles. States integrate their RTO databases with VAHAN through Parivahan Sewa portal to automate vehicle registration and fitness certification. Integration with VAHAN allows states to more accurately compute motor vehicle tax (a significant own-revenue source) and detect tax evasion — the Tamil Nadu RAC's leveraging of VAHAN data is a model of e-governance applied to revenue administration.</li>
<li><strong>State Fiscal Stress — Punjab and Andhra Pradesh:</strong> Several Indian states face severe fiscal stress characterised by high revenue deficits, large contingent liabilities from state-owned enterprises and state guarantees, and debt-to-GSDP ratios exceeding the FRBM-recommended 20%. Punjab's debt is approximately 44% of GSDP; Andhra Pradesh (post-bifurcation) has struggled with legacy liabilities. The contrast with Tamil Nadu's more disciplined fiscal management, despite similarly large welfare expenditures, illustrates the impact of revenue administration quality on fiscal health.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Opinion: Karnataka SIR's 19.5% deletion rate is democracy's warning sign — EC must act
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 9 · Opinion</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Electoral rolls; Election Commission; voter disenfranchisement; democratic accountability
- GS Paper II → Governance → Transparency; rule of law; fundamental rights (right to vote)

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
An opinion piece flagged the alarming scale of deletions from Karnataka's electoral rolls under the ongoing Special Intensive Revision (SIR) — an average of 19.5% across the state, with some urban constituencies seeing deletions of 50–65% of their registered voters. The author argued that this is statistically implausible: a deletion rate of 20% would imply that one-fifth of all registered voters in Karnataka had either died, migrated, or were duplicates — far exceeding any reasonable estimate. Drawing on RTI responses from district electoral officers in three Bengaluru constituencies, the author showed that the majority of deletions were based on "field survey" findings where the enumerator marked a voter as "not traceable" — a process that can result in genuine voters being struck off if they are at work or out of the house during the single visit. The author called for: mandatory second verification before any deletion; a public grievance portal for voters to appeal proposed deletions; independent audit of SIR deletion rates by district; and prosecution of BLOs who file false "not traceable" reports. The piece situated the Karnataka SIR within a broader national concern about the integrity of electoral rolls.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Electoral Roll Integrity — Legal Framework:</strong> The Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 (framed under the Representation of the People Act, 1950) prescribe the procedure for deletion of names from electoral rolls. Under Rule 21A, a person can be deleted only after they have been given notice and an opportunity to be heard. Field deletions based on "not found at address" are supposed to be provisional, with the voter given 30 days to appear before the Electoral Registration Officer (ERO). Critics allege this procedure is routinely bypassed in practice.</li>
<li><strong>Right to Vote and Constitutional Status:</strong> The right to vote is not listed as a Fundamental Right under Part III of the Constitution — it is a statutory right created by the Representation of People's Act, 1951. However, the Supreme Court has in several judgments (including Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, 1978) held that free and fair elections are a basic feature of the Constitution and that the ECI has plenary powers to ensure this, including the duty to maintain accurate electoral rolls. The People's Union for Civil Liberties (PUCL) v Union of India (2003) further held that voters have a right to know candidates' criminal antecedents — expanding the relationship between voters and their constitutional rights.</li>
<li><strong>Booth Level Officers (BLOs) — Accountability Gap:</strong> BLOs are typically government employees (primary school teachers, revenue officials) assigned to verify voter rolls at a single polling booth of ~1,200 voters. They are responsible for adding new voters (Form 6), deleting dead or migrated voters (Form 7), and correcting errors (Form 8). BLOs receive a small honorarium for this work (₹1,000–2,000 per roll revision cycle) and have no specific statutory accountability for errors or falsifications. The ECI trains BLOs but has limited capacity to audit the quality of their fieldwork across 1 million+ polling stations nationally.</li>
<li><strong>Urban Migration and Roll Accuracy:</strong> India's rapid urbanization has created a structural challenge for electoral rolls: millions of internal migrants are registered to vote in their place of origin but work in cities and may not return to vote; they may also have registered at their city address without deleting the original entry, creating duplicates. Urban constituencies (especially in Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi) therefore tend to have the highest rates of ghost entries, migration-related absences, and anomalous deletions. The ECI's remote voting pilot and Elector Photo Identity Card (EPIC) linkage with Aadhaar (proposed but challenged in courts) are intended to address this.</li>
<li><strong>Right to Information (RTI) as Electoral Accountability Tool:</strong> Civil society organisations and journalists have increasingly used RTI applications to audit electoral processes — from booth-wise deletion lists to training records of BLOs to party-wise voter share statistics. The ECI partially exempts its internal deliberative processes from RTI but election-related data (final electoral rolls, deletion totals) is public. RTI-based journalism has been instrumental in exposing discrepancies in several states' roll revisions, contributing to the kind of public scrutiny the opinion piece advocates.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Reserve Bank of India's August 2026 Bulletin — an internal research publication — flagged that the ongoing conflict in West Asia (involving Israel, Iran, and Houthi forces in the Red Sea) is weighing on India's economic outlook through three channels: (1) elevated crude oil prices (Brent crude at $88–92/barrel) above the $75 baseline assumed in India's fiscal calculations, increasing India's import bill and widening the current account deficit; (2) Red Sea shipping disruptions that have rerouted Indian exports and imports via the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10–15 days to transit times and 20–30% to freight costs; and (3) suppressed remittance growth from Indian expatriates in Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, where economic activity has softened due to oil-revenue uncertainty. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>RBI Bulletin:</strong> A monthly publication of the Reserve Bank of India containing research articles, data, and speeches by RBI officials. Unlike the Monetary Policy Report (bi-annual) or Annual Report, the Bulletin is not a policy document but provides analytical perspectives on economic and financial conditions. Articles in the Bulletin express the views of individual authors and not the RBI as an institution, though they reflect concerns that inform RBI's thinking.</li>
<li><strong>India's Crude Oil Import Dependence:</strong> India imports approximately 85% of its crude oil requirement, making it the world's third-largest oil importer. India's crude oil import bill for 2024–25 was approximately $130 billion. A $10/barrel increase in crude prices widens India's current account deficit by approximately $12–15 billion annually. India has been diversifying its crude import sources — Russia now supplies ~35%, Middle East ~45%, and other sources ~20% — but the Middle East remains critical for both crude oil and LPG imports.</li>
<li><strong>Red Sea Crisis and Houthi Attacks:</strong> Beginning in November 2023, Yemen's Houthi movement launched missile and drone attacks on commercial shipping in the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb strait, targeting vessels with perceived links to Israel. By 2026, this had caused a prolonged rerouting of approximately 20–25% of global container traffic away from the Suez Canal via the Cape of Good Hope — adding 10–14 days and significant cost to shipping. The IMF estimated the Red Sea crisis reduced global trade volume by 1.3% and increased shipping costs by 400–800% (container freight rates).</li>
<li><strong>India-GCC Relations and Remittances:</strong> India receives the world's largest remittances — approximately $120 billion in 2024 (World Bank data). Gulf countries (UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain) collectively account for approximately 35–40% of total Indian remittances, sent by an estimated 9 million Indian workers. Remittances are a critical source of foreign exchange and support consumption in labour-exporting states like Kerala, UP, Bihar, and Rajasthan. GCC economic softness reduces construction and hospitality employment — the primary sectors for Indian migrant workers.</li>
<li><strong>Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) and Inflation Targeting:</strong> India adopted a flexible inflation targeting framework in 2016, setting an inflation target of 4% (with a tolerance band of ±2%) for the MPC under the RBI Act. The MPC has six members — three from the RBI (including the Governor, who chairs it) and three external members appointed by the government. The MPC sets the repo rate (the rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks) as its primary instrument. If CPI inflation remains above 6% for three consecutive quarters, the MPC must write a report to the government explaining the failure and its remedial action — a discipline mechanism that makes the MPC's external-pressure-awareness directly policy-relevant.</li>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Compressed biogas sector struggles with feedstock supply chains; policy gaps flagged
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Agriculture</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 13 · Business</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Bioenergy; renewable energy; waste management; agricultural residue
- GS Paper III → Agriculture → Stubble burning; farm waste; circular economy

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India's compressed biogas (CBG) sector, promoted under the Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation (SATAT) scheme since 2018, is facing a critical feedstock supply chain problem: plants struggle to source sufficient quantities of agricultural residue, municipal solid waste (MSW), and animal dung at economically viable prices to operate at full capacity. An industry survey cited in the article found that fewer than 30% of the 1,000+ SATAT-registered plants are operating above 50% capacity, and several have shut down due to feedstock uncertainty. Key bottlenecks include: absence of an organized municipal organic waste segregation system that can supply MSW to biogas plants reliably; competition from direct-use applications for crop residue (mulching, cattle feed); lack of a minimum assured price for biogas feedstock that would incentivize farmers to supply straw rather than burn it; and insufficient blending mandates that would guarantee offtake of CBG by oil marketing companies (OMCs). Stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana — a major air quality and health issue — remains prevalent partly because farmers lack economically attractive alternatives, despite biogas conversion being technically viable.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Compressed Biogas (CBG) and the SATAT Scheme:</strong> CBG is biogas (primarily methane) compressed to about 250 bar for use as a vehicular fuel, equivalent to Compressed Natural Gas (CNG). The SATAT (Sustainable Alternative Towards Affordable Transportation) scheme, launched in 2018 by the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, provides a guaranteed procurement price (₹46/kg initially) and allows private entrepreneurs to set up CBG plants and sell to Oil Marketing Companies (IOC, BPCL, HPCL). The scheme targets production of 15 million metric tonnes of CBG per year by 2024 — a target far from achieved.</li>
<li><strong>Stubble Burning and Air Quality:</strong> Post-harvest burning of paddy stubble in Punjab and Haryana (October–November) is a primary contributor to the severe air pollution in Delhi-NCR during the post-monsoon season. Stubble burning releases PM2.5, NOx, and CO — pollutants linked to respiratory disease. The National Green Tribunal (NGT) has repeatedly banned stubble burning and imposed penalties on violating farmers. The central government's ex-situ management schemes (offering subsidies for machines to collect and transport stubble to biogas or biomass power plants) have seen low adoption due to logistics costs.</li>
<li><strong>Biogas vs Biomethane — Policy Distinction:</strong> Biogas is the raw product of anaerobic digestion (typically 50–65% methane, 35–45% CO₂). Biomethane (or Renewable Natural Gas — RNG) is biogas purified to >95% methane and either injected into the natural gas grid or compressed as CBG. India's gas grid injection policy for biomethane has been framed but not fully operationalized; CBG is the commercially predominant form. The EU mandates biomethane grid injection; India's approach of dedicated OMC offtake through SATAT is an alternative model but requires reliable feedstock aggregation.</li>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">

</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">AI</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → AI; autonomous weapons; robotics; dual-use technology governance
- GS Paper II → International Relations → International institutions; disarmament; humanitarian law

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6kb 0;">
A joint statement by the UN Secretary-General and the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) on 25 August 2026 warned that Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) — AI-driven weapons that can select and engage targets without meaningful human control — are "no longer hypothetical" and that proliferation to non-state actors is already occurring, based on battlefield observations in Ukraine, Gaza, and Sudan. The statement called for an immediate global moratorium on LAWS and the commencement of negotiations on a legally binding international instrument to prohibit or strictly regulate them under International Humanitarian Law (IHL). The statement noted that the existing Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) framework at the UN in Geneva has been deadlocked for a decade, with Russia, the U.S., and Israel blocking the opening of negotiations. India's position on LAWS has evolved from cautious observer to active supporter of regulation — a shift driven partly by India's own development of AI-assisted weapons systems and its assessment that unregulated LAWS would primarily advantage technologically superior adversaries in asymmetric conflict.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS):</strong> LAWS are weapon systems that, once activated, can select and engage targets without human intervention. They are distinct from remote-controlled weapons (where a human operator makes the kill decision) and from fire-and-forget missiles (which are pre-programmed but have no situational judgment). Examples include loitering munitions with AI-enabled target recognition, autonomous naval mines, and AI-guided drone swarms. Key ethical concern: Article 36 of the Geneva Conventions requires meaningful human control over the decision to use lethal force to ensure compliance with IHL principles of distinction and proportionality.</li>
<li><strong>Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW):</strong> A 1980 UN treaty (with protocols) that prohibits or restricts certain types of weapons deemed excessively injurious or indiscriminate. LAWS have been discussed under the CCW's Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) since 2014, but consensus for a binding protocol has been blocked by major military powers (especially Russia and the U.S.) who insist on a non-binding "political declaration" approach. The CCW operates by consensus, giving any state a veto over outcomes.</li>
<li><strong>International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and LAWS:</strong> IHL (codified in the Geneva Conventions and their Additional Protocols) requires all weapons users to distinguish between combatants and civilians (distinction), ensure that harm to civilians is not disproportionate to military advantage (proportionality), and take all feasible precautions to minimize civilian harm (precaution). Critics of LAWS argue that current AI cannot reliably satisfy these principles in complex battlefield environments with civilians present — and that even if it could, accountability (for violations) is impossible when a machine makes the kill decision.</li>
<li><strong>India's Position on AI Governance in Defence:</strong> India has been increasingly active in international AI governance discussions, including the Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit (2023) and subsequent Seoul AI Summit (2024), but has been more cautious about binding commitments on defence AI applications — a balance it must maintain between its growing AI-defence industrial complex and its support for multilateralism. India's Defence AI Council and the Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX) programme are developing indigenous AI for various military applications, creating a domestic stake in the regulatory question.</li>
<li><strong>ICRC's Mandate and Non-State Actor Proliferation:</strong> The International Committee of the Red Cross is the guardian of International Humanitarian Law — it is a neutral, impartial humanitarian organization with a unique international mandate. The ICRC has been one of the most consistent and early advocates for LAWS regulation, publishing major reports in 2011, 2018, and 2023 on the legal and ethical challenges. Its concern about non-state actor access to LAWS reflects the observation that commercially available drone technology (including AI-guided FPV drones) has already been weaponized by armed groups in Ukraine, Gaza, and the Sahel at relatively low cost.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Russian President Vladimir Putin signed a presidential decree on 25 August 2026 allowing the Russian state to temporarily seize and assume operational control of private or municipal infrastructure — including energy facilities, transport networks, and communication systems — that has been damaged or destroyed by Ukrainian drone or missile strikes, and has not been repaired within 30 days. The decree is framed as a wartime emergency measure to maintain essential services and accelerate repair timelines but has drawn sharp criticism from Russian business groups and legal scholars who argue it effectively allows expropriation without judicial oversight. The context is significant: Ukrainian long-range drone strikes (enabled by Western technology transfer) have increasingly targeted Russian energy infrastructure deep inside Russia — oil refineries, power stations, and rail yards — causing cumulative disruption to Russian domestic energy supply and weakening war-economy morale. The decree signals that Russia's wartime economy is under sufficient pressure that the state is resorting to centralized control mechanisms last used in the Soviet era. For India, the decree raises concerns about the security of Indian investments and projects in Russia and the long-term viability of Russian energy supplies at discounted prices.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Russia-Ukraine War — Current Trajectory (August 2026):</strong> The conflict, which began with Russia's full-scale invasion on 24 February 2022, has entered a grinding attritional phase. Russia controls approximately 20% of Ukraine's territory (including Crimea, occupied since 2014, and most of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson oblasts). Ukraine has sustained its defence through Western arms supplies (including F-16 fighters, long-range ATACMS missiles, and Storm Shadow cruise missiles) and its own domestic drone manufacturing. Neither side has achieved a decisive breakthrough since the failed 2023 Ukrainian counteroffensive.</li>
<li><strong>Ukrainian Drone Strikes on Russian Territory:</strong> Ukraine has developed an extensive long-range drone programme (primarily one-way attack UAVs — FPV drones for close range, and Shahed-like OWA drones for long range) targeting Russian oil refineries (Moscow, Ryazan, Saratov), airfields (Engels, Novoshakhtinsk), and military logistics hubs. Drone strikes on Russian energy infrastructure since late 2023 have caused measurable reductions in Russian refining capacity and domestic fuel availability, contributing to internal economic pressure that the Putin decree reflects.</li>
<li><strong>Wartime Economic Controls in Russia:</strong> Russia has progressively expanded state control over its economy since 2022 — nationalizing assets of departing Western companies, suspending capital account convertibility, introducing export duties on commodities, and mobilizing defence industry production. The Putin decree extends this into domestic infrastructure governance. Russia's GDP has shown unexpected resilience (growing 3.6% in 2023, 3.1% in 2024) due to high defence spending, but structural vulnerabilities — tight labour markets, sanctions-driven inflation, and technology import restrictions — are increasingly apparent.</li>
<li><strong>India's Investments in Russia:</strong> Several Indian public sector units have investments in Russia: ONGC Videsh holds a 20% stake in the Sakhalin-1 oil field; Indian Oil and OIL India have stakes in Vankorneft. These investments face legal uncertainty under Western sanctions and now under Russia's own expanding state control mechanisms. India's trade with Russia (primarily Indian exports of machinery, pharmaceuticals, and tea vs Russian exports of crude oil, fertilizers, and coal) has grown dramatically since 2022 but faces persistent payment and banking settlement challenges due to SWIFT restrictions.</li>
<li><strong>Frozen Russian Assets and Reparations Debate:</strong> Western countries have frozen approximately $300 billion of Russian central bank assets held in Euroclear and other Western financial systems. A protracted debate over whether to use the interest from these assets (approximately $50 billion) to finance Ukrainian reconstruction was resolved in 2024–25 with the G7 agreeing to provide a $50 billion loan to Ukraine backed by the interest on frozen Russian assets. Russia has threatened legal action and retaliation through asset seizure of Western-linked interests in Russia — the Putin infrastructure decree fits this pattern of escalating economic counter-measures.</li>
</ul>
