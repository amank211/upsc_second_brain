**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Statutory bodies; Supreme Court; legal profession regulation; Bar Council of India

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Supreme Court has directed that all major decisions of the Bar Council of India (BCI) must be made after due consultation with the Attorney General (AG) and the Solicitor General (SG). The order follows a dispute over the appointment of Manan Mishra — a BJP Rajya Sabha member — as BCI's pro-tem chairperson, a move challenged on grounds of conflict of interest and procedural impropriety. The Court has also mandated time-bound elections for the BCI leadership, signalling disquiet over the politicisation of the apex legal regulatory body. The BCI is a statutory body under the Advocates Act 1961 that regulates the legal profession, prescribes standards of professional conduct, and represents advocates across India. The SC's intervention underscores the tension between the independence of the legal profession and partisan appointments to regulatory positions, a recurring governance concern with broader implications for rule of law and institutional integrity.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Bar Council of India (BCI) — Advocates Act 1961:</strong> The BCI is a statutory body constituted under Section 4 of the Advocates Act 1961. It regulates legal education, prescribes standards of professional conduct for advocates, and hears disciplinary proceedings. State Bar Councils are subordinate bodies; BCI is the apex regulatory authority for the legal profession in India.</li>
<li><strong>Attorney General and Solicitor General:</strong> The Attorney General (Article 76) is the chief law officer of the Government of India and has the right of audience in all courts. The Solicitor General is the second law officer, assisting the AG. Their consultation in the SC's order is significant — it underscores the constitutional importance of insulating professional regulation from executive/political interference.</li>
<li><strong>Pro-tem chairperson:</strong> A pro-tem (Latin: for the time being) appointment is a temporary, interim arrangement pending regular elections. In the BCI context, the controversy arose because the appointee held a partisan political position (BJP RS MP), raising questions about whether a sitting legislator can simultaneously chair a statutory professional regulatory body.</li>
<li><strong>Conflict of interest in regulatory appointments:</strong> The UPSC examination has tested the concept of conflicts of interest in the appointment of members to regulatory and quasi-judicial bodies. The principle — that a regulator must be independent of regulated entities and of political authority — is embedded in landmark SC judgments like <em>Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India</em> (NJAC case, 2015).</li>
<li><strong>SC's supervisory jurisdiction over statutory bodies:</strong> Under Article 142 (complete justice) and its writ jurisdiction under Article 32, the Supreme Court can issue directions to statutory bodies to ensure constitutional and statutory compliance. Time-bound election directions are part of this corrective mandate, previously used in cases involving cricket board (BCCI) and cooperative societies.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">UN sets pathway to tackle 'inevitable breach' of 1.5°C global warming limit</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Climate change; global warming; international climate agreements; carbon budgets
- GS Paper II → International Relations → Multilateral institutions; UNEP; COP negotiations; India's climate commitments

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) has released its "Limiting Overshoot" report, establishing a formal pathway for what it calls the "inevitable" breach of the 1.5°C warming threshold set by the Paris Agreement. The report acknowledges that 1.5°C will be temporarily exceeded but argues that aggressive mitigation and carbon dioxide removal (CDR) can bring temperatures back below that threshold — a scenario termed "overshoot." This framing was incorporated into the COP30 decision taken in Belém, Brazil, known as the "Global Mutirão" (Portuguese for collective action), which mandated enhanced Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and timelines for CDR deployment. The report marks a significant shift in international climate diplomacy: from preventing overshoot to managing it. For India — one of the world's largest emitters and a leader of the Global South — the report raises questions about burden-sharing, historical responsibility, and the feasibility of negative-emission technologies for developing nations.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Paris Agreement (2015) and 1.5°C target:</strong> Adopted at COP21 in Paris, the Agreement commits nations to limiting global warming to well below 2°C, preferably 1.5°C, above pre-industrial levels. The 1.5°C target is now recognized as especially critical for small island states and vulnerable regions. India ratified the Agreement in 2016 and has committed to net-zero emissions by 2070.</li>
<li><strong>UNEP and its mandate:</strong> The United Nations Environment Programme, headquartered in Nairobi, Kenya, is the leading global authority on the environment. UNEP publishes the annual Emissions Gap Report and the Global Environmental Outlook. It does not have binding powers but provides authoritative data that shapes COP negotiations and NDC ambition cycles.</li>
<li><strong>Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR):</strong> CDR encompasses a range of technologies and natural processes — afforestation, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), direct air capture (DAC), enhanced weathering — that remove CO₂ from the atmosphere. The IPCC's 1.5°C special report (2018) found that almost all pathways to 1.5°C involve CDR, raising equity concerns as costs are disproportionate for developing nations.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature overshoot scenario:</strong> An overshoot scenario is one where global temperatures exceed 1.5°C temporarily before being brought back down through CDR. The IPCC has studied such scenarios, but critics argue they rely on unproven technologies at scale and create moral hazard by reducing urgency for near-term emission cuts — a key tension in India's negotiating position.</li>
<li><strong>COP30 — Belém, Brazil (2025):</strong> The 30th Conference of Parties under the UNFCCC was held in Belém in the Amazon, symbolically chosen to highlight forest conservation. The "Global Mutirão" decision was the landmark outcome, requiring all parties to submit stronger NDCs with explicit CDR pathways by 2026. It represents the first formal acknowledgment in a COP decision that 1.5°C will be breached.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
In the seventh month of the U.S.-Iran military conflict, Iran escalated attacks on Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) member states — targeting Kuwait, Bahrain, and Iraq — following continued U.S. airstrikes on Iranian nuclear and military sites. A particularly lethal strike hit a wedding venue in Kuhestak, a port town on the Strait of Hormuz, causing mass civilian casualties. The attacks prompted emergency consultations among GCC states and the U.S. Fifth Fleet, which is based in Bahrain. The Strait of Hormuz — through which approximately 21 million barrels of oil pass daily — remains the world's single most critical maritime energy chokepoint. India's energy security is directly implicated: approximately 50% of India's crude imports originate from Gulf states, and any prolonged blockade of the Strait would trigger global crude price spikes and supply disruptions. India has been walking a diplomatic tightrope, maintaining ties with both Iran and the GCC states, and has more than 8 million Indian diaspora workers in Gulf countries.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Strait of Hormuz — strategic significance:</strong> The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman. It is the world's most important oil transit chokepoint — roughly 20–21% of global petroleum liquids and 25% of global LNG pass through it. Any closure would force much longer alternative routes (around the Arabian Peninsula), dramatically increasing shipping costs and delivery times.</li>
<li><strong>Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC):</strong> The GCC is a political and economic alliance of six Arab states of the Arabian Peninsula: Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman. Established in 1981, it was partly motivated by the Iran-Iraq War. The U.S. maintains significant military presence across GCC states — CENTCOM's air operations hub is in Qatar, and the Fifth Fleet is in Bahrain.</li>
<li><strong>India-Iran relations and sanctions:</strong> India has historically maintained ties with Iran despite U.S. pressure. India was among the top importers of Iranian oil before 2019, when it halted imports under threat of U.S. secondary sanctions (CAATSA). India has used the Chabahar Port agreement (exempted from sanctions) as a strategic interest. The current conflict creates pressure on India to choose sides explicitly.</li>
<li><strong>India's energy import dependence:</strong> India imports approximately 85% of its crude oil requirement. The Gulf (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iraq, Kuwait) and Russia are the dominant suppliers. Any sustained disruption in Gulf supplies raises issues of strategic petroleum reserves (India has SPR capacity at Vishakhapatnam, Padur, and Mangaluru — total ~5.33 million metric tonnes, covering ~9.5 days of consumption).</li>
<li><strong>Indian diaspora in Gulf:</strong> Over 8.5 million Indians live and work in Gulf countries, making the GCC the largest destination for the Indian diaspora. Remittances from the Gulf account for a substantial share of India's total inward remittances (~$107 billion in FY24). A regional conflict triggers evacuation concerns (Operation Vande Bharat precedent in Yemen/COVID) and remittance disruption risks.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Allahabad HC quashes law student's NSA detention, awards ₹5 lakh compensation</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Labour</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Fundamental rights; preventive detention; National Security Act; judicial review; High Courts

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Allahabad High Court has quashed the National Security Act (NSA) detention of Akriti Chaudhary, a law student who was detained after participating in a workers' protest in Noida. The court held that the grounds for detention were a "concocted story" and that invoking the NSA — a preventive detention law meant for threats to national security — against a student protesting labour conditions was a gross abuse of executive power. The HC awarded ₹5 lakh compensation to Chaudhary, holding the state responsible for the illegal detention. The case illustrates the misuse of preventive detention laws to suppress civil society activism, a pattern that courts have repeatedly checked. NSA allows detention without trial for up to 12 months, making judicial review through habeas corpus — the constitutional remedy under Article 226 — the only check on such detentions. The judgment underscores the importance of the HC's supervisory role over executive overreach under preventive detention statutes.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Security Act 1980 (NSA):</strong> A central preventive detention law enacted in 1980, the NSA allows administrative detention of a person for up to 12 months without trial if the authority is satisfied that detaining the person is necessary to prevent them from acting in a manner prejudicial to national security, public order, or maintenance of essential services. Grounds must be communicated to the detenu but can be withheld if disclosure is against public interest.</li>
<li><strong>Preventive detention and Article 22:</strong> Article 22 of the Constitution permits preventive detention subject to safeguards: the detenu must be informed of grounds, must be allowed to make a representation, and must be produced before an Advisory Board within five weeks. Despite these safeguards, courts have consistently found procedural shortcuts in NSA detentions. The 44th Amendment (1978) removed the right of preventive detainees to legal counsel.</li>
<li><strong>Habeas corpus — Article 226:</strong> Habeas corpus (literally "produce the body") is the most important writ for personal liberty. High Courts can issue it under Article 226; the Supreme Court under Article 32. It requires the detaining authority to justify the detention before the court. In preventive detention cases, courts examine whether (a) the grounds are relevant, (b) the nexus between the ground and the detention is rational, and (c) the procedure was followed.</li>
<li><strong>Compensation for illegal detention:</strong> The Supreme Court in Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar (1983) first held that courts can award compensation for illegal deprivation of personal liberty under Article 21. Subsequent judgments (Bhim Singh v. State of J&K, DK Basu v. State of West Bengal) have expanded this to cases of wrongful detention and custodial violations, establishing state liability in public law.</li>
<li><strong>Workers' protests and fundamental rights:</strong> The right to peaceful protest is an implicit facet of Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech and expression) and Article 19(1)(b) (right to assemble peaceably and without arms). Courts have held that using preventive detention laws against peaceful protesters is unconstitutional unless there is a direct and proximate threat to public order — a higher threshold than mere "law and order" disturbance.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Inside Delhi prisons: gangs, collusion and proposed transfers</h2>

<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Prison reforms; administration of criminal justice; institutional accountability
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Organised crime; gangster networks; internal security challenges

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
An investigative report reveals how Delhi's prisons have become operational hubs for gang networks run by Neeraj Bawana, Hashim Baba, and the Anmol Bishnoi gang. Despite incarceration, gang leaders continue to coordinate extortion, contract killings, and drug distribution using mobile phones smuggled into jails. Jammer technology meant to block signals has significant coverage gaps, and officials point to collusion between prison staff and gang operatives. In response, authorities are proposing the Transfer of Prisoners (Punjab Amendment) Act 2025 as a template to enable inter-state transfers of high-risk prisoners to break up networks. The phenomenon — termed "crime from prison" — exposes systemic failures in prison administration: overcrowding (Delhi prisons at ~175% capacity), understaffing, inadequate surveillance, and corruption. Prison reform has been recommended by multiple committees (Mulla Committee 1983, Justice Krishna Iyer reports) but implementation remains weak. Prisons being a State List subject (Schedule VII, List II) further fragments oversight.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Prison administration — constitutional and legislative framework:</strong> "Prisons" is Entry 4 of the State List (List II, Schedule VII), making prison administration a state subject. The primary central legislation is the Prisons Act 1894 — still operative — which is widely considered archaic. The Model Prison Manual 2016 (MHA) provides updated guidelines but lacks statutory force. The BPR&D (Bureau of Police Research and Development) publishes annual prison statistics (India Prison Statistics Report).</li>
<li><strong>Overcrowding and undertrial crisis:</strong> As per NCRB Prison Statistics India 2022, India's prisons have an occupancy rate of ~131% nationally. Undertrial prisoners constitute approximately 76% of the total prison population — a structural indicator of delays in the criminal justice system. Delhi's Tihar Jail complex is among the most overcrowded in the country.</li>
<li><strong>Anmol Bishnoi and transnational gangster networks:</strong> Anmol Bishnoi is the brother of Lawrence Bishnoi, currently in Tihar Jail. Anmol, believed to be operating from Canada, has been designated by the U.S. as a global terrorist. The Bishnoi gang has claimed responsibility for several high-profile targeted killings including NCP leader Baba Siddiqui (2024). It exemplifies the nexus between organised crime, diaspora funding, and domestic violence.</li>
<li><strong>Prisoner transfer laws:</strong> The Transfer of Prisoners Act 1950 (central) allows transfer of convicted prisoners between states with state consent. The proposed Punjab amendment model would streamline this for high-security undertrials. The Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India (2022) also touched on the need for robust mechanisms to prevent crime from prison.</li>
<li><strong>Mulla Committee recommendations (1983):</strong> The All India Committee on Jail Reforms (1983), chaired by Justice A.N. Mulla, made 658 recommendations covering prison conditions, undertrial release, prisoner rights, and staff reform. Most remain unimplemented. The recommendations form a standard reference in UPSC essays and mains answers on criminal justice reform in India.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Agniveer involvement ruled out in military weapon theft from Secunderabad cantonment</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Defence</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Defence procurement; weapon security; military discipline; Agniveer scheme
- GS Paper II → Governance → Military accountability; security forces

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Military Intelligence has ruled out Agniveer involvement in the theft of AK-203 assault rifles, INSAS rifles, and 9mm pistols from the armoury of 20 Madras Regiment at Secunderabad Cantonment. Initial suspicion had fallen on recently recruited Agniveers given the scheme's short tenure and reduced bonding incentives, but investigators have cleared them. The theft — which involved multiple service weapons — points instead to lapses in armoury management and insider collusion from permanent cadre personnel. The Agnipath scheme, launched in June 2022, recruits soldiers for a four-year short-service tenure (Agniveers), with 25% retained as permanent cadre thereafter. Critics of the scheme had warned that lower pay, shorter tenure, and reduced retirement benefits could reduce institutional loyalty; proponents argue it lowers the pension burden and injects young talent. The incident will likely renew scrutiny of armoury security protocols across cantonments, especially as the AK-203 is India's newest frontline assault rifle produced under an India-Russia joint venture at Korwa Ordnance Factory, UP.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Agnipath Scheme (June 2022):</strong> Launched by the Ministry of Defence in June 2022, Agnipath recruits youth aged 17.5–23 years into the Army, Navy, and Air Force for four years. Recruits (called Agniveers) receive a monthly salary of ₹30,000–40,000 and a Seva Nidhi corpus of ~₹11.7 lakh on exit. 25% of each batch are retained in regular service. The scheme aims to reduce the armed forces' pension burden, which consumes ~25% of the defence budget.</li>
<li><strong>AK-203 — India-Russia joint production:</strong> The AK-203 is a 7.62mm assault rifle developed by Kalashnikov Concern (Russia). India and Russia signed an agreement in 2021 to produce 6.01 lakh AK-203 rifles at the Indo-Russian Rifles Private Limited (IRRPL) facility at Korwa Ordnance Factory, Amethi, Uttar Pradesh. This is India's largest small-arms procurement deal and a flagship "Make in India" defence project. The AK-203 is being inducted to replace the INSAS rifle.</li>
<li><strong>INSAS rifle:</strong> The Indian Small Arms System (INSAS) is a 5.56×45mm assault rifle developed by the Ordnance Factories Board (now Advanced Weapons and Equipment India Limited). Though it entered service in 1998, it has been criticized for reliability issues in extreme climates (documented during Kargil 1999 and subsequent deployments). The AK-203 is its successor for the Indian Army.</li>
<li><strong>Armoury security and military law:</strong> Weapon theft from military establishments is prosecuted under the Army Act 1950 and the Official Secrets Act 1923. The Army Act provides for court martials and is the primary disciplinary code for army personnel. Armouries are subject to periodic inspections by regimental officers and the Inspector General of Ordnance; lapses are treated as serious breaches of military discipline.</li>
<li><strong>Military Intelligence (MI):</strong> Military Intelligence is a branch of the Indian Army's general staff responsible for battlefield intelligence and counter-intelligence within defence establishments. It operates under the Directorate General of Military Intelligence (DGMI). MI investigations into internal security lapses (weapon theft, espionage, insider threats) are distinct from civilian police investigations and are not subject to civilian oversight under the CrPC.</li>
</ul>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Centre-state relations; marine fisheries regulation; EEZ management
- GS Paper III → Agriculture → Fisheries; marine resources; coastal livelihood

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Supreme Court has directed the Tamil Nadu government to frame rules under the Marine Fishing Regulation Rules 2020 to allow purse seine fishing vessels to transit through coastal waters to reach the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) for licensed deep-sea fishing. The dispute arose because purse seine nets — large encircling nets banned in near-shore coastal waters to protect artisanal fisherfolk — are permitted in the EEZ under central EEZ Rules 2025. However, vessels cannot reach the EEZ without passing through the 12-nautical-mile territorial sea (state jurisdiction). The Court's direction attempts to reconcile state regulatory power over coastal fishing with central jurisdiction over the EEZ, which falls under the Maritime Zones of India Act 1976. The case is significant for cooperative federalism in maritime governance, where overlapping state and central jurisdictions over different maritime zones create regulatory friction that affects the livelihoods of both large vessel operators and small-scale fisherfolk.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Maritime zones and jurisdictional framework:</strong> Under the Maritime Zones of India (MZI) Act 1976 (UNCLOS-aligned), India claims: Territorial Sea (12 nautical miles from baseline — state jurisdiction over fishing), Contiguous Zone (24 nm), Exclusive Economic Zone (200 nm — central jurisdiction, sovereign rights over resources), and Continental Shelf. Fisheries in the EEZ are regulated by central law; coastal fisheries are a state subject (Entry 21, List II — fisheries not beyond territorial waters).</li>
<li><strong>Purse seine fishing:</strong> Purse seine nets are large nets deployed to surround entire schools of fish. While efficient, they are associated with overfishing, bycatch of non-target species (including dolphins and juveniles), and displacement of artisanal fisherfolk who use traditional gear. Most coastal states (TN, Kerala, Goa) ban purse seines in the inshore zone to protect small-scale fishers. They are permitted in the EEZ for licensed deep-sea operators.</li>
<li><strong>Marine Fisheries (Regulation and Management) Act 2023 (central):</strong> Parliament enacted a central marine fisheries law in 2023 for the first time, covering the EEZ. It regulates licensing, conservation measures, and illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing. Prior to this, only the EEZ (Regulation of Fishing) Act 1981 governed EEZ fishing, but without a comprehensive management framework.</li>
<li><strong>Blue economy and PM Matsya Sampada Yojana:</strong> The central government's flagship fisheries scheme, PM Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY, 2020), allocates ₹20,050 crore to boost fish production and deep-sea fishing infrastructure. India is the world's third-largest fish producer. Deep-sea fishing expansion is a key plank of India's blue economy strategy, which aims to double the contribution of ocean-based industries to GDP by 2030.</li>
<li><strong>Artisanal vs. industrial fisherfolk conflict:</strong> A recurring tension in India's fisheries governance is between mechanised/industrial fishing boats (often corporate-owned) and traditional artisanal fisherfolk. The former have greater lobbying power and access to policy; the latter represent about 4 million active fishers dependent on near-shore resources. SC judgments have repeatedly emphasised the need for coastal regulation rules that protect the livelihoods of traditional fishing communities.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A study has valued the Pichavaram mangrove forest in Tamil Nadu — one of the largest mangrove ecosystems in India — at ₹2,485 crore using the Total Economic Value (TEV) framework. The TEV methodology captures not just direct use values (fish, timber) but also indirect use values (coastal protection, carbon sequestration as "blue carbon"), option values (future uses), and non-use or existence values (biodiversity conservation). The study recommends a Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES) mechanism to compensate local communities and fund conservation, recognising that mangroves are among the most carbon-dense ecosystems on Earth — sequestering carbon 3–5 times more efficiently per unit area than tropical forests. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Total Economic Value (TEV) framework:</strong> TEV is an economic framework that assigns monetary values to all components of a natural ecosystem: direct use values (provisioning services — fisheries, NTFPs), indirect use values (regulating and supporting services — storm protection, carbon sequestration, water purification), option values (insurance value for future use), quasi-option values (preserving options under uncertainty), and non-use/existence values (intrinsic value of biodiversity). TEV exercises translate ecological data into a language policymakers and planners can use for cost-benefit analysis.</li>
<li><strong>Blue carbon:</strong> Blue carbon refers to carbon captured and stored by ocean and coastal ecosystems — mangroves, salt marshes, seagrasses — collectively termed "blue carbon ecosystems." They sequester carbon in biomass and especially in sediment, where organic carbon can remain stored for millennia. Mangroves store 3–5 times more carbon per unit area than tropical forests, making their conservation a highly cost-effective climate mitigation strategy. India's mangrove cover (as per FSI 2023) is ~4,992 sq km.</li>
<li><strong>Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES):</strong> PES is a market-based conservation instrument where beneficiaries of ecosystem services (e.g. fisheries downstream, cities protected from floods) compensate providers (e.g. forest communities, mangrove conservation bodies) for maintaining those services. India lacks a national PES framework but the Environment Ministry has been piloting schemes; states like Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand have experimented with watershed-level PES arrangements.</li>
<li><strong>MISHTI scheme (2023):</strong> The Mangrove Initiative for Shoreline Habitats & Tangible Incomes (MISHTI) was announced in Union Budget 2023-24. It seeks to expand mangrove cover along India's coastline through a convergence of MGNREGS and CAMPA (Compensatory Afforestation Management and Planning Authority) funds, targeting 9 coastal states and island territories. The scheme is implemented by MoEFCC in collaboration with state forest departments.</li>
<li><strong>Pichavaram — ecological significance:</strong> Pichavaram, located near Chidambaram, Tamil Nadu, is the second-largest mangrove forest in India after the Sundarbans. It spans ~1,100 hectares and supports diverse avifauna, fish nurseries, and a rich invertebrate community. The forest provided natural protection against the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, demonstrating the coastal buffering function of healthy mangrove ecosystems — a key argument for their economic valuation.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Tenth Schedule — Anti-Defection Law (52nd Amendment, 1985):</strong> Disqualifies a legislator who voluntarily gives up party membership or votes contrary to party direction. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Electoral roll revision — SIR vs. Summary Revision:</strong> Electoral rolls are maintained under the Representation of People's Act 1950 and Registration of Electors Rules 1960. Annual Summary Revision updates the roll with notice periods. Special Intensive Revision (SIR) — comprehensive door-to-door enumeration before major elections — rewrites the roll from scratch and can produce large-scale deletions of stale entries. <li><strong>Form 7 — deletion application:</strong> Form 7 allows any elector to object to another's inclusion (grounds: death, shift, non-residence). Bulk Form 7 filings — by Booth Level Agents (BLAs) of political parties or Electoral Registration Officers — can trigger mass deletions without individual notice. Opposition parties allege BLA access is misused to file bulk objections against rival communities' registrations, a practice difficult to detect or prevent without systematic audit.</li>
<li><strong>EC's constitutional status and independence:</strong> Article 324 gives the EC complete superintendence over elections. The CEC's security of tenure was modified by the Election Laws Amendment Act 2023, replacing the collegium committee (which included the CJI) with one including the PM and a Cabinet minister. The SC in Anoop Baranwal v. UoI (2023) had directed an independent committee — the 2023 Amendment partially reversed this, raising independence concerns.</li>
<li><strong>Right to vote — constitutional basis:</strong> Article 326 provides adult suffrage for every citizen above 18. The right to vote is a statutory right (not a fundamental right per se in traditional doctrine), but SC has increasingly applied Article 21 and Article 14 to arbitrary denial of voter registration. Arbitrary deletion without notice may violate natural justice principles applicable to quasi-judicial electoral registration acts.</li>
<li><strong>Maharashtra electoral roll controversy (2024):</strong> Pre-Maharashtra election (November 2024) controversies about voter list manipulation were major political issues. MVA (Maha Vikas Aghadi) alleged systematic deletions from minority voter rolls. Sibal's SC petition seeks post-election accountability and prospective remedies: mandatory individualised notice before deletion, independent audit of SIR deletions, and stricter Form 7 scrutiny — addressing structural issues for future electoral integrity.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
British scientists have developed ZPY-CpG-Ch, a promising candidate for a universal vaccine against Streptococcus pneumoniae, the bacterium responsible for pneumococcal pneumonia and bacterial meningitis. Unlike existing vaccines (PCV-13, PCV-15, PCV-20) that target specific serotypes, a universal vaccine would protect against all approximately 100 serotypes. The candidate uses reverse vaccinology — computational identification of conserved bacterial proteins from the pathogen's genome — rather than traditional serotype-specific antigen approaches. Pneumococcal disease kills over 300,000 children under five annually worldwide; India carries a disproportionate share. Universal protection would be a landmark public health achievement for low- and middle-income countries where serotype-specific vaccines provide incomplete coverage due to serotype replacement. India introduced PCV into its Universal Immunisation Programme (UIP) in 2017 and manufactures the vaccine domestically through the Serum Institute of India.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Streptococcus pneumoniae:</strong> S. pneumoniae (pneumococcus) causes pneumonia, bacterial meningitis, septicaemia, and acute otitis media. It is the leading cause of vaccine-preventable deaths in children under five globally. India has one of the world's highest pneumococcal disease burdens, concentrated in high-fertility, high-under-five-mortality states (UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, MP). Bacterial pneumonia is also a leading cause of AMR-related deaths, making vaccination an AMR-prevention strategy.</li>
<li><strong>Existing PCVs and serotype replacement:</strong> Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines conjugate polysaccharide antigens from specific serotypes to a protein carrier. PCV-13 covers 13 serotypes; PCV-20 covers 20. However, approximately 100 serotypes exist. Non-vaccine serotypes can fill the niche vacated by vaccinated serotypes (serotype replacement), limiting long-term protection. A universal vaccine targeting conserved proteins eliminates this limitation.</li>
<li><strong>Reverse vaccinology:</strong> Pioneered by Rino Rappuoli in developing the Bexsero meningococcal B vaccine, reverse vaccinology uses computational genome analysis to identify conserved bacterial proteins as targets — inverse of traditional pathogen-grow-and-inactivate approaches. It is especially valuable for highly variable pathogens where antigen diversity defeats conventional methods. The approach has accelerated vaccine development timelines significantly.</li>
<li><strong>Universal Immunisation Programme (UIP):</strong> India's UIP provides free vaccines against 13 diseases. PCV was introduced in 2017, initially in select states, and progressively expanded nationwide. India's domestic PCV production through Serum Institute of India is a strategic public health capability. The UIP covers approximately 26 million newborns annually and is among the world's largest immunisation programmes by absolute numbers.</li>
<li><strong>One Health and AMR:</strong> Reducing bacterial pneumonia burden is also an antimicrobial resistance (AMR) strategy. S. pneumoniae has developed resistance to penicillin, macrolides, and some cephalosporins. Vaccination reduces infection frequency and therefore antibiotic use, reducing selection pressure for resistant strains. India's National Action Plan on AMR (2017) identifies vaccination as a key pillar alongside antibiotic stewardship.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Japan's record bear attacks — driven by climate change-induced disruption of mountain forest food availability, rural depopulation, and shrinking human-wildlife buffer zones — have provoked debate about culling as the primary management response. A Nagano coexistence programme (established 1995) using Karelian Bear Dogs to aversively condition bears away from human settlements offers a non-lethal evidence-based alternative. The case has direct relevance for India's human-wildlife conflict crisis: elephant attacks kill 400-500 people annually; leopards, tigers, and bears also account for significant casualties across forest-fringe communities.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Human-wildlife conflict (HWC) in India:</strong> India reports among the world's highest HWC casualties. Elephant attacks kill approximately 400-500 people annually; tiger and leopard attacks add hundreds more. MoEFCC manages HWC through Project Elephant (1992) and Project Tiger (1973). HWC is concentrated in forest-agriculture boundary zones — expanding due to agricultural encroachment, wildlife corridor fragmentation, and climate-driven range shifts. Ex-gratia compensation is the primary state-level tool, often inadequate and delayed.</li>
<li><strong>Wildlife Protection Act 1972 — Schedule I and culling:</strong> Schedule I species (elephant, tiger, leopard, gaur) receive the highest legal protection. Culling requires declaring an animal "vermin" under Section 62 of the WPA — a cumbersome process requiring state-Centre coordination. Translocation is the default alternative but has high failure rates for large mammals. Aversive conditioning as used in Japan offers a humane, ecologically sound alternative that keeps the animal in its habitat.</li>
<li><strong>Climate change and wildlife behaviour:</strong> IPCC AR6 documents increased human-wildlife conflict as a climate impact in Asia. Climate-driven phenological shifts alter forest food availability — poor mast years in forests drive bears (Japan) and elephants (India) into agricultural areas. In India, erratic monsoon affects Western Ghats forest produce, driving elephant raids. This human-wildlife conflict-climate nexus is a growing research and policy area.</li>
<li><strong>Aversive conditioning:</strong> Aversive conditioning uses negative stimuli (trained dogs, rubber bullets, firecrackers, capsaicin sprays) to condition wild animals to avoid human settlements. Karelian Bear Dogs from Finland are used in Japan and U.S. bear aversion programmes. The method requires consistent community participation and repeated conditioning. Success is measured by reduced bear incursions without lethal control — maintaining the animal in its ecosystem unlike culling or translocation.</li>
<li><strong>Elephant corridors in India:</strong> Project Elephant identified 88 elephant reserves; WTI and WWF-India have mapped approximately 101 elephant corridors, 20% critically disrupted. Legal protection through Protected Area status and eco-sensitive zone notifications is patchy. The Centrally Sponsored Scheme on Project Elephant funds states for HWC compensation, fencing, and early warning systems (SMS alerts, community monitoring). India could adopt Japan's community-based aversive conditioning model in elephant-conflict zones.</li>
</ul>

The Hindu's second editorial analyses the structural failure behind India's recurring onion price crises, arguing that successive governments have managed onion prices reactively — with export bans, buffer releases, and import orders — rather than addressing root causes: inadequate cold storage, poor price discovery mechanisms, and the absence of a stable procurement framework for farmers. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Cold chain infrastructure deficit:</strong> India loses approximately 15-30% of fruit and vegetable produce post-harvest due to inadequate cold chain infrastructure — storage, processing, and transport. The National Centre for Cold Chain Development (NCCD) estimates cold chain infrastructure gap at 37 million metric tonnes of storage capacity and 62,000 refrigerated transport vehicles. MSP for onions is complicated by its three-season harvesting cycle, highly perishable nature, and the enormous regional price variation. CACP has recommended a price stabilisation endowment for horticultural crops.</li>
<li><strong>Reactive vs. proactive governance in agriculture:</strong> The pattern of reactive crisis management in Indian agricultural policy — acting after prices spike or crash rather than building structural capacity — reflects political economy constraints. Proactive measures (cold chain investment, price stabilisation funds) require upfront fiscal commitment and have diffuse, delayed political benefits. <div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

Tribunals (Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Pennaiyar, Ravi-Beas, Vansadhara) have faced long delays — some spanning decades. <h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Constitutional faultlines in the FCRA Amendment Bill 2026</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 10 · Text and Context</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II -> Indian Polity -> Fundamental rights; freedom of association; civil society; FCRA; proportionality doctrine

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The FCRA (Foreign Contribution Regulation Act) Amendment Bill 2026 introduces a "Designated Authority" (DA) — a senior MHA official — empowered to provisionally vest, take possession of, and manage assets of organisations whose FCRA registration is suspended or cancelled on grounds of national security or public interest. Critics identify several constitutional faultlines: (a) the DA mechanism bypasses judicial oversight — provisional vesting is an executive act without mandatory prior court scrutiny; (b) "public interest" is undefined, creating scope for arbitrary use; (c) the proportionality doctrine (whether the restriction is proportionate to the threat) — increasingly applied by Indian courts following the Modern Dental College judgment and the internet shutdown cases — is not built into the Bill's framework; and (d) the right to manage one's own affairs under Article 19(1)(c) (freedom of association) is potentially violated without adequate procedural safeguards. The Bill also tightens sub-grant restrictions, already severely limited by the 2020 amendment.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Foreign Contribution Regulation Act (FCRA) 1976 and 2020 Amendment:</strong> FCRA regulates the receipt and use of foreign contributions by associations, academics, and individuals. The 2020 amendment (which the SC upheld in Noel Harper v. UoI, 2022) tightened: mandatory SBI FCRA account, prohibition on sub-granting foreign funds to other NGOs, enhanced Aadhaar-based KYC, and increased MHA oversight powers. FCRA cancellations have affected thousands of civil society organisations including Amnesty International India, Greenpeace India, and multiple religious organisations.</li>
<li><strong>Article 19(1)(c) and freedom of association:</strong> Article 19(1)(c) guarantees the right to form associations or unions. This right is subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(4) on grounds of sovereignty, public order, or morality. Courts have held that associations formed for lawful purposes cannot be dissolved without fair procedure and judicial oversight. The FCRA amendment's DA mechanism, allowing asset vesting without court involvement, raises questions about its consistency with Article 19(1)(c) protections.</li>
<li><strong>Proportionality doctrine in Indian constitutional law:</strong> The proportionality doctrine requires that restrictions on fundamental rights must be: (a) in pursuance of a legitimate aim, (b) suitable to achieve that aim, (c) necessary (least restrictive means), and (d) not disproportionate in impact on the right-holder's dignity. The SC first applied proportionality explicitly in Modern Dental College v. State of MP (2016) and expanded it in internet shutdown cases (Anuradha Bhasin v. UoI, 2020). The FCRA Bill's critics argue the DA mechanism fails the necessity and proportionality tests.</li>
<li><strong>Civil society and national security — global context:</strong> The use of foreign funding regulation to constrain civil society is a documented global trend (Russia's "foreign agent" law, Hungary's NGO law, Israel's transparency law). The UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights to Freedom of Peaceful Assembly and of Association has flagged India's FCRA as one of several laws globally that excessively restrict civic space. The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights also identify civil society organisations as key actors whose independence states must protect.</li>
<li><strong>Noel Harper v. UoI (2022) — SC on FCRA 2020:</strong> The SC upheld the FCRA 2020 amendments, holding that regulation of foreign funds does not violate fundamental rights since accepting foreign contributions is not a fundamental right. The court applied a deferential standard of review on national security grounds. Critics argued the ruling gave the executive excessive deference; the 2026 Bill's DA mechanism goes further than the 2020 amendments in executive self-authorisation — raising whether the deferential standard from Harper can be extended to cover asset seizure without judicial scrutiny.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Can a 'distinguished jurist' be appointed to the Supreme Court?</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Judiciary</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 11 · Text and Context</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II -> Indian Polity -> Supreme Court; judicial appointments; Collegium system; Article 124(3)

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Article 124(3)(c) of the Constitution provides that a "distinguished jurist" — in addition to High Court judges and advocates with ten years' standing — can be appointed as a Supreme Court judge. The provision has never been used in 76 years of the Republic. This Text and Context piece examines why: the Constituent Assembly debate (Felix Frankfurter was the model — a constitutional law professor appointed to the U.S. Supreme Court); BCI Rule 49 bars full-time law teachers from appearing in courts, making academic jurists ineligible for the "advocate" category (requiring 10 years' standing); and the Collegium's process — driven by HC seniority lists — creates structural barriers against non-judicial nominees. With debates about bringing external expertise into constitutional adjudication and improving SC's institutional diversity, the dormant "distinguished jurist" clause could gain renewed attention — especially for appointments in specialist areas (competition law, environmental law, international law).
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 124(3) — SC judge eligibility:</strong> Article 124(3) provides three routes to SC appointment: (a) judge of a HC for at least 5 years; (b) advocate of a HC for at least 10 years; or (c) distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President. Route (c) has never been used. The President acts on advice of the Collegium (established by SC in Second and Third Judges Cases, 1993 and 1998) for SC appointments.</li>
<li><strong>Felix Frankfurter as model:</strong> Felix Frankfurter (1882-1965) was a Harvard Law School professor appointed to the U.S. Supreme Court by FDR in 1939, where he served until 1962. He had no prior judicial experience but was a leading constitutional scholar and Roosevelt advisor. Constituent Assembly members cited his model when debating the "distinguished jurist" clause — imagining appointments of eminent academics, practitioners, or legal scholars whose non-judicial career made them ineligible as advocates but qualified as jurisprudential experts.</li>
<li><strong>BCI Rule 49 — bar on full-time law teachers:</strong> Bar Council of India Rule 49 under the Advocates Act 1961 prohibits advocates from practicing if they are engaged in any other full-time service or employment — including as full-time law teachers. This effectively prevents law professors from maintaining an "advocate" standing of 10 years. Combined with the Collegium's practice of never using the "distinguished jurist" category, academic legal scholars are entirely excluded from SC appointment, limiting the bench's exposure to academic constitutional scholarship.</li>
<li><strong>Collegium system — constitutional basis:</strong> The Collegium system — where the CJI and 4 senior-most SC judges recommend appointments — was established through the Second Judges Case (SC Advocates-on-Record Association v. UoI, 1993) and the Third Judges Case (Presidential Reference, 1998). The NJAC Act 2014, which sought to replace the Collegium, was struck down in 2015. The Collegium's process is opaque and driven primarily by seniority in HCs — making non-judicial "distinguished jurist" appointments structurally unlikely without deliberate policy change.</li>
<li><strong>Comparative context — academic judges:</strong> Multiple apex courts appoint academic jurists: Germany's Federal Constitutional Court includes professors by convention; Canada's Supreme Court Act allows practitioners and academics; France's Conseil Constitutionnel has no professional requirement. The consistent experience is that academic judges bring specialist expertise and long-term doctrinal consistency. India's SC has grown in caseload and constitutional complexity — specialist chambers (for economic regulation, environment, criminal law) with jurist appointments could improve quality of adjudication in technical domains.</li>
</ul>
