  ISRO launches advanced Earth observation satellite EOS-05 aboard GSLV-F17

**UPSC Mains Classification**

- GS Paper III → Infrastructure → Strategic space infrastructure

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
ISRO successfully launched Earth Observation Satellite-05 (EOS-05) aboard GSLV-F17 on September 4, 2026, at 2:55 AM IST from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre, Sriharikota. The 19th flight of the Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle placed the 2,367 kg satellite in a Sub-Geostationary Transfer Orbit. EOS-05 is India's first geosynchronous imaging satellite — designed to stare continuously at a fixed region, providing round-the-clock surveillance, disaster monitoring, and weather forecasting that periodic Sun-synchronous orbit satellites cannot match. ISRO Chairman V. Narayanan hailed the launch as a milestone. The success follows two significant setbacks — PSLV-C61 (May 2025) and PSLV-C62 (January 2026) — restoring confidence in India's heavy-lift capability. EOS-05 will augment India's remote sensing constellation for border surveillance, agricultural monitoring, and disaster response, with its persistent staring orbit of particular strategic value for continuous Pakistan and China border monitoring.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>GSLV (Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle):</strong> India's medium-lift rocket capable of placing ~2,500 kg in Geostationary Transfer Orbit, using an indigenously developed cryogenic upper stage (CE-7.5 engine) after technology denial by the US in the 1990s. GSLV-F17 was the 19th flight; early failures drove development of the more powerful LVM3 (formerly GSLV Mk III) for heavier payloads.</li>
<li><strong>Geosynchronous vs. Sun-Synchronous Orbit:</strong> Geosynchronous satellites orbit at ~35,786 km matching Earth's rotation, hovering over a fixed area for persistent surveillance. Sun-Synchronous Orbit (SSO) satellites at ~600 km revisit the same area every few days — suited for systematic mapping. EOS-05 fills a strategic gap not addressed by India's existing Cartosat, RISAT, and HySIS satellites in SSO.</li>
<li><strong>Sub-GTO and Satellite Propulsion:</strong> Sub-Geostationary Transfer Orbit is an intermediate elliptical orbit below the standard GTO apogee. The satellite raises itself to final geosynchronous orbit using its onboard apogee motor. GSLV's lower capacity compared to LVM3 necessitated this approach to keep payload mass within the launcher's limits.</li>
<li><strong>EOS Series and National Remote Sensing Centre:</strong> ISRO renamed its earth observation constellation to EOS in 2020 for uniformity. NRSC under ISRO operates the constellation serving agriculture, forestry, water resources, disaster management, and urban planning under the National Geospatial Policy 2022. Data sharing follows the Remote Sensing Data Policy (RSDP) 2011.</li>
<li><strong>Defence Space Agency (DSA) and Dual-Use Doctrine:</strong> DSA was established in 2019 under the Integrated Defence Staff to coordinate tri-service space operations. India's civilian EOS satellites serve dual civilian-strategic roles; EOS-05's geosynchronous staring capability is of particular intelligence value for real-time border surveillance — a strategic shift from India's earlier purely periodic remote sensing architecture.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Two workers trapped in a tunnel collapse in Nepal were rescued alive after nine days, in a multi-agency operation that involved Indian NDRF teams alongside Nepalese rescue forces. The incident occurred during infrastructure construction in Nepal's seismically active mountainous terrain. The rescue drew comparisons with India's own Silkyara-Barkot tunnel collapse (Uttarakhand, November 2023), where 41 workers were trapped for 17 days. India's NDRF has a strong track record of cross-border humanitarian deployments — the 2015 Nepal earthquake rescue being the most prominent — exemplifying the 'Neighbourhood First' foreign policy principle of disaster diplomacy. The incident spotlights the need for mandatory geological surveys, tunnel safety standards, and rapid-response frameworks under the SAARC Disaster Management Centre, which has been largely stalled since 2016 due to India-Pakistan tensions.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Disaster Response Force (NDRF):</strong> Established under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, NDRF has 16 battalions drawn from CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP, SSB, and Assam Rifles. It has deployed internationally in Nepal (2015 earthquake), Sri Lanka (2017 floods), and Bangladesh — making disaster diplomacy a key soft power instrument under India's neighbourhood policy.</li>
<li><strong>Silkyara Tunnel Collapse (2023) — Key Precedent:</strong> In November 2023, 41 workers were trapped for 17 days in the Silkyara-Barkot tunnel (Uttarakhand) under the Char Dham project. Rescue involved American auger drilling through debris. The incident triggered NHIDCL safety audits and informed South Asian rescue protocol design for subsequent tunnel emergencies.</li>
<li><strong>SAARC Disaster Management Centre (SDMC):</strong> Established in New Delhi in 2006, SDMC provides policy advice and capacity-building for disaster risk reduction across South Asia. Stalled since the 2016 Islamabad Summit cancellation due to India-Pakistan tensions, bilateral channels (NDRF deployments) have effectively replaced formal SAARC mechanisms in regional disaster cooperation.</li>
<li><strong>Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030):</strong> The UN global blueprint adopted at Sendai, Japan, sets four priorities: understanding disaster risk, strengthening governance, investing in resilience, and enhancing preparedness. India's National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP, revised 2019) is aligned with Sendai. Infrastructure resilience — including tunnel safety protocols — is a core component.</li>
<li><strong>Nepal's Geological Vulnerability:</strong> Nepal straddles the Main Central Thrust (MCT) and Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) — active fault systems in the Himalayan collision zone. Its seismic hazard rating (Zone IV-V) makes tunnel construction particularly risky. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake (magnitude 7.8, 9,000 deaths) demonstrated the scale of Nepal's exposure; inadequate geological surveys before construction remain the leading preventable risk factor.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Delhi Police added fresh charges to an existing FIR against activists associated with Citizens for Justice and Peace (CJP), a human rights NGO known for legal aid to riot victims and CAA-NRC resistance, following a protest organised by the organisation. CJP, co-founded by Teesta Setalvad, has been under sustained scrutiny — Setalvad was arrested in 2022 on charges of fabricating evidence in the post-Godhra riot cases, a case that drew criticism from civil liberties groups. The accumulation of FIR charges raises broader constitutional questions about the use of criminal law to restrict civil society dissent. Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(b) of the Constitution guarantee freedom of speech and peaceful assembly, subject only to narrowly defined restrictions. The Shreya Singhal (2015) judgment's "chilling effect" doctrine is directly applicable: laws that deter legitimate advocacy through fear of prosecution dilute constitutional freedoms even if no conviction follows.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Citizens for Justice and Peace (CJP):</strong> Mumbai-based civil liberties organisation founded after the 2002 Gujarat riots to provide legal aid to victims. It filed affidavits in the Zakia Jafri case (SC, challenging the SIT's clean chit to then-CM Modi) and actively challenged the CAA-NRC framework. Secretary Teesta Setalvad's arrest in 2022 was upheld for trial by the SC, with the Court declining to quash the FIR — a contested decision criticised by jurists for its potential chilling effect.</li>
<li><strong>Article 19 — Freedom of Speech and Assembly:</strong> Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech; 19(1)(b) guarantees peaceful assembly without arms. Restrictions under Articles 19(2)–19(3) must be reasonable and relate to enumerated grounds (public order, sovereignty, security of State, etc.). In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015), the SC struck down Section 66A of the IT Act for being vague and having a "chilling effect" on legitimate expression — the benchmark now applied to assess speech-restricting laws.</li>
<li><strong>FIR and the Lalita Kumari Judgment (2014):</strong> The SC's Constitution Bench in Lalita Kumari v. State of U.P. mandated mandatory FIR registration for cognizable offences — police cannot conduct a preliminary inquiry before registering. However, the addition of charges after initial FIR registration involves invocation of Section 173 BNSS (Section 173 CrPC earlier) for supplementary chargesheet, a process susceptible to harassment if used without evidentiary basis.</li>
<li><strong>FCRA and Civil Society Restrictions:</strong> The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 (amended 2020) restricts foreign-funded NGOs. The 2020 amendments banned sub-granting and mandated designated FCRA accounts. Amnesty International India shut operations in 2020 citing FCRA freeze on funds. CIVICUS Monitor has rated India's civil space as 'Repressed' since 2019 — a designation used in UN Human Rights Council reviews.</li>
<li><strong>Role of Civil Society in Indian Democracy:</strong> The Supreme Court in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) recognized PIL standing for civil society. The National Policy on Voluntary Sector (2007) committed to an enabling environment for NGOs. Internationally, the UN Declaration on Human Rights Defenders (1998) affirms the right to promote and protect human rights — a standard India is obligated to uphold as a signatory to the ICCPR.</li>
</ul>
</div>

HarperCollins India announced it will publish a memoir by Sonia Gandhi on November 10, 2026. Sonia Gandhi served as Congress president from 1998 to 2022 — the longest tenure by any Congress chief — before handing over to Mallikarjun Kharge. Born in Italy, she married Rajiv Gandhi in 1968 and entered politics after his assassination in 1991. As UPA chairperson (2004–2014), she was the de facto power centre despite not holding constitutional office, overseeing landmark social legislation through the National Advisory Council — MGNREGA, RTI, NFSA, and the Forest Rights Act. Her decision to forgo the prime ministership in 2004 in favour of Manmohan Singh remains a defining moment in post-independence political history. The memoir is expected to illuminate internal Congress dynamics, coalition-era governance, and her foreign-origin controversy. Publication coincides with Congress's organisational revamp ahead of UP assembly elections.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Congress Party Presidents — Key Timeline:</strong> The Indian National Congress (est. 1885) has had distinguished presidents including Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Annie Besant (first woman president, 1917), Mahatma Gandhi, and Jawaharlal Nehru. Sonia Gandhi's 1998–2022 tenure is the longest. Mallikarjun Kharge, elected in 2022, is the first non-Gandhi family president since Sitaram Kesri (1997). Dynastic control remains a persistent critique in political discourse.</li>
<li><strong>National Advisory Council (NAC) and Social Legislation:</strong> Chaired by Sonia Gandhi, NAC was an advisory body to the UPA government (2004–2014) that drafted MGNREGA (2005), RTI Act (2005), NFSA (2013), and Forest Rights Act (2006). NAC's constitutional status was debated — as a non-elected body influencing legislation, it raised accountability questions; critics argued it represented a parallel power centre bypassing Cabinet responsibility.</li>
<li><strong>Citizenship and Foreign-Origin Political Eligibility:</strong> Sonia Gandhi renounced Italian citizenship and obtained Indian citizenship under the Citizenship Act, 1955. The Constitution does not bar foreign-born Indian citizens from elected office (Articles 84 and 173 set eligibility for Parliament — citizen of India, minimum age, not holding office of profit). Her origin was litigated in the Supreme Court in Swaraj Abhiyan v. Election Commission (no bar was found).</li>
<li><strong>UPA Coalition Arithmetic (2004 and 2009):</strong> The UPA won 218 and 262 seats (with allies) in 2004 and 2009 respectively, governing with outside support of Left parties (2004–2008) and then SP/BSP. The coalition structure — with post-poll alliance arithmetic, Common Minimum Programme negotiations, and minority government dynamics — remains a key template for understanding Indian coalition politics, examined in GS2 under parliamentary functioning.</li>
<li><strong>Political Memoirs as Historical Sources:</strong> Memoirs by political leaders — P.V. Narasimha Rao's "The Insider," Pranab Mukherjee's trilogy, P. Chidambaram's "Undaunted" — are treated as primary sources for post-independence political history by historians and UPSC. They provide insider accounts of cabinet decision-making, foreign policy choices, and party dynamics that official records often omit.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Houthis (Ansar Allah):</strong> A Zaydi Shia revivalist movement from Yemen's Saada region, backed by Iran and allied with Hezbollah. They seized Sanaa in 2014–15 and control ~30% of Yemen's territory including most populated areas. <li><strong>Operation Raahat (2015) and India's Yemen Stakes:</strong> India evacuated 4,741 nationals and 960 foreign nationals from Yemen in Operation Raahat — one of the largest non-combatant evacuation operations in its history, involving INS Sumitra and commercial aircraft via Oman and Djibouti. Approximately 10,000 Indians remain in Yemen. India imports over 80% of its crude oil by sea via Gulf routes, making Red Sea security a strategic priority.</li>
<li><strong>Bab-el-Mandeb Strait:</strong> A 29 km-wide chokepoint between Yemen and Djibouti/Eritrea, one of the world's five critical maritime chokepoints (alongside Strait of Hormuz, Malacca, Suez, and Panama). ~10% of global trade transits annually. Houthi attacks since Nov 2023 caused a 40% drop in Suez Canal traffic, diverting to the Cape of Good Hope route and raising global shipping insurance premiums by 200–400%.</li>
<li><strong>UNSC Resolution 2216 (2015):</strong> Imposed a targeted arms embargo on the Houthis and forces loyal to former President Saleh. The resolution authorised the Saudi-led coalition to enforce the embargo. China and Russia have repeatedly vetoed UNSC resolutions seeking to extend accountability measures or restrict Houthi arms flows from Iran — reflecting great power competition in the West Asia context.</li>
<li><strong>India's Strategic Autonomy in Red Sea Crisis:</strong> India has not joined the US-led Operation Prosperity Guardian (Dec 2023) to protect Red Sea shipping, maintaining strategic autonomy. However, the Indian Navy has independently deployed warships for merchant vessel escort in the region and conducted anti-piracy patrols under its Extended Neighbourhood maritime doctrine, balancing security interests without formal alliance commitments.</li>
</ul>
