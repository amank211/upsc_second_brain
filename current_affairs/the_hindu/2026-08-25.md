<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Three newborns killed in fire at Maharashtra hospital's SNCU</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Health</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Accountability in public healthcare delivery; regulatory oversight of hospitals
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Health infrastructure; vulnerable populations (newborns/NICU)

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A devastating fire broke out in the Special Newborn Care Unit (SNCU) of a government hospital in Amravati, Maharashtra, killing three premature newborns. The fire reportedly originated from a ventilator malfunction; fire alarms and sprinkler systems failed to activate in time. Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis ordered a high-level inquiry and promised action against those responsible. The incident exposes chronic gaps in fire-safety compliance in public hospitals, inadequate maintenance of medical equipment, and weak accountability in healthcare infrastructure management. SNCUs are critical tertiary-care units providing intensive support for sick or premature newborns and are mandated under the National Health Mission. The tragedy revives debate about NHM funding adequacy, state health budgets, and enforcement of Clinical Establishment Act standards across public and private facilities. It also highlights the vulnerability of the poorest patients, who depend entirely on public health systems for life-saving interventions.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Newborn Care Unit (SNCU):</strong> Established under the National Health Mission's Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health (RMNCH+A) strategy, SNCUs are district-hospital-level units that provide round-the-clock intensive care to sick and premature newborns. India has over 900 functional SNCUs across states. Their performance is a key indicator under NHM monitoring frameworks.</li>
<li><strong>Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010:</strong> A central law that mandates minimum standards for all clinical establishments (hospitals, clinics, labs) including fire safety, staffing, and equipment maintenance. States must adopt it; adoption remains uneven. Maharashtra has its own Maharashtra Medical Council Act and clinical establishment rules, but enforcement is weak in public facilities.</li>
<li><strong>National Health Mission (NHM):</strong> Launched in 2005, NHM funds states for health infrastructure, human resources, and technology including equipment for SNCUs. The Centre-State funding ratio is 60:40 (90:10 for special category states). Underutilisation of NHM funds and delayed procurement have repeatedly been flagged by CAG reports.</li>
<li><strong>Fire Safety in Hospitals — NABH & NBC Standards:</strong> National Building Code 2016 and NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals) standards mandate functional fire-detection, suppression, and evacuation systems in health facilities. However, most public hospitals are not NABH-accredited, and compliance with NBC is rarely audited, creating persistent safety gaps.</li>
<li><strong>Accountability Mechanisms:</strong> District Collectors are the nodal authority for disaster response at the district level; the State Disaster Response Force (SDRF) handles immediate rescue. For healthcare lapses, the state government can invoke the Indian Penal Code (now Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita) provisions on culpable homicide by negligence (Section 105/106 BNS), but prosecution of public officials remains rare.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">T.N. drops Parandur airport project; new sites identified for Terminal 5</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Infrastructure</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Infrastructure → Airport development; land acquisition; public-private partnership
- GS Paper II → Governance → Centre-State coordination; policy reversal due to public opposition

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Tamil Nadu government has officially abandoned the proposed greenfield airport at Parandur, near Chennai, following sustained farmer protests over fertile agricultural land acquisition and environmental concerns. The state has now identified alternative sites for a new Terminal 5 expansion within or adjacent to the existing Chennai International Airport. The Parandur project, proposed to ease capacity pressures at Chennai airport (which handles over 22 million passengers annually and is operating close to saturation), faced resistance since land surveys began in 2022. The reversal underscores the perennial tension between aviation infrastructure expansion and agricultural land rights in densely populated southern districts. It also raises questions about the adequacy of pre-project Social Impact Assessments and the efficacy of consent mechanisms under the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR Act). Terminal 5 expansion within the existing footprint would require significant land within the Airport Authority of India's (AAI) current boundary.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>LARR Act, 2013:</strong> The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act replaced the colonial Land Acquisition Act of 1894. It mandates Social Impact Assessment (SIA), consent of 70–80% of affected families for PPP/private projects, and compensation at 2–4× circle rates. Government projects for public purposes have fewer consent requirements but still need SIA.</li>
<li><strong>Airports Authority of India (AAI):</strong> A statutory body under the Airports Authority of India Act, 1994 that manages 137 airports across India, including Chennai. AAI has been expanding terminal capacity under the UDAN scheme and National Civil Aviation Policy 2016, but greenfield airports require MoEFCC environmental clearances and state land acquisition support.</li>
<li><strong>National Civil Aviation Policy (NCAP), 2016:</strong> India's first comprehensive civil aviation policy targets 1 billion air trips per year by 2040. It promotes regional connectivity (UDAN), cargo hubs, and MRO industries. Chennai airport's terminal saturation is a bottleneck flagged under NCAP's infrastructure gap analysis.</li>
<li><strong>Social Impact Assessment (SIA):</strong> Mandated under LARR 2013, SIA must be conducted by an independent multi-disciplinary expert group and includes public hearings. The Parandur reversal suggests SIA findings were not adequately factored into initial project approval, a common failure mode in large infrastructure decisions.</li>
<li><strong>PPP in Airports:</strong> India's airport privatisation began with Delhi and Mumbai in 2006 under the PPP model (long-term concession to private operators). AAI retains ownership of land and assets; private entities operate and develop. Greenfield airports like Dholera and Navi Mumbai are under various PPP arrangements. The PPPAC (Public-Private Partnership Appraisal Committee) approves large aviation PPP projects.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Karnataka SIR: 1.07 crore names removed; electorate falls to 4.46 crore</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Electoral rolls; Election Commission of India; Special Intensive Revision
- GS Paper II → Governance → Voter rights; transparency in electoral administration

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Karnataka's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls has resulted in the deletion of 1.07 crore voter names, bringing the total electorate down to 4.46 crore. The Election Commission of India (ECI) conducted the SIR to remove duplicate, dead, and shifted voters, using AASDDO (Absent/Shifted/Dead/Duplicate/Others) categories. Opposition parties and civil society have raised concerns that bulk deletions — many filed via Form 7 by Booth Level Agents (BLAs) — risk disenfranchising genuine voters, particularly migrant workers, daily-wage labourers, and marginalized communities who may not be present to object. The controversy echoes national debates about the integrity of voter list management, the adequacy of safeguards against politically motivated deletions, and whether the ECI's current BLA system provides adequate oversight. ECI maintains that the process follows due procedure: a Booth Level Officer reviews each deletion claim and the voter has a right to file Form 6 to re-register.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR):</strong> An ECI exercise conducted before elections to thoroughly update the electoral roll. Unlike the regular summary revision (twice yearly), SIR involves door-to-door enumeration by BLOs, intensive verification of claims, and bulk processing of deletions and additions. SIR has been controversial in states where large-scale deletions have occurred close to elections.</li>
<li><strong>Form 7 (Objection to Inclusion):</strong> Under the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960, Form 7 allows any elector to object to the inclusion of another person's name. A BLA can file multiple Form 7 objections, but current rules limit this to 10 per day. If a single BLA files above this threshold without Electoral Registration Officer (ERO) prior approval, it is supposed to trigger a review — but enforcement has been inconsistent.</li>
<li><strong>Booth Level Agent (BLA) vs. Booth Level Officer (BLO):</strong> BLOs are government officials responsible for maintaining rolls at the polling-booth level; they are the primary verification authority. BLAs are party representatives who can file claims and objections on behalf of their party. The distinction is crucial — BLAs file, BLOs verify. Complaints allege that BLO verification has been superficial in bulk-deletion scenarios.</li>
<li><strong>AASDDO Categories:</strong> The ECI classifies voters for deletion under Absent (A), Shifted (S), Dead (D), Duplicate (D), and Others (O) — collectively AASDDO. Each category has a prescribed verification process before deletion. Critics argue the "Shifted" and "Absent" categories are prone to abuse, as migrant workers may be temporarily absent but retain domicile rights in their home constituency.</li>
<li><strong>Legal Framework — Representation of the People Act, 1950:</strong> Section 22 governs corrections in rolls; Section 23 governs inclusions. The ERO has quasi-judicial powers over enrollment disputes. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the right to vote, while statutory (not fundamental), cannot be arbitrarily curtailed, and that the ECI must follow the principle of audi alteram partem (right to be heard) before any deletion.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">CRPF officer injured, houses torched as tension rises in Manipur</h2>

<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Security</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Insurgency in North-East; inter-ethnic conflict; CRPF deployment
- GS Paper II → Governance → Breakdown of law and order; Centre-State relations in conflict zones

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Fresh violence in Manipur left a CRPF personnel injured and several houses torched along the Kuki-Naga tension corridor near National Highway 2. The incident reflects the prolonged ethnic conflict between Meitei, Kuki-Zo, and Naga communities that has plagued Manipur since May 2023, displacing over 60,000 people. The state government and Centre have struggled to achieve a durable peace, with militant groups affiliated with various factions periodically violating buffer zones and ceasefire understandings. NH-2 (the Imphal-Jiribam highway) is a strategic lifeline whose blockades and ambushes have repeatedly disrupted supplies to the valley. The Army, Assam Rifles, and CRPF operate under a unified command structure in the state, but the civilian administration remains paralysed in conflict-affected districts. The violence also complicates the upcoming Census 2027 operations, as discussed separately on Page 4.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Armed Forces Special Powers Act (AFSPA), 1958:</strong> Grants armed forces special powers to conduct operations in "disturbed areas." Manipur has been under AFSPA since 1958, with periodic extensions. The Act has been challenged before the Supreme Court (Naga People's Movement, 1998; Extra-Judicial Execution case, 2016) but upheld with the caveat that excesses must be investigated. Its continued application is a contentious issue in North-East peace talks.</li>
<li><strong>Unified Command Structure:</strong> In states with active insurgency, the Centre establishes a Unified Command with the state's Chief Minister as chair and the Army GOC, IG CRPF, and DGP as members. It is meant to coordinate civilian and military operations. Critics argue it erodes civilian supremacy as military considerations often override administrative ones in practice.</li>
<li><strong>Manipur Conflict Dynamics (2023–present):</strong> Violence erupted in May 2023 following a High Court order directing the state government to recommend ST status for Meiteis. Kuki-Zo groups, who already hold ST status, opposed this, fearing dilution of their land and reservation rights. The conflict has now hardened into a quasi-territorial division of the state along valley (Meitei) and hill (Kuki-Zo) lines.</li>
<li><strong>NH-2 Strategic Significance:</strong> National Highway 2 (formerly NH-53) connects Imphal with Jiribam and eventually to the rest of India via Assam. It is one of only two surface supply routes to Manipur's valley. Blockades on this highway have been weaponised by all sides; a sustained blockade in 2023 caused acute shortages of food, fuel, and medicines in Imphal.</li>
<li><strong>Suspension of Operations (SoO) Agreements:</strong> The Centre and state have SoO agreements with several Kuki militant groups since 2008, under which these groups remain in designated camps in exchange for suspension of offensive operations. Following the 2023 violence, the state government unilaterally ended SoO with some groups, which the Centre reversed, highlighting Centre-State tensions in managing the conflict.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">NCDC Amendment Act, 2026 is an onslaught on federal rights: SKM</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Federalism</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Federalism; Seventh Schedule; Centre-State legislative relations
- GS Paper II → Governance → Cooperative societies; Centre vs. State jurisdiction

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Sikkim Krantikari Morcha (SKM), the ruling party of Sikkim, has condemned the National Cooperative Development Corporation (Amendment) Act, 2026 as an unconstitutional encroachment on states' rights. The party argues that "Cooperation" falls under Entry 32 of the State List of the Seventh Schedule, making it an exclusive state subject. The Centre, however, has been steadily expanding its footprint in the cooperative sector — most visibly by creating a dedicated Ministry of Cooperation in 2021 and pushing the Multi-State Cooperative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023. The NCDC Amendment Act, 2026 reportedly extends NCDC's lending and supervisory mandate over state-registered cooperative societies, which critics see as converting a Concurrent-type framework into central dominance. The controversy connects to larger debates about "cooperative federalism" versus the Centre's push for "One Nation" uniformity in economic and social regulation. Several opposition-led states share SKM's concerns.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Seventh Schedule — State List Entry 32:</strong> "Incorporation, regulation and winding up of corporations, other than those specified in List I, and universities; unincorporated trading, literary, scientific, religious and other societies and associations; co-operative societies." This makes cooperation a State subject. The Centre's constitutional basis for NCDC and multi-state cooperatives derives from Entry 43 (Union List) for corporations incorporated under central law and the Concurrent List Entry 44 for "incorporation, regulation, etc." of certain corporations.</li>
<li><strong>National Cooperative Development Corporation (NCDC):</strong> Established in 1963 under the NCDC Act, it is a statutory body under the Ministry of Cooperation that provides financial assistance for cooperative development programmes. Its traditional mandate covers storage, marketing, and processing cooperatives. The 2026 Amendment reportedly expands its regulatory powers beyond purely financial assistance roles.</li>
<li><strong>Ministry of Cooperation (2021):</strong> Created by bifurcating the erstwhile Agriculture Ministry, this standalone ministry is seen as a tool to strengthen the Centre's role in cooperative governance. Its stated objectives include modernising cooperative laws, promoting multi-state cooperatives, and integrating cooperatives into the National Cooperative Policy framework.</li>
<li><strong>Multi-State Cooperative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023:</strong> Amended the MSCS Act, 2002 to increase government oversight — including mandatory representation for Central Government nominees on boards of large multi-state cooperatives, concurrent audit requirements, and new dispute resolution mechanisms. Opposition states called it an attempt to "capture" apolitical cooperative structures.</li>
<li><strong>Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State Relations (1988):</strong> Recommended that State List subjects should remain inviolate and that Parliament should not use Article 249 (legislation on State subjects in national interest with Rajya Sabha approval) casually. The Punchhi Commission (2010) reiterated that cooperative federalism requires consultation mechanisms, not unilateral central action on state subjects.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Stone quarries near Assam's TSAT semiconductor plant pose risk to ₹27,000 crore project</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Industry</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Industry → Semiconductor ecosystem; Make in India; electronics manufacturing
- GS Paper III → Environment → Mining regulation; vibration damage to sensitive infrastructure

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Tata Semiconductor Assembly and Test (TSAT) plant at Jagiroad, Assam — a ₹27,000 crore investment under India's semiconductor mission — is threatened by blasting operations at stone quarries in close proximity. Semiconductor manufacturing and packaging require vibration-free environments; even minor ground tremors from quarry blasting can damage precision equipment and contaminate cleanrooms. Local authorities have so far failed to enforce adequate exclusion zones around the facility. The TSAT Jagiroad project, announced in 2024 under the India Semiconductor Mission's modified scheme (providing 50% fiscal support under the Semicon India Programme), represents a critical node in India's ambition to reduce chip import dependence (India currently imports over $24 billion in semiconductors annually). The incident highlights the challenge of last-mile regulatory enforcement when large industrial projects are situated near existing extractive industries, and raises questions about the adequacy of environment and land-use planning around strategically sensitive investments.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) / Semicon India Programme:</strong> Launched in December 2021 with ₹76,000 crore outlay, ISM offers 50% fiscal support for semiconductor fabs, display fabs, and ATMP (Assembly, Testing, Marking and Packaging) units. Approved projects include Tata Electronics (fab in Dholera, ATMP in Jagiroad), Micron Technology (ATMP in Sanand), and CG Power-Renesas (ATMP in Sanand).</li>
<li><strong>TSAT Jagiroad (Assam):</strong> Tata's Assembly, Test, and Packaging facility at Jagiroad in Morigaon district, Assam, is India's first major semiconductor packaging plant. It will manufacture chips for automotive, industrial, and consumer electronics markets. The Assam government provided land and infrastructure support as part of its semiconductor policy.</li>
<li><strong>Cleanroom Sensitivity and Vibration:</strong> Semiconductor packaging cleanrooms require Class 10–100 environments (particles per cubic foot) and vibration isolation down to nanometre tolerances. Even low-level ground vibrations from quarry blasting — transmitted through soil — can cause yield losses, equipment damage, and product contamination. Industry standards require vibration levels below VC-D criteria (~6.25 μm/s RMS) in critical zones.</li>
<li><strong>Mining Regulation — Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957:</strong> Central legislation governing grant of mining leases. Stone quarries (minor minerals) are regulated by states under Section 15 of the MMDR Act. Exclusion zones around sensitive infrastructure are not automatically specified — they require state government notification or court orders, leaving a governance gap.</li>
<li><strong>India's Semiconductor Import Dependence:</strong> India imports approximately $24–27 billion worth of semiconductors annually, making it one of the world's largest chip importers relative to domestic production (nearly zero). The India Semiconductor Mission aims to create domestic capacity for 85% of India's semiconductor demand by 2030, covering automotive, defence, telecom, and consumer electronics verticals.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">SC bans constructions within 100m of Rajasthan's Jojari River; hazardous industries within 500m</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 3 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → River conservation; judicial intervention in environmental protection
- GS Paper II → Governance → Judiciary; Article 21 (right to clean environment)

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Supreme Court of India has imposed a ban on all constructions within 100 metres of the Jojari River in Rajasthan and prohibited hazardous industries within 500 metres. The Jojari, a seasonal river flowing through Jodhpur, has been severely impacted by industrial effluent discharge, untreated sewage, and encroachments along its banks. The SC's intervention follows a suo motu case and PIL petitions highlighting the river's ecological degradation. The ruling is significant as it applies the precautionary principle and the public trust doctrine to riparian zones, reinforcing that rivers are not merely drainage channels but living ecosystems with constitutional protection under Articles 21 and 48A. Rajasthan's State Pollution Control Board (SPCB) has been directed to submit a remediation plan. The judgment is likely to have ripple effects on similar river-encroachment disputes in other states.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Public Trust Doctrine:</strong> Articulated in India in M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1997), this doctrine holds that natural resources — rivers, forests, air — are held in trust by the state for public use and cannot be alienated for private profit. It has been the basis for numerous SC and High Court orders protecting rivers, lakes, and forests from encroachment and industrial pollution.</li>
<li><strong>Precautionary Principle:</strong> One of the principles of international environmental law (Principle 15, Rio Declaration, 1992) adopted by Indian courts: where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures. It has been used to impose buffer zones and moratoria on industrial activity near sensitive ecosystems.</li>
<li><strong>Article 21 and Right to Clean Environment:</strong> The SC has read into Article 21 (right to life) the right to a healthy and clean environment (Subhash Kumar v. <li><strong>Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974:</strong> The primary legislation for water pollution control in India, implemented through Central and State Pollution Control Boards. Enforcement has been weak in many states; CAG reports have repeatedly noted low compliance rates and inadequate SPCB capacity.</li>

<ul>
<li><strong>Census Act, 1948:</strong> Governs the conduct of the decennial population census by the Office of the Registrar General and Census Commissioner of India (under the Home Ministry). The 84th Constitutional Amendment (2001) froze delimitation until 2026; post-2026 delimitation will be based on the first census after 2026 — making Census 2027 data directly consequential for seat distribution and potentially the number of Lok Sabha seats under the 101st Amendment (One Nation One Election context).</li>

<li><strong>House Listing Phase:</strong> The first phase of Census operations (normally September–October preceding the population enumeration year) maps every household's physical structure, ownership, amenities, and assets. <div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The UK Met Office warns the developing El Niño could peak at over 3°C above normal Pacific sea surface temperatures — the strongest in over a century — potentially making 2027 the hottest year ever recorded. For India, strong El Niño events associate with below-normal monsoon rainfall, drought risk in Peninsular India and Vidarbha, and above-normal summer temperatures. A rapid El Niño onset in late 2026 could affect the Rabi season and reduce Kharif 2027 prospects dramatically. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO):</strong> Anomalous warming of Central/East Pacific SSTs (Niño 3.4 region) disrupts the Walker Circulation, weakening the South Asian monsoon trough. The 2015–16 Super El Niño (~2.6°C) produced below-normal but not catastrophic rainfall in India, partly offset by a positive IOD. A 3°C+ peak would surpass 2015–16 and could produce the most severe rainfall deficiency in decades.</li>
<li><strong>PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana):</strong> India's flagship crop insurance scheme (2016); premiums are 1.5–2% Kharif, 2% Rabi, 5% commercial crops (heavily subsidised). <li><strong>National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC), 2008:</strong> 8 National Missions including the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA) addressing drought and heat-stress farm resilience. <li><strong>IMD Seasonal Forecasting:</strong> IMD issues Extended Range Forecasts (2–4 weeks) and Seasonal Outlooks (June–September ISMR) using the Climate Forecast System (CFS) model and APCC multi-model ensemble. </ul>

This editorial analyses the SC's 9-judge constitutional bench ruling: the majority (6:3) held that the Industrial Relations Code (IRC) 2020 — which replaced the Industrial Disputes Act 1947 from November 2025 — must be interpreted on its own terms, independent of the 1978 BWSSB triple test for "industry." **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>BWSSB v. T. Rajappa (1978):</strong> A 7-judge SC bench held "industry" under IDA includes any systematic activity with employer-employee cooperation for goods/services production satisfying human wants — covering hospitals, municipalities, clubs, and charitable trusts. <div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
This Text & Context explainer traces the judicial history of "industry" from BWSSB 1978 to the 2026 9-judge bench's IRC-specific ruling. BWSSB held "industry" broadly — systematic activity with employer-employee cooperation producing goods/services for community needs, covering hospitals, universities, and municipalities (the BWSSB triple test). **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Industrial Disputes Act (IDA), 1947:</strong> The foundational labour legislation governing industrial disputes, retrenchment, layoff, closure, and conciliation/adjudication mechanisms for 78 years. <li><strong>9-Judge Constitutional Bench:</strong> The 6:3 majority definitively resolves the "industry" definitional question for the IRC era — binding on all subordinate courts. <li><strong>Trade Union Rights under IRC:</strong> The IRC retains trade union registration from the Trade Unions Act 1926 but changes the recognition threshold — unions representing 51% of workers in an "industry" get sole negotiating status. If "industry" narrows, fewer workplaces fall under collective bargaining, effectively reducing trade union leverage in hospitals, NGOs, and educational institutions.</li>
<li><strong>EODB vs. Labour Rights Tension:</strong> India's EODB improvements (World Bank rank: 142nd in 2014 → 63rd in 2019) included labour law flexibility as a key pillar. The four Labour Codes are the legislative expression of this shift. The IRC ruling is the first major judicial confirmation of its scope — and the minority dissent signals that the tension between labour protection and flexibility will continue to be litigated as the IRC is applied across sectors.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
UPI celebrates its 10th anniversary on August 25, 2026 — exactly a decade since NPCI launched it in 2016. FY17: 1.78 crore transactions. FY26: 24,162 crore transactions worth approximately ₹314 lakh crore — a 13,000-fold volume surge. UPI is now operational in 11 countries including Singapore (PayNow-UPI linkage), UAE, UK, France, and Mauritius. Domestically it accounts for over 80% of all retail digital payments by volume. Ongoing innovations include UPI Lite (offline ≤₹500), UPI 123PAY (feature phones), and UPI-One World (foreign visitors). The anniversary is a platform for showcasing India's Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) model globally at G20 and bilateral forums.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Unified Payments Interface (UPI):</strong> Developed by NPCI, launched August 2016. Real-time interbank transfer via Virtual Payment Address, built on IMPS rails. Interoperable across banks and third-party apps (PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm, BHIM). Operates 24/7/365; per-transaction limit ₹1 lakh (₹5 lakh for certain categories). Zero MDR for person-to-person and sub-₹2,000 merchant transactions is government policy.</li>
<li><strong>NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India):</strong> Not-for-profit umbrella organisation under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, owned by a bank consortium with RBI oversight. Runs UPI, IMPS, RuPay, NACH, FastTag, BBPS, and other payment systems. NPCI International Payments Limited (NIPL) handles UPI's global expansion into new corridors.</li>
<li><strong>India Stack and Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI):</strong> Open API-based government digital infrastructure — Aadhaar (identity), eKYC, DigiLocker (documents), UPI (payments), ABDM (health). India Stack is cited as a model for DPI — open, interoperable, government-backed platforms enabling private innovation at scale. India promotes DPI export to Global South nations through the G20 DPI Framework (2023).</li>
<li><strong>UPI Internationalisation:</strong> India-Singapore PayNow-UPI linkage (2023) was the first cross-border corridor for real-time remittances; since followed by UAE, UK, France, Mauritius, Nepal, and Bhutan. India aims to position UPI as the preferred protocol for the 18 million Indian diaspora and India-linked trade corridors. RBI is exploring UPI-SWIFT interconnection for correspondent banking integration.</li>
<li><strong>Zero MDR Tradeoffs:</strong> Since January 2020, zero MDR for UPI and RuPay debit transactions drove adoption but removed the revenue model for payment service providers, causing market consolidation (3–4 large players dominate the UPI app market). NPCI and RBI are studying a tiered MDR model for large-value merchant transactions to sustain the ecosystem's financial viability without discouraging small-merchant adoption.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">U.S. vows 'economic asphyxiation' of Iran with expanded secondary sanctions</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Diplomacy</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 14 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → Iran; US sanctions; West Asia geopolitics; energy security
- GS Paper III → Economy → Oil market; Strait of Hormuz; impact on India

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
US Treasury Secretary Bessent announced expanded "maximum pressure" secondary sanctions on Iran targeting digital assets, gold trade, aviation, and shipping — describing it as "economic asphyxiation." Secondary sanctions threaten third-country entities including Indian companies and banks that deal with Iran. With the Strait of Hormuz threatened by IRGC Navy blockade as leverage, oil markets remain on edge. India, which imports significant Iranian crude during sanctions-waiver periods (~25 MT/year during JCPOA era), faces renewed pressure to curtail residual Iranian energy ties. Expanded secondary sanctions also complicate India's Chabahar Port strategy, for which the US had maintained a humanitarian exemption.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Secondary Sanctions:</strong> Target non-US persons transacting with sanctioned entities — threatening exclusion from the US financial system. Extraterritorial reach makes US sanctions highly effective but internationally controversial. India faced this pressure over Iranian oil in 2018–19 and was granted a temporary Special Purpose Vehicle waiver that eventually expired without renewal.</li>
<li><strong>Strait of Hormuz:</strong> Approximately 33–39 km wide at its narrowest; ~20–21 million barrels/day pass through (~20% of global petroleum trade). A closure would spike oil prices potentially above $150/barrel, directly impacting India's import bill, current account deficit, and domestic inflation. The IRGC Navy's fast-boat interdiction capability makes this a credible (if mutually devastating) threat.</li>
<li><strong>JCPOA (Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action), 2015:</strong> Nuclear deal between Iran and P5+1 limiting Iran's enrichment in exchange for sanctions relief. Trump administration withdrew in 2018; Biden-era Vienna talks (2021–23) to revive it collapsed. Iran has since expanded uranium enrichment to near-weapons-grade (60%), making any new deal terms significantly harder to negotiate and the maximum-pressure strategy more aggressive than its 2018–19 predecessor.</li>
<li><strong>India-Iran Oil Trade History:</strong> India was historically one of Iran's top three oil customers (~25 MT/year during JCPOA era, with concessional terms — rupee payment, extended credit, freight and insurance discounts). Following 2018 sanctions re-imposition, India wound down Iranian imports to near-zero by mid-2019. Current expanded secondary sanctions make even residual trade extremely risky for Indian refiners (IOC, BPCL, MRPL).</li>
<li><strong>Chabahar Port — India's Strategic Interest:</strong> India invested in Iran's Chabahar Port (Sistan-Baluchestan) as an alternative trade route to Afghanistan and Central Asia, bypassing Pakistan. The US had granted a specific humanitarian exemption for Chabahar. Expanded secondary sanctions create banking and legal risks even for Chabahar-related transactions — potentially jeopardising India's connectivity strategy and relationships with Afghan and Central Asian trading partners.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">U.S. proposes $103,265 H-1B visa fee; major implications for Indian IT sector and diaspora</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Diaspora</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 15 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-US relations; Indian diaspora; skilled migration
- GS Paper III → Economy → IT sector; remittances; skilled labour export

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The US DHS has proposed a new H-1B visa fee of $103,265 — a dramatic increase from the current ~$5,000–10,000 range — as a protectionist measure to reduce reliance on high-skilled temporary foreign workers and prioritise domestic hiring. Indians account for over 70% of H-1B approvals annually; Indian IT companies (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL) are among the largest H-1B users. The fee would make H-1B economically unviable for most medium-skilled roles and significantly raise costs even for critical-skill positions. It must survive regulatory comment and potential legal challenges. India is expected to raise this bilaterally under iCET and trade frameworks, as remittances from the Indian-American tech diaspora (~$12–15 billion annually) represent a significant invisible export for India.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>H-1B Visa Programme:</strong> US non-immigrant visa for specialty occupation workers. Annual cap: 65,000 regular + 20,000 master's-degree exemption. Demand vastly exceeds supply; USCIS conducts a lottery. Holders stay up to 6 years (3+3) and can extend with an approved I-140 immigrant petition. H-4 spousal visa holders can work if the H-1B holder has an approved I-140.</li>
<li><strong>Indian Dominance in H-1B:</strong> Indians receive ~70–75% of H-1B approvals annually. Major IT exporters (TCS, Infosys, Wipro) use H-1B to staff US client sites. India's US IT services business (~$150+ billion annually) depends on the ability to deploy skilled engineers temporarily in the US market — making the proposed fee an existential challenge to current delivery models.</li>
<li><strong>Remittances to India:</strong> India is the world's largest remittance recipient (~$120+ billion in FY2024, World Bank). The US is the single largest source (~20%). The Indian-American tech diaspora (estimated 4–5 million) is among the highest-earning immigrant communities globally. A chilling of H-1B issuance would reduce this inflow over time and affect NRI deposit growth in Indian banks.</li>
<li><strong>H-1B Reform History:</strong> The Obama era raised prevailing wage requirements; Trump's first term drastically reduced approvals via a surge in Requests for Evidence (RFE); Biden reversed several Trump-era restrictions. The proposed $103,265 fee is the most aggressive fee-based deterrence ever proposed — primarily targeting Indian IT body-shopping firms while also affecting US-headquartered MNCs that rely on H-1B for specialist roles.</li>
<li><strong>India's Bilateral Response Toolkit:</strong> India can raise the H-1B fee under the India-US Trade Policy Forum, the iCET framework, or at G20. India's leverage: the large US goods trade presence; IT services that reduce US corporate costs significantly; Indian market access concessions on data localisation. India has historically preferred diplomatic engagement over retaliatory measures in tech-labour disputes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Mannar locals fear ecological fallout amid Sri Lanka's green energy push at Thambapavani</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Sri Lanka relations; Palk Strait; Tamil community
- GS Paper III → Environment → Renewable energy siting; coastal ecology; wind energy

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Communities around Mannar, northern Sri Lanka, are alarmed over the proposed 104 MW Thambapavani wind farm, citing coastal flooding risks, disruption to artisanal fishing, and damage to migratory bird corridors. The project became politically sensitive after Adani Green Energy pulled out of Sri Lanka wind investments in 2025 following US DoJ bribery allegations. Sri Lanka has halted new wind energy licences while reviewing renewable policy. Sri Lanka targets 70% renewable electricity by 2030 and needs foreign investment to achieve it, but the Mannar episode illustrates the tension between green investment ambitions and community and ecological concerns. The dispute carries an India dimension through Tamil fishing community politics and the unresolved Sri Lankan Tamil political question around the 13th Amendment.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Adani Green in Sri Lanka:</strong> Had signed agreements for 484 MW of wind power in Sri Lanka. Following US DoJ bribery allegations in November 2024, Sri Lanka's Parliament called for review of Adani contracts. The company exited in 2025, creating a vacuum in Sri Lanka's renewable energy pipeline that other foreign investors have been cautious about filling given the political controversy.</li>
<li><strong>Sri Lanka's 70% Renewable Target:</strong> Aims for 70% renewable electricity generation by 2030 from a current ~50% (hydro-dominated) mix. Needs approximately 2,000 MW of new wind and solar. Foreign investment is essential as Sri Lanka's post-2022 economic crisis severely constrained public capital expenditure; the IMF bailout further limits fiscal space for state energy investment.</li>
<li><strong>Gulf of Mannar Biosphere Reserve:</strong> UNESCO-designated biosphere reserve — one of Asia's most ecologically rich shallow marine environments, home to dugongs, sea turtles, coral reefs, seagrass beds, and critical fish breeding grounds. Also a major migratory bird corridor. Wind turbines in this zone raise concerns about bird-strike mortality, habitat disruption, and coastal wind-pattern alteration affecting artisanal fishing communities dependent on seasonal winds.</li>
<li><strong>India-Sri Lanka Fishing Disputes:</strong> Indian fishermen (Tamil Nadu's Rameswaram community) and Sri Lankan fishermen contest fishing rights in the Palk Strait and Gulf of Mannar. Sri Lanka's Navy periodically arrests Indian fishermen for crossing the International Maritime Boundary Line (IMBL). The Mannar wind project adds another layer to local Tamil community sensitivities, intersecting with the unresolved Tamil political question around the 13th Amendment and provincial devolution.</li>
<li><strong>Coastal Buffer Zone Policy:</strong> India's CRZ Notification 2019 restricts activities within 50–200m of the High Tide Line in ecologically sensitive areas. Sri Lanka has analogous rules under its Coast Conservation Act, but post-economic-crisis enforcement capacity is limited — a challenge mirrored across developing countries balancing green investment needs with coastal community rights and ecological protection obligations.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Trump's Kim gambit deepens doubts among Asian allies over U.S. reliability; India 'very cautious'</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Security</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 15 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → US Indo-Pacific policy; QUAD; US alliances; North Korea
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Strategic autonomy; India's defence posture in Indo-Pacific

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Trump administration has scaled back Ulchi Freedom Shield joint exercises with South Korea, cancelled Ssangyong amphibious drills, and put on hold a $14 billion Taiwan arms package — as part of diplomatic outreach to North Korea's Kim Jong Un. Japan fears reduced US commitment to alliance guarantees and potential cost-hike demands. India described its position as "very, very cautious" — a notably emphatic qualifier reflecting worry about US unpredictability in the Indo-Pacific and India's interest in strategic autonomy. The Pentagon has also renamed INDOPACOM back to PACOM, symbolically removing India's explicit incorporation in the command's name — raising questions about the durability of US commitments central to India's QUAD framework.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Ulchi Freedom Shield / Ssangyong Exercises:</strong> Ulchi Freedom Shield is the annual large-scale combined US-South Korea command-post exercise simulating defence against North Korean attack. Ssangyong is the US-ROK amphibious landing exercise. Scaling back follows Trump's first-term pattern (2018 Singapore Summit concessions). South Korea and Japan view any scaling back as weakening deterrence and emboldening North Korea's nuclear posture.</li>
<li><strong>Taiwan Arms Package ($14 billion):</strong> A US Foreign Military Sales package to Taiwan (air defence, naval weapons, armoured vehicles). Placing it "on hold" signals willingness to use Taiwan's security as a bargaining chip with China — deepening ally doubts about reliability of US security commitments. The Taiwan Relations Act (1979) commits the US to provide "defensive arms" but the President retains discretion over timing and scale.</li>
<li><strong>INDOPACOM → PACOM Renaming:</strong> The US Indo-Pacific Command was renamed from USPACOM to USINDOPACOM in 2018 to signal the strategic importance of India and the Indian Ocean. Reverting to PACOM symbolically removes India's explicit incorporation — potentially signalling reduced US emphasis on the Indian Ocean and the India relationship in its Pacific military architecture.</li>
<li><strong>India's Strategic Autonomy:</strong> India participates in the QUAD (with US, Japan, Australia) but has declined formal alliance structures, maintaining non-alignment tradition. Foundational agreements (LEMOA 2016, COMCASA 2018, BECA 2020) enable military interoperability without a mutual defence treaty. When US de-prioritises Indo-Pacific commitments, India's QUAD engagement becomes harder to sustain against growing Chinese pressure — forcing recalibration of India's strategic balance.</li>
<li><strong>North Korea's Nuclear Status and NPT Implications:</strong> North Korea is estimated to possess 40–50 nuclear warheads; tested ICBMs with intercontinental range; withdrew from the NPT in 2003. Any US-Kim deal that freezes (rather than rolls back) North Korea's nuclear programme effectively accepts it as a de facto nuclear state — with implications for non-proliferation norms that India, itself a de facto nuclear state outside the NPT, watches carefully as a precedent for the broader international nuclear order.</li>
</ul>
</div>
