**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Prime Minister Narendra Modi held bilateral talks with Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian on the sidelines of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) Summit in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan — marking the first high-level India-Iran engagement since the United States and Israel launched military operations against Iran in February 2026. Modi called for an immediate ceasefire and peaceful resolution of the conflict, stressing India's vital interest in freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint for roughly 20% of global oil trade. Pezeshkian attributed the conflict to U.S. and Israeli aggression, while expressing willingness for dialogue. Modi also extended an invitation to Pezeshkian to attend the BRICS Summit in New Delhi on September 12–13, 2026, where India holds the presidency. The meeting underlines India's policy of maintaining independent diplomatic channels with all parties to the West Asia conflict, consistent with its doctrine of strategic autonomy. Iran's membership of both the SCO and BRICS gives the engagement a multilateral dimension beyond bilateral energy interests.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO):</strong> A Eurasian political, economic, and security alliance founded in 2001, headquartered in Beijing. India became a full member in 2017 alongside Pakistan. The 2026 SCO Summit was held in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan. The SCO's mandate covers counter-terrorism, trade facilitation, and regional connectivity.</li>
<li><strong>Strait of Hormuz:</strong> A narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which approximately 20–21% of global crude oil and one-third of global LNG trade passes daily. India imports around 85% of its crude oil requirements, making Hormuz navigation a critical strategic interest. Iran has repeatedly threatened closure of the strait during escalations with Western powers.</li>
<li><strong>India's strategic autonomy:</strong> A cornerstone of India's foreign policy that involves maintaining independent, simultaneously engaged relationships with competing powers — the U.S., Russia, China, Iran — rather than aligning exclusively with any bloc. It allows India to pursue economic and security interests without alliance constraints, first articulated under Nehru as non-alignment and evolved under subsequent governments.</li>
<li><strong>India-Iran Chabahar Port Agreement:</strong> India signed a 10-year contract with Iran's Shahid Beheshti Port Company in May 2024 to operate the Chabahar port, despite U.S. sanctions. Chabahar offers India a strategic route bypassing Pakistan to access Afghanistan and Central Asian markets. The agreement is India's most significant investment in Iranian territory.</li>
<li><strong>BRICS 2026 India Presidency:</strong> India holds the BRICS Presidency for 2026 and is hosting the BRICS Summit on September 12–13 in New Delhi. Iran was admitted to BRICS as a full member in January 2024. The summit's agenda includes Global South cooperation, de-dollarisation discussions, and a new development finance framework.</li>
</ul>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Electoral rolls; Election Commission of India; right to vote
- GS Paper II → Governance → Transparency; ECI's powers; judicial oversight of elections

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Election Commission of India's Special Intensive Revision Round 3 (SIR 3.0) of electoral rolls has resulted in the deletion of 6.15 crore names — representing 17.04% of the 36.08 crore entries on the affected rolls. Delhi recorded the highest deletion rate at 32.78%, while Maharashtra alone saw 2.06 crore deletions. The ECI published the draft revised rolls for public objection in August 2026 ahead of upcoming state elections. The Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the SIR process in March 2026 after opposition parties challenged it, alleging the exercise was being misused to disenfranchise voters from specific communities. The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD), reviewing India for the first time since 2007, expressed concern that the SIR may disproportionately affect Muslim voters. SIR involves Booth Level Officers conducting door-to-door verification to remove deceased, duplicated, and permanently relocated electors while enrolling new eligible voters — a process the ECI maintains is essential for roll accuracy.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR):</strong> A periodic process under the Representation of the People Act, 1950, during which Booth Level Officers (BLOs) conduct physical household-level verification to add eligible voters, delete dead/shifted electors, and correct entries. SIR is more intensive than routine summary revisions conducted annually and is used when a major cleansing of rolls is required before elections.</li>
<li><strong>Election Commission of India (ECI):</strong> A constitutional body established under Article 324, vested with superintendence, direction, and control of elections to Parliament and state legislatures. The Chief Election Commissioner has security of tenure equivalent to a Supreme Court judge and can only be removed by Parliament through impeachment. The ECI is the sole authority to prepare, maintain, and revise electoral rolls.</li>
<li><strong>Representation of the People Act, 1950:</strong> The primary legislation governing the preparation and revision of electoral rolls. Section 21 empowers the ECI to order special revisions; Section 22 provides for correction of entries on application or suo motu. The Act mandates that rolls be prepared on a house-to-house basis by enumeration officers.</li>
<li><strong>UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD):</strong> A treaty body monitoring compliance with the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD, 1965), to which India is a signatory. CERD reviewed India for the first time since 2007 in 2026 and expressed concern about the SIR exercise's potential disproportionate impact on Muslims, citing the statistical concentration of deletions in Muslim-majority constituencies.</li>
<li><strong>Right to vote (Article 326 and 19):</strong> Article 326 guarantees the right to vote to every citizen above 18 years on grounds of adult suffrage, subject to disqualifications under law. The Supreme Court in Anuradha Bhasin (2021) and PUCL (2003) has held that the right to vote, while a statutory right, is so fundamental to democracy that restrictions must pass a test of proportionality and non-arbitrariness.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Deputy Chief Minister Losii Dikho of the Naga People's Front (NPF), along with eight other Naga MLAs from the BJP-led ruling coalition in Manipur, wrote to Union Home Minister Amit Shah on August 29, 2026, threatening to withdraw support from the N. Biren Singh government. The letter demanded action against Deputy Chief Minister Nemcha Kipgen (a Kuki-Zo leader), the arrest of Kuki-Zo civil society leaders, and the abrogation of the Suspension of Operations (SoO) agreement — originally signed in 2008 with 25 Kuki-Zo armed groups and periodically renewed. The Naga legislators allege the SoO framework allows Kuki-Zo groups to operate with impunity while Naga civilians face violence. The threat comes amid intensifying Naga-Kuki ethnic violence in Manipur following the February 2026 expansion of the conflict — which had previously remained primarily between Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities since May 2023 — into Naga-inhabited areas of Kangpokpi and Senapati districts.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Suspension of Operations (SoO) Agreement:</strong> A tripartite ceasefire arrangement between the Centre, the Manipur state government, and Kuki-Zo armed groups, first signed in 2008. Under SoO, designated groups must confine their cadres to specified camps and abstain from violent activities in exchange for a ceasefire. The Centre terminated SoO with two groups in March 2023 following violations, but the framework remains with 23 groups.</li>
<li><strong>Manipur ethnic conflict (May 2023):</strong> Violence erupted in May 2023 between the predominantly Hindu Meitei valley community and Christian Kuki-Zo hill tribes following Meitei demands for Scheduled Tribe status. Over 200 persons were killed, 60,000+ displaced, and thousands of structures destroyed. The conflict expanded in 2026 to include Naga communities, creating a three-way confrontation.</li>
<li><strong>Naga People's Front (NPF):</strong> A regional political party representing Naga tribal interests in Manipur and Nagaland. The NPF is a coalition partner in Manipur's BJP-led government; its legislators' support is critical to maintaining the government's majority in the 60-member Manipur Legislative Assembly.</li>
<li><strong>Article 355 and internal disturbance:</strong> Article 355 of the Constitution imposes a duty on the Centre to protect every state against internal disturbance and to ensure states are governed in accordance with the Constitution. Although President's Rule under Article 356 has not been invoked in Manipur, the Centre has deployed substantial paramilitary forces and the Home Minister has chaired multiple security review meetings since May 2023.</li>
<li><strong>Sixth Schedule protections:</strong> Manipur's hill areas (inhabited by Naga and Kuki-Zo tribes) fall under the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution, providing for autonomous district councils (ADCs) with legislative and judicial powers over tribal matters. The ADCs for Naga and Kuki-Zo areas have been significant arenas for competing ethnic claims during the conflict.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">India rejects PCA's Hague ruling; says Indus Waters Treaty remains 'in abeyance'</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Foreign Policy</span>

<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 & 5 · Front Page / News</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Pakistan relations; Indus Waters Treaty; international arbitration
- GS Paper III → Water Resources → Transboundary river management; Ratle Hydroelectric Project

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India formally rejected the Permanent Court of Arbitration's (PCA) ruling at The Hague that the Indus Waters Treaty (IWT) of 1960 remains "fully in force." The PCA, chaired by Prof. Sean D. Murphy of George Washington University, issued interim measures restraining India from proceeding unilaterally with the Ratle Hydroelectric Project (RHEP) on the Chenab river and other western-river projects. India called the proceedings a "grave violation," arguing the World Bank constituted the tribunal "in patent breach" of the IWT's own dispute resolution provisions, which require exhausting the Permanent Indus Commission and Neutral Expert mechanisms before invoking arbitration. India had put the IWT "in abeyance" following the Pahalgam terror attack of April 22, 2025, in which 26 civilians were killed. This is the first time since the treaty's signing in 1960 that India has suspended its obligations. India contends that the treaty cannot be enforced on India while Pakistan sponsors cross-border terrorism. The ruling has significant implications for India's planned hydroelectric development on western rivers allocated primarily to Pakistan under the IWT.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Indus Waters Treaty (1960):</strong> Signed between India and Pakistan under World Bank mediation, the IWT allocates the six rivers of the Indus basin: India gets unrestricted use of the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) and limited use of western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) for non-consumptive purposes. Pakistan receives primary use of the western rivers. The treaty survived three wars and is considered one of the most durable water-sharing agreements globally.</li>
<li><strong>Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA):</strong> An intergovernmental organisation established by the 1899 Hague Convention for the Pacific Settlement of International Disputes. The PCA is not a court per se but provides administrative support for international dispute resolution proceedings. Under IWT Article IX and Annexure G, disputes unresolved through bilateral channels may be referred to a Court of Arbitration whose secretariat is the PCA.</li>
<li><strong>Ratle Hydroelectric Project (RHEP):</strong> A 850 MW run-of-river project on the Chenab river (a western river) in Kishtwar district, Jammu & Kashmir, constructed by NHPC. Pakistan objected that RHEP's design — particularly its pondage and operating parameters — violates IWT provisions. India maintained the project complies fully with treaty obligations. The RHEP dispute triggered the PCA proceedings.</li>
<li><strong>IWT dispute resolution mechanism (Article IX):</strong> The IWT prescribes a three-tier resolution process: (1) the Permanent Indus Commission (bilateral, annual meetings), (2) a Neutral Expert appointed jointly for technical matters, (3) a Court of Arbitration for legal disputes. India argues Pakistan bypassed the Neutral Expert process and directly invoked the PCA, making the proceedings procedurally invalid — a position India has maintained since 2016.</li>
<li><strong>India's "in abeyance" stance post-Pahalgam (April 2025):</strong> Following the Pahalgam attack, India announced the IWT would be suspended — the first such suspension in 65 years. India formally notified Pakistan seeking a renegotiation of the treaty under changed circumstances (Pakistan's alleged state-sponsored terrorism). Under international law (Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, Article 62), material change of circumstances can be grounds for suspension of a treaty.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Urban governance; Delhi Development Authority; LG-government relations
- GS Paper III → Environment → Urban green spaces; Yamuna floodplain; heritage conservation; Aravalli ridge

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Delhi Lieutenant Governor Taranjit Singh Sandhu outlined key elements of the Master Plan for Delhi 2047 (MPD 2047) in an op-ed, focusing on green-blue infrastructure and heritage preservation. The plan proposes reclaiming 1,700 hectares of Yamuna floodplain for ecological restoration, developing a 52-kilometre urban cycling corridor to reduce vehicular pollution, and protecting the Delhi Ridge — 7,784 hectares of the Aravalli forest range — from further encroachment. MPD 2047 also plans to document and conserve 1,500 heritage assets under Conservation and Restoration Management (CRM) Plans. A key financial tool proposed is Transferable Development Rights (TDR), which compensates landowners whose properties are restricted for heritage or green use by granting equivalent development rights usable or tradeable elsewhere. The plan is prepared by the Delhi Development Authority (DDA) under the Delhi Development Act, 1957 and succeeds MPD 2021. Its green-blue provisions directly respond to repeated National Green Tribunal orders on Yamuna floodplain encroachment.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Master Plan for Delhi (MPD):</strong> A statutory land-use plan prepared by the Delhi Development Authority under the Delhi Development Act, 1957. The plan governs zoning, density, infrastructure, and green space in Delhi and is revised approximately every 20 years. MPD 2001 and MPD 2021 preceded MPD 2047. Plans require central government approval as Delhi is a Union Territory.</li>
<li><strong>Delhi Development Authority (DDA):</strong> A statutory body established under the Delhi Development Act, 1957, responsible for planned development of Delhi. DDA acquires land, develops housing, and prepares the Master Plan. It functions under the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs and is chaired by the Lieutenant Governor, making it distinct from most state urban bodies.</li>
<li><strong>Yamuna floodplain and NGT orders:</strong> The Yamuna floodplain in Delhi spans approximately 9,700 hectares but has been heavily encroached upon by residential colonies, industrial units, and stadiums. The National Green Tribunal has issued multiple orders requiring removal of encroachments and restoration of the floodplain's natural flood-attenuation and groundwater-recharge functions.</li>
<li><strong>Transferable Development Rights (TDR):</strong> A planning mechanism under which a landowner whose property is restricted from development (heritage, green belt, road widening) receives TDR certificates equivalent to the lost floor area ratio. These can be used by the owner or sold to developers in designated receiving zones where higher FAR is permitted. TDR is used in Mumbai's development control regulations and is now proposed for Delhi under MPD 2047.</li>
<li><strong>Delhi Ridge (Aravalli extension):</strong> A southern spur of the Aravalli mountain range running through Delhi in four segments covering 7,784 hectares. Classified as a Reserved Forest, the Ridge provides urban cooling, groundwater recharge, and biodiversity corridors. It has faced severe encroachment from construction projects and quarrying activity over the decades.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Freedom of religion; anti-conversion laws; state legislative powers
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Minority rights; religious freedom; coalition politics

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Goa government's proposed anti-conversion legislation has been deferred following internal pressure from minority MLAs within the BJP-led coalition. The Bill proposed life imprisonment and a ₹50,000 fine for forced conversions, with ₹5 lakh compensation to victims. Catholic MLAs Michael Lobo, Delilah Lobo, and Lourenco pressed Chief Minister Pramod Sawant to shelve the legislation, citing its potential to alienate Goa's substantial Catholic minority and damage communal harmony. Goa is unique among BJP-governed states in having approximately 25% Christian population — a legacy of 450 years of Portuguese colonisation — making the political calculus of anti-conversion legislation considerably more sensitive than in states such as UP or MP. The episode illustrates how coalition arithmetic constrains the state-level legislative agenda even on ideologically important issues for the ruling party.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Anti-conversion laws (Freedom of Religion Acts):</strong> Multiple BJP-governed states have enacted laws restricting conversions through fraud, force, or inducement — Madhya Pradesh (1968, amended 2022), Uttar Pradesh (2021), Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka (2022), and Uttarakhand (2022). They typically require advance notice to district authorities for voluntary conversions and impose criminal penalties of 1–10 years imprisonment for coercive conversions.</li>
<li><strong>Article 25 — Freedom of Religion:</strong> Guarantees all citizens the right to freely profess, practise, and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, and health. The Supreme Court in Rev. Stanislaus vs. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977) held the right to propagate does not include a right to convert others, thereby upholding state anti-conversion laws as constitutional.</li>
<li><strong>Goa's Christian demography:</strong> Approximately 25% of Goa's population is Christian (predominantly Catholic), among the highest in any Indian state — a legacy of Portuguese colonisation (1510–1961). The Goa Inquisition (1561–1774) involved both forced conversions and suppression of Hindu practices, making the subject historically charged in both directions.</li>
<li><strong>Goa Civil Code (Uniform Civil Code precedent):</strong> Goa retains Portuguese civil law in matters of family and inheritance — making it the only Indian state with a functioning Uniform Civil Code. This is frequently cited in national debates as a model for a nationwide UCC. The state's pluralistic legal framework contrasts with demands for uniformity from religious majority perspectives.</li>
<li><strong>Coalition arithmetic as a legislative constraint:</strong> Coalition governments in Indian states routinely face constraints from partners representing minority or regional interests. Anti-conversion legislation in Goa risks not only losing coalition support but also alienating Catholic voters whose constituencies the BJP needs for its Goa majority. This is a classic example of coalition fragmentation limiting ideological legislation.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Collegium system:</strong> A judge-led appointment mechanism for the Supreme Court and High Courts, evolved through three Supreme Court judgments — S.P. Gupta case (First Judges' Case, 1982) where executive primacy was held; Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (Second Judges' Case, 1993) where judicial primacy was established; and the Presidential Reference (Third Judges' Case, 1998) which defined the Collegium as the CJI plus the four senior-most SC judges. <li><strong>Roster controversy:</strong> The Chief Justice of a High Court has the power to constitute benches and assign cases (roster). The Supreme Court, in the 2018 Press Conference by four SC judges, had highlighted similar concerns at the national level.</li>
<li><strong>Memorandum of Procedure (MoP):</strong> A document governing the process for judicial appointments, negotiated between the executive and the Collegium. This unresolved MoP remains a source of tension between judiciary and executive.</li>
</ul>

Article 19(3) allows the state to impose reasonable restrictions on this right in the interests of India's sovereignty and integrity, or public order. The Supreme Court in Ramlila Maidan Incident (2012) held that restrictions on assembly must be proportionate and the police cannot disperse peaceful assemblies arbitrarily.</li>
<li><strong>BRICS Summit 2026 (New Delhi):</strong> India holds the BRICS Presidency in 2026 and is hosting the BRICS Summit on September 12–13 in New Delhi. BRICS comprises Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, and newer members (UAE, Saudi Arabia, Ethiopia, Egypt, Iran). Security arrangements for the summit involve multi-layer cordoning of central Delhi and movement restrictions.</li>
<li><strong>Section 144 CrPC / BNSS and preventive restrictions:</strong> Section 144 of the CrPC (now Section 163 BNSS) empowers a District Magistrate or Executive Magistrate to prohibit assembly of five or more persons in a specified area when there is apprehension of imminent danger. The Supreme Court has held in Anuradha Bhasin (2020) that such orders must be temporary, reasoned, and subject to judicial review.</li>
<li><strong>FIR quashing under Section 482 CrPC / Section 528 BNSS:</strong> The High Court has inherent power under Section 482 CrPC (528 BNSS) to quash FIRs where proceedings are manifestly groundless or amount to abuse of process. However, FIRs for serious offences like rioting and attempt to murder are rarely quashed at the investigation stage. The question of whether mass arrests at a political demonstration amount to abuse of process is a recurring tension in Indian constitutional law.</li>
<li><strong>Anti-government protest policing:</strong> India has seen recurring controversies over the policing of anti-government demonstrations, from the anti-CAA protests (2019–20) to the farmers' protests (2020–21). Courts have emphasised that the right to peaceful protest is fundamental and police action must be proportionate; however, the line between lawful protest and public disorder is often contested in politically sensitive contexts.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Congress spokesperson Jairam Ramesh challenged the government's Q1 FY27 GDP growth figure of 7.8%, calling it a "Greatly Distorted Picture" (GDP). He cited several structural concerns: depressed private investment despite headline growth; consumer confidence at its lowest since the COVID-19 pandemic; a widening trade deficit with China; declining household savings rates; and a sharp increase in the wealth of India's five richest families while real wages have stagnated or declined. These critiques highlight the quality-of-growth debate — whether GDP growth driven primarily by government capital expenditure and financial sector performance translates into broad-based welfare improvements and private sector dynamism. The observations align with concerns raised by some economists about K-shaped recovery patterns in India's post-COVID growth trajectory.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>GDP measurement methodology (India):</strong> India's GDP is measured at constant (2011-12) prices using the expenditure method: GDP = Private Consumption + Government Consumption + Gross Fixed Capital Formation + Changes in Inventories + Net Exports. CSO/MoSPI releases advance, second advance, first revised, and final estimates. The base year was last revised to 2011-12 in 2015; a new base year (2022-23) revision is pending.</li>
<li><strong>Private investment cycle:</strong> Gross Fixed Capital Formation (GFCF) — a proxy for investment — has risen to 34.3% of GDP in Q1 FY27, driven largely by government capex. Private corporate investment, however, remains subdued due to excess capacity, global demand uncertainty, and high real interest rates. A private investment recovery is critical for job creation and sustainable growth.</li>
<li><strong>Household savings decline:</strong> India's household financial savings (net, as % of GDP) declined from 7.5% (2020-21) to approximately 5.1% (2022-23) — the lowest in decades — as households drew down savings post-COVID and took on debt. This reduced the pool available for productive investment and indicated stress in household balance sheets despite headline growth.</li>
<li><strong>India-China trade deficit:</strong> India's merchandise trade deficit with China has exceeded $80–100 billion annually in recent years, driven by imports of electronics, solar panels, chemicals, and active pharmaceutical ingredients. Despite calls for import substitution and PLI schemes targeting these sectors, the China trade deficit has proven difficult to reduce rapidly.</li>
<li><strong>K-shaped recovery:</strong> A post-recession recovery pattern in which upper-income groups and asset owners recover quickly while lower-income workers and informal sector participants stagnate or decline further. India's post-COVID recovery showed characteristics of K-shaped divergence: stock markets and corporate profits recovered sharply, while rural distress, informal sector employment, and real wages remained under pressure.</li>
</ul>
</div>
