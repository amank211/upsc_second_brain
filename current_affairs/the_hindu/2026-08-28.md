  Nepal flood toll hits 389; rescue work in top gear
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Geography → Glacial lake outburst floods; Himalayan hazards; climate change and mountain ecosystems
- GS Paper III → Disaster Management → Early warning systems; cross-border disaster response; HADR operations

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A catastrophic flash flood swept through Nepal's Rasuwa district on Wednesday, 27 August 2026, killing at least 389 people and leaving 910 missing — 517 of them foreign nationals from over 30 countries. The flood originated from what preliminary investigations suggest was a glacial collapse or ice-rock avalanche in Tibet's Himalayan range, which temporarily blocked the Bhotekoshi river; when the blockage failed, a massive surge of water and debris tore through settlements, destroying towns "in a span of a couple of hours" according to Nepal's Foreign Minister. With 19 bridges and 40 km of roads damaged, motorable access was cut off beyond Bidur in adjacent Nuwakot district, where Nepal's PM Balendra Shah reached by road. Satellite imagery subsequently revealed a new lake forming upstream, raising fears of a secondary flood. Nepal's Army, police and Armed Police Force were racing against time to rescue survivors from mountain villages, having saved over 800 people by Thursday evening. Among the missing are 178 Indian nationals — the highest of any foreign nationality — most of them Kailash Mansarovar Yatra pilgrims who were transiting through Rasuwagadhi to enter Tibet. India immediately dispatched HADR relief consignments and External Affairs Minister Jaishankar held a high-level review meeting. This disaster is the latest in a series of Himalayan GLOF events intensified by climate change.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF):</strong> A GLOF occurs when a dam containing a glacial lake fails — typically an ice or moraine dam — releasing millions of tonnes of water, ice and debris in a sudden surge. The Indian Himalayas alone have ~15,000 glaciers and ~7,500 glacial lakes, making the region among the most GLOF-prone in the world. The 2023 South Lhonak Lake disaster in Sikkim, which destroyed the 1,200 MW Teesta-III hydropower project, is the most cited recent precedent.</li>
<li><strong>Kailash Mansarovar Yatra (KMY):</strong> An annual pilgrimage to Mount Kailash and Lake Mansarovar in Tibet, China, patronised by the Indian government through the MEA since 1981. Two routes are used — Lipulekh Pass (Uttarakhand) and Nathu La (Sikkim), with the Rasuwagadhi route through Nepal being an alternate civilian route. Thousands of Indian pilgrims use Nepal's road network to transit into Tibet, making them vulnerable to Himalayan disasters.</li>
<li><strong>Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief (HADR):</strong> India's HADR doctrine under the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) mandates pre-positioning of relief materials and rapid deployment to neighbouring countries. India has deployed HADR in Nepal (2015 earthquake), Sri Lanka (2022 economic crisis) and Myanmar (2023 floods). The "neighbourhood first" policy governs the speed and scale of such responses.</li>
<li><strong>Bhotekoshi River:</strong> Originates in Tibet and flows into Nepal, joining the Sun Koshi. The corridor is known for repeated flood events including a major 2016 GLOF. It is a critical transit route for trade and pilgrimage between Nepal and Tibet. Hydropower projects (including the 111 MW Rasuwagadhi plant) in this corridor are routinely at risk from upstream glacial events.</li>
<li><strong>Western Disturbance and Himalayan flooding:</strong> Extratropical cyclonic systems from the Mediterranean (Western Disturbances) interact with the monsoon over the Himalayas to produce extreme precipitation. However, this flood appears linked to glacial collapse rather than rainfall alone, underscoring how glacial retreat driven by global warming is increasingly the primary risk factor for high-altitude flash floods in the Hindu Kush-Himalayan (HKH) arc.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar confirmed on Thursday that at least 290 Indian nationals remained "uncontactable" following the Nepal flash flood, and that India was working closely with the Nepalese side to locate them. A second consignment of 37.5 tonnes of HADR material — medicines, food packets and relief supplies — was handed over to Nepal's Science and Technology Minister Mahabir Pun. Groups of unaccounted Indians include 86 pilgrims from Tamil Nadu (two travel groups), 32 from West Bengal in Timure (Rasuwa district), 61 from diverse states via Richa Tours, and 33 from Kerala. Additionally, 63 Indian workers at the Trishuli-1 hydropower project and 21 Tamil Nadu pilgrims were rescued. Jaishankar chaired a review meeting with Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri, attended via video by India's ambassadors to Nepal (Naveen Shrivastava) and China (Vikram Doraiswamy). The absence of stable telecom in the disaster zone complicated rescue coordination. India's swift HADR deployments reflect the operational application of its "neighbourhood first" foreign policy in a crisis involving Indian nationals in a border country.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India-Nepal Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1950):</strong> This foundational bilateral treaty grants Indian and Nepali nationals equal rights in each other's territory including travel, residence and employment. The resulting open-border regime means thousands of Indians work in Nepal — including in hydropower projects like Trishuli-1 — complicating consular tracking during disasters.</li>
<li><strong>National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) Act, 2005:</strong> Established under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, the NDMA is India's apex body for disaster policy, planning and capacity-building. It coordinates the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) and India's HADR stockpiles for domestic and cross-border deployments. The NDMP provides a multi-hazard framework including for GLOF events.</li>
<li><strong>Kailash Mansarovar Yatra — MEA oversight:</strong> The MEA's Consular, Passport and Visa (CPV) Division administers this pilgrimage, coordinating with China for the Lipulekh and Nathu La routes, and with Nepal for the Rasuwagadhi route. The MEA maintains a register of yatra participants, which aided the identification of missing Indian nationals in this disaster.</li>
<li><strong>"Neighbourhood First" Policy:</strong> Formally articulated since 2014, this doctrine prioritises SAARC neighbours in India's foreign policy through connectivity, trade, and humanitarian assistance. Rapid HADR deployment to Nepal is one of its consistent operational expressions, repeated after the 2015 Nepal earthquake, 2017 Terai floods, and now the 2026 Rasuwa GLOF.</li>
<li><strong>Indian Ambassador to China's role:</strong> Ambassador Vikram Doraiswamy's attendance at the review meeting is significant because the flood appears to have originated from the Tibetan side of the border, and some missing pilgrims were last known to be crossing into Tibet. India's diplomatic channel through Beijing is therefore a parallel track for information gathering alongside the Kathmandu embassy.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Jharkhand Congress launches statewide protest opposing Mines and Minerals Amendment Act, 2026
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Federalism</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Centre-State relations; fiscal federalism; legislative competence over mines and minerals
- GS Paper III → Economy → Mining regulation; District Mineral Foundation; resource revenue sharing

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Indian National Congress launched a statewide agitation across Jharkhand against the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2026, accusing the Centre of unconstitutionally clawing back states' control over mineral royalties and District Mineral Foundation (DMF) funds. Congress leaders argued the amendment overrides the Supreme Court's landmark 2024 ruling — Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India — which affirmed that states have independent taxing power over minerals under Entry 49 of the State List, beyond the royalty stream prescribed by Parliament. Jharkhand, one of India's most mineral-rich states hosting coal, iron ore, bauxite and mica deposits, is heavily dependent on DMF contributions (up to 33% of royalties from miners) to fund health, education and livelihood programs in tribal and mining-affected areas under the PMKKY scheme. Protesters warned that the amendment's proposed changes to royalty calculations and revenue-sharing ratios would directly shrink the DMF corpus, gutting tribal welfare spending. The protest signals a broader federal flashpoint: mineral-bearing states — including Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Rajasthan — view mining revenue as a matter of constitutional fiscal autonomy rather than a discretionary Centre-State transfer.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>MMDR Act, 1957 and its Amendments:</strong> The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 is the parent legislation governing all non-coal mining in India. It vests power over Schedule I (major) minerals — iron ore, bauxite, manganese, copper, etc. — in the Centre under Entry 54 (Union List). The 2015 Amendment mandated transparent e-auction of mining leases (replacing discretionary allocation) and introduced the DMF and National Mineral Exploration Trust (NMET). Coal is separately governed by the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act, 1973.</li>
<li><strong>District Mineral Foundation (DMF):</strong> Established under Section 9B of the MMDR Act (inserted by the 2015 Amendment), DMFs are non-profit statutory bodies in all mineral-bearing districts funded by mandatory contributions from lessees — 33% of royalty (for pre-2015 leases) and 10% (for post-2015 auctioned leases). DMF funds are to be spent on Pradhan Mantri Khanij Kshetra Kalyan Yojana (PMKKY) priorities: drinking water, environment, healthcare, education, and livelihood restoration for mining-affected communities, especially Scheduled Tribe populations.</li>
<li><strong>MADA v. SAIL (2024) — 9-judge Constitution Bench:</strong> In Mineral Area Development Authority &amp; Anr. v. M/S Steel Authority of India &amp; Ors., the Supreme Court overruled India Cement Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu (1990) and held that states can levy taxes on lands used for mining under Entry 49 (State List: "taxes on lands and buildings") even after Parliament fixes royalty rates under Entry 54 (Union List). This confirmed the states' fiscal autonomy in the mining sector, which the 2026 Amendment is alleged to undermine.</li>
<li><strong>Entries 23, 54 and the 7th Schedule tension:</strong> Entry 23 (State List) gives states power to "regulation of mines and mineral development subject to the provisions of List I with respect to regulation and development under Union control." Entry 54 (Union List) grants Parliament power to regulate mines "to the extent to which such regulation and development under Union control is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest." The tension between these two entries has produced sustained litigation over royalty, cesses and levies imposed by states on miners.</li>
<li><strong>Jharkhand's mineral economy and tribal rights:</strong> Jharkhand accounts for roughly 40% of India's mineral production by value, hosting ~40% of national coal reserves, and large deposits of iron ore and copper. The state has a 28% Scheduled Tribe population (the highest tribal concentration of any large state), many of whom live in mining-affected zones. The Fifth Schedule of the Constitution protects Scheduled Area land rights; this, combined with PESA (Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas Act, 1996) and the Forest Rights Act, 2006, means any shrinkage in DMF funding strikes directly at constitutionally mandated tribal welfare obligations.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Shah hails water management in Gujarat under Modi; says no drought in 25 years
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Water Management</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Agriculture</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → State-level water management; best practices in government policy
- GS Paper III → Agriculture → Irrigation; drought management; inter-basin water transfers

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Union Home Minister Amit Shah credited Gujarat's water management model — built around the Sardar Sarovar Project, the SAUNI (Saurashtra Narmada Avataran Irrigation) Yojana, the Sujalam-Sufalam canal network, and a mass check-dam program — with eliminating droughts in the state for 25 consecutive years under BJP rule. Speaking at an event, Shah noted that the Narmada's waters had been channelled into Saurashtra and Kutch, historically the most drought-prone regions, via SAUNI's pipeline network linking the main canal to over 100 reservoirs across eight districts. He contrasted Gujarat's self-sufficiency with other states' water crises. The political framing of Gujarat as a governance template is significant: Gujarat's water-security model is frequently cited in policy debates on inter-state river linking, participatory groundwater management, and the role of large irrigation infrastructure in agricultural resilience. The check-dam movement — in which the state built over one lakh structures — transformed the water table in semi-arid districts and is studied as a model of community-government co-management of water resources.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>SAUNI Yojana (Saurashtra Narmada Avataran Irrigation):</strong> Launched by the Gujarat Government in 2012, this scheme uses four pipeline links to carry surplus Narmada water from the Sardar Sarovar main canal into 115 reservoirs across the eight drought-prone districts of Saurashtra. By delivering water to existing storage infrastructure rather than building new canals, SAUNI bypasses land acquisition hurdles and complements kharif season needs when Narmada surplus peaks. It is estimated to irrigate over 10 lakh hectares and has been cited as a model for states with rain-shadow agricultural zones.</li>
<li><strong>Sardar Sarovar Dam and Narmada Water Disputes Tribunal:</strong> The 138.68 m dam across the Narmada at Navagam, Gujarat, was built pursuant to the Narmada Water Disputes Tribunal Award (1979) which allocated 9 MAF to Gujarat, 18.25 MAF to Madhya Pradesh, 0.25 MAF to Maharashtra, and 0.5 MAF to Rajasthan. The project, whose full reservoir level was achieved in 2017, displaced over 2 lakh families (the Narmada Bachao Andolan-led rehabilitation controversy is a landmark environmental justice case). The inter-state water sharing and canal network built around Sardar Sarovar is the backbone of Gujarat's drought-proofing.</li>
<li><strong>Check-dam programme in Gujarat:</strong> Starting in 2000, Gujarat undertook a massive community-driven check-dam construction drive in partnership with NGOs (notably the Aga Khan Rural Support Programme) to slow monsoon runoff and recharge aquifers across semi-arid Saurashtra and North Gujarat. Over 1.5 lakh check dams were built. Studies showed groundwater levels rising by 3–10 metres in target zones, reducing dependence on erratic rainfall for drinking water and small-scale irrigation.</li>
<li><strong>Sujalam-Sufalam Canal:</strong> A 605 km canal in North Gujarat connecting the Rupen, Banas and Saraswati rivers, designed to divert flood waters during southwest monsoon into tanks and reservoirs. It serves as a "water highway" to recharge aquifers in the arid Banaskantha and Patan districts, complementing the Sardar Sarovar left-bank canal. Gujarat treats it as a model of intra-state water redistribution from water-surplus to water-deficit zones.</li>
<li><strong>National Water Policy, 2012:</strong> India's policy framework (currently under revision as National Water Policy 2020 Draft) advocates integrated water resource management, basin-level planning, demand-side conservation, and prioritisation of drinking water over irrigation in allocations. It recognises inter-basin transfers as a tool for redistributing water from surplus to deficit basins, underpinning the National Perspective Plan for inter-linking rivers — which Gujarat's Narmada-to-Saurashtra transfer operationally resembles at a state scale.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Constitutional provisions on untouchability; Article 17; social reform legislation
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Caste discrimination; SC/ST protection; political mobilisation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Congress party launched a political attack on the BJP over an incident in Haldwani, Uttarakhand, where BJP-aligned figures were reported to have defended a community ritual accused of enforcing caste-based discrimination or untouchability. Congress leaders alleged the incident reveals the party's "true casteist nature" despite its public stance of inclusive social policy. The controversy sits at the intersection of cultural practice, constitutional prohibition (Article 17 abolishes untouchability as a fundamental right), and electoral caste calculus in a state with significant Dalit and OBC populations. The BJP has historically challenged the "casteist" label, claiming its welfare programs — PM Awas Yojana, Ujjwala, Jan Dhan — are caste-neutral in delivery. The row demonstrates how incidents of alleged caste discrimination at the local level quickly become national political weaponry, with both parties seeking to claim the social justice mantle ahead of state assembly elections. For UPSC, the episode is an entry point to understanding the gap between constitutional ideals (Articles 14, 15, 17) and ground-level social reality in India's caste order.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 17 — Abolition of Untouchability:</strong> A unique Fundamental Right that operates against private conduct (not just state action), Article 17 declares untouchability abolished and its practice an offence punishable by law. Unlike other Fundamental Rights enforceable only against the state, Article 17 has horizontal effect — it binds private individuals. Its enabling legislation is the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (originally the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955).</li>
<li><strong>Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955:</strong> Criminalises enforcement of untouchability in all its forms — denial of access to temples, wells, shops, restaurants, roads, educational institutions, and hospitals on the basis of caste. Penalties range from fines to imprisonment. The Supreme Court has held that social boycotts of caste groups can amount to untouchability under this Act, expanding its reach beyond traditional "temple entry" contexts.</li>
<li><strong>SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 and Amendment 2015:</strong> Goes beyond the civil rights framework to criminalise a broader set of offences against Scheduled Castes and Tribes — from forced consumption of inedible matter and stripping clothes, to preventing access to irrigation water and imposing social boycotts. The 2015 Amendment added new offences, made the list of crimes non-bailable by default, and inserted provisions against "intentional insult or intimidation." A special court mechanism and state monitoring committees are mandated.</li>
<li><strong>Caste mobilisation in Uttarakhand:</strong> Uttarakhand has a Scheduled Caste population of approximately 19% (highest proportion among hill states), concentrated in the Kumaon division where Haldwani is located. OBC communities (Kumhar, Gurjar, Lodh) form another significant bloc. Electoral outcomes in the state have repeatedly turned on caste arithmetic, with competing parties courting both dominant forward castes (Brahmin, Rajput — together ~45%) and marginalised groups. Congress's "casteist BJP" charge is a direct bid to consolidate Dalit-OBC votes.</li>
<li><strong>NCSC — National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338):</strong> A constitutional body set up under Article 338, the NCSC investigates complaints of atrocities, monitors safeguards under the Constitution and laws, and submits annual reports to Parliament. It has quasi-judicial powers to summon witnesses and demand documents. Whenever untouchability or caste discrimination incidents attract national attention, the NCSC has the mandate to take suo motu cognisance — making it directly relevant to this news item's institutional background.</li>
</ul>
</div>

  Alleged use of children in poll campaign not a ground to set aside victory, T.N. CM tells Madras HC
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Representation of the People Act, 1951; corrupt practices; grounds for voiding elections; election petitions

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Tamil Nadu Chief Minister M.K. Stalin's counsel argued before the Madras High Court that deploying children in election campaigns — even if proved — does not constitute a "corrupt practice" under the Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951, and cannot legally void an election result. A petitioner had challenged Stalin's election victory on grounds that children were used in political rallies, allegedly in violation of child labour regulations. The defence's argument rests on the settled legal principle that grounds for annulling an election under Sections 100 and 123 of the RPA are exhaustively enumerated — the statute makes no cross-reference to the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act — and courts have consistently refused to read fresh grounds into what is a statutory list. The case illustrates the strict constructionist approach Indian courts take to election petitions and the tension between child protection law and electoral jurisprudence, both relevant areas for UPSC GS II.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Representation of the People Act, 1951 — Section 100:</strong> This section exhaustively lists the grounds on which a High Court may declare an election void, including disqualification of the returned candidate, improper acceptance/rejection of nomination papers, failure to comply with election rules, and corrupt practice by or with the consent of the candidate. The list is closed — courts cannot add new grounds not specified in the statute.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Articles 81 and 82 — Parliamentary representation and delimitation:</strong> Article 81 sets the maximum composition of the Lok Sabha at 550 elected members (530 from states, 20 from UTs) and prescribes that the number of seats from each state shall be allocated in proportion to its population. Article 82 mandates that after every decennial census, the allocation of Lok Sabha seats and the division of each state into parliamentary constituencies shall be readjusted by an authority designated by Parliament — i.e., the Delimitation Commission. <li><strong>42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976:</strong> Inserted a proviso to Articles 81 and 82 freezing the total number of Lok Sabha seats at 1971 census figures (then 545) until the first census after 2000. This freeze created the current imbalance: southern states' populations grew slower but their seat counts are calculated on 1971 figures, so they are technically over-represented relative to their current population.</li>
<li><strong>84th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001:</strong> Extended the freeze on the total number of Lok Sabha and State Legislature seats (using 1971 figures) until the first census taken after 2026. Delimitation of constituency boundaries within states was permitted under the 2002 Delimitation Commission, but no reallocation of seats between states was done. The 2026 deadline means the 2021 Census (already delayed due to COVID-19 and now further deferred) will trigger the first seat-reallocation delimitation since 1971.</li>
<li><strong>106th Constitutional Amendment — Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (2023):</strong> Inserted new Articles 330A, 332A, and 334A reserving one-third of Lok Sabha seats, State Assembly seats, and Delhi assembly seats for women. This creates a constitutional dependency: no women's reservation until delimitation, and no delimitation until the census is conducted and notified. <li><strong>Delimitation Commission — statutory framework:</strong> The Delimitation Commission Act, 2002 is the current statute under which delimitation commissions are constituted. The Commission (chaired by a retired Supreme Court judge, with the Chief Election Commissioner and state Election Commissioners as ex-officio members) has final, non-justiciable authority on constituency boundaries — its orders cannot be questioned in any court. Past commissions operated in 1952, 1963, 1973, and 2002 (only boundaries, not seats). The upcoming commission will have to juggle seat reallocation between states (political dynamite) and boundary redrawing (administrative exercise) simultaneously.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Constitutional basis of reservation — Articles 15 and 16:</strong> Article 15(4) allows the state to make special provisions for the advancement of "socially and educationally backward classes" or SC/STs. <li><strong>Indra Sawhney v. <div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 — cosmetics regulation:</strong> This central legislation governs the import, manufacture, distribution, and sale of drugs and cosmetics in India. A public health commentary argues that India's disease surveillance and clinical response to Salmonella must expand beyond its focus on Salmonella Typhi (which causes typhoid, the disease attracting the most public health investment) to encompass non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) — particularly S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis — which are major zoonotic pathogens responsible for invasive infections in children and immunocompromised patients. Unlike S. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Salmonella — taxonomy and disease burden:</strong> Salmonella is a genus of Gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae with over 2,600 serovars. S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi (A, B, C) cause enteric (typhoid) fever — a systemic disease in humans only. Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) serovars — S. Typhimurium, S. Enteritidis, S. Heidelberg — cause self-limiting gastroenteritis in most people but invasive bloodstream infections (iNTS) in young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised patients. India notifies approximately 1-5 million typhoid cases annually but NTS surveillance is far weaker — the actual NTS disease burden is probably substantially higher than reported.</li>
<li><strong>One Health framework:</strong> A collaborative approach (formalized by WHO-FAO-WOAH Tripartite) recognising that ~60% of known infectious diseases are zoonotic, and that human, animal, and ecosystem health are interdependent. India's National One Health Mission was approved by the Union Cabinet in February 2022, with a National One Health Advisory Committee chaired by the Principal Scientific Adviser, and includes disease surveillance, AMR containment, and pandemic preparedness components.</li>
<li><strong>NAP-AMR — India's National Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance:</strong> Launched in 2017 (2017-2021, extended to 2025) under a One Health framework, NAP-AMR covers actions in human medicine (rational antibiotic use, hospital infection control), veterinary and agriculture (antibiotic stewardship in animal husbandry), food production (FSSAI AMR standards), environment (antibiotic contamination of water bodies from pharmaceutical effluent), and surveillance (ICMR-AMR network). A major gap: India still permits over-the-counter purchase of critical antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins) in veterinary shops without prescription.</li>
<li><strong>Typhoid Conjugate Vaccine (TCV):</strong> A next-generation typhoid vaccine using a polysaccharide conjugated to a protein carrier (Vi-TCV), providing stronger and longer-lasting immunity than the older Vi polysaccharide vaccine, and usable in infants from 6 months of age. WHO recommends TCV for children in typhoid-endemic countries. India launched a national TCV campaign (Typbar-TCV, produced by Bharat Biotech) in 2022 under the Universal Immunisation Programme — targeting children aged 9-15 months in priority districts. TCV addresses S. Typhi only; it has no effect on NTS.</li>
<li><strong>FSSAI — Salmonella standards in food:</strong> The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (under FSSAI Act, 2006) sets microbiological standards for processed food. FSSAI mandates Salmonella-free status (zero tolerance) for ready-to-eat poultry products. However, informal wet markets (where most Indian consumers buy fresh poultry) operate outside FSSAI's regular inspection net; Salmonella contamination rates in fresh poultry at informal markets have been documented at 25-60% in published studies. FSSAI's 2020 Hygiene Ratings scheme for eateries partially addresses the gap but covers restaurants, not live-bird markets.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Building Code (NBC) 2016 — hospital fire safety:</strong> Published by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), the NBC is a model code adopted by states through their building by-laws. Several states have notified minimum standards under the Act that include fire NOC (No Objection Certificate) as a licensing condition, but inspection frequency and fire safety renewal requirements vary widely. The Parley format presents two opposed positions on English's status in India. The case for treating English as "foreign" rests on its colonial origin, its role in reproducing elite privilege (urban private-school children access opportunities denied to rural mother-tongue learners), and the constitutional directive (Articles 343-351) to promote Hindi and regional languages as official and judicial languages. Against this, the counter-position argues that English is now indigenised — "Indianised English" or Hinglish is a distinct linguistic register — and serves a democratising function particularly for Dalits and OBCs, for whom proficiency in English bypasses caste-embedded Brahminical gatekeeping of classical Sanskrit-mediated knowledge systems. B.R. Ambedkar explicitly endorsed English as a liberating language. NEP 2020 navigates this tension by recommending mother-tongue-based instruction up to Grade 5 while retaining English as part of the three-language formula — a pragmatic compromise that satisfies neither the pro-Hindi advocates nor the pro-English modernists fully. The piece is relevant to UPSC Essay paper (themes: language and nationhood, cultural identity, social justice) and GS I/II (linguistic diversity, language policy).

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Constitutional provisions on language (Part XVII, Articles 343-351):</strong> Article 343 makes Hindi (Devanagari script) the official language of the Union; Article 344 mandates a Language Commission to promote Hindi's progressive use. The Official Languages Act, 1963 continued English as a co-official language for all official purposes of the Union, effectively deferring the all-Hindi transition indefinitely. Article 345 allows state legislatures to adopt any regional language as official state language. Article 350A mandates adequate facilities for instruction in mother tongue at primary stage for linguistic minority children.</li>
<li><strong>Three-language formula — history and NEP 2020:</strong> First proposed by the Kothari Commission (1964-66), formalised in the National Policy on Education (NPE) 1968. In non-Hindi states: students learn regional language (L1), Hindi (L2), and English (L3). In Hindi-speaking states: students learn Hindi (L1), English (L2), and another Indian language (L3 — typically a South Indian language, though compliance has been poor). NEP 2020 reaffirms the formula while emphasising mother-tongue instruction in early grades, and recommending that the L3 need not be English — it can be any modern Indian language, classical language, or a foreign language.</li>
<li><strong>Ambedkar and English — Dalit epistemology:</strong> B.R. In his view, replacing English with Sanskrit or Hindi would reinforce Brahminic cultural hegemony over knowledge production. This position is continued today by scholars like Kancha Ilaiah (Bahujan Samaj's English champion) and influences policy debates on medium of instruction for government schools serving SC/ST populations.</li>
<li><strong>English and social mobility — empirical evidence:</strong> Multiple economic studies (World Bank India studies, NCAER surveys) have documented that English proficiency in India is associated with substantially higher earnings — a 30-40% wage premium over otherwise comparable non-English-proficient workers in the formal economy. This premium reflects both genuine skill (English enables access to global job markets and formal corporate employers) and a signalling function (English-medium education signals elite social capital). The wage premium creates powerful demand for English-medium instruction from parents across income strata — including from poor and rural households.</li>
<li><strong>India's linguistic diversity — 8th Schedule and Census data:</strong> India's 8th Schedule to the Constitution recognises 22 languages. The 2011 Census documented 121 mother tongues with more than 10,000 speakers, and 19,569 distinct languages or dialects as mother tongues. India has no single "common language" spoken natively by a majority: Hindi is the largest mother tongue group (~52% of population in the broadest definition), but "Hindi" aggregates multiple distinct languages (Bhojpuri, Awadhi, Maithili, etc.). This diversity is why the three-language formula — rather than a monolingual solution — has been the consensus compromise since 1968.</li>
</ul>

The Text & Context feature examines India's structural energy security vulnerability and whether a comprehensive "strategic fuel system" — covering crude oil, LNG, and LPG reserves — is feasible. India's existing Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) holds approximately 5.33 million metric tonnes (MMT) of crude oil at three underground rock cavern facilities (Padur and Mangaluru in Karnataka, Vishakhapatnam in AP), equivalent to roughly 9.5 days of consumption — far below the IEA's recommended 90 days. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India's Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) — Phase 1:</strong> Three underground rock caverns: Vishakhapatnam (1.33 MMT), Mangaluru (1.5 MMT), Padur (2.5 MMT) — total 5.33 MMT of crude oil, managed by Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Limited (ISPRL), a wholly owned subsidiary of HPCL. <li><strong>LNG storage technology constraints:</strong> Natural gas is liquefied at -162°C for sea transport (LNG); regasification terminals convert it back to gas. <li><strong>LPG and PMUY — household energy security:</strong> India imports approximately 50% of its LPG (propane/butane mixture) primarily from Saudi Arabia, UAE and the US. <li><strong>Hydrocarbon Vision 2047 and energy independence:</strong> India's long-term energy policy frameworks (Hydrocarbon Vision 2025, updated to 2030 and beyond) target reducing import dependence by increasing domestic crude production, scaling renewable energy (reducing power sector oil use), and building a domestic petrochemical industry to add value to imported crude. The Strategic Fuel Reserve expansion fits within this architecture — it buys time for diversified supply during disruptions without reducing structural import dependence, which requires decades of energy transition.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Subhash Chandra, founder and promoter of Zee Entertainment Enterprises and the broader Essel Group, was set to pay ₹6.5 crore under a personal insolvency resolution plan — a fraction of the estimated ₹22,000+ crore owed by Essel Group companies to lenders (including IL&FS, Yes Bank, and IDBI Bank). The case proceeds under Part III of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016, which covers insolvency for individuals and unlimited liability partnership firms — distinct from the corporate insolvency (Part II/CIRP) track that handles company-level debt. The dramatic disparity between the debt admitted and the settlement amount highlights a structural criticism: India's personal insolvency framework under IBC, unlike the UK or US bankruptcy codes, does not compel full asset disclosure and liquidation of all promoter assets before allowing a minimal repayment plan. Creditors (who must vote on the repayment plan) face the choice of accepting a small payment now or waiting for contested litigation. The Essel Group's personal insolvency case is significant because it tests whether the IBC's personal insolvency provisions meaningfully deter reckless borrowing by promoters who enjoy limited liability through their corporate vehicles.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 — structure:</strong> IBC consolidates laws relating to reorganisation and insolvency resolution of corporate persons, partnership firms, and individuals. Part I: preliminary; Part II: Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) and liquidation for companies/LLPs — adjudicated by NCLT; Part III: Insolvency Resolution Process for Individuals and Partnership Firms — adjudicated by Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT); Part IV: regulation of IPs (insolvency professionals) and IPAs (agencies) by IBBI (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India).</li>
<li><strong>DRT vs. NCLT — institutional distinction:</strong> Corporate insolvency (Part II) under IBC is handled by the NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal), with appeals to NCLAT. Personal insolvency (Part III) is handled by the DRT (Debt Recovery Tribunal), with appeals to DRAT (Debt Recovery Appellate Tribunal). The DRT was originally created under the Recovery of Debts Due to Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 1993, primarily for loan recovery; the IBC expanded its mandate to personal insolvency adjudication. India has 39 DRTs across major cities.</li>
<li><strong>Personal insolvency — repayment plan mechanism:</strong> Under Part III of IBC, an individual debtor can propose a repayment plan (for debtors below a threshold) or apply for insolvency resolution. An insolvency professional facilitates creditor negotiations. The repayment plan must be approved by a vote of creditors representing at least 75% of the total admitted debt value. A plan that pays significantly less than the admitted debt can still be approved if creditors conclude it offers more than liquidation of the individual's declared assets. This mechanism creates the incentive for large promoters to conceal assets or claim limited personal net worth.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
As "agentic AI" — AI systems capable of taking autonomous multi-step actions (booking tickets, executing code, sending emails, making purchases) on behalf of users without per-action human approval — proliferates in enterprise software, the cyber insurance industry is confronting a new class of liability scenarios for which traditional policy frameworks were not designed. Insurers face three novel challenges: (a) determining who is liable when an AI agent causes harm — the enterprise deploying the agent, the platform providing the AI model, or the model developer; (b) pricing "prompt injection" risk, where malicious content in a webpage or email hijacks an agent's instructions to execute unauthorised actions; and (c) assessing systemic risk from the concentration of millions of enterprise agents on a small number of foundation models — meaning a model-level vulnerability could trigger simultaneous losses across all policyholders, a correlated failure unlike traditional targeted cyber attacks. The article cites emerging policy language: exclusions for "autonomous AI decisions" (analogous to war exclusions), coverage sub-limits for AI-agent-caused financial loss, and requirements for "human-in-the-loop" checkpoints as a condition of full coverage. For UPSC, this article connects GS III (AI governance, cybersecurity) with GS II (regulatory frameworks, liability law) in the context of India's DPDP Act, IT Act amendments, and evolving CERT-In guidance on AI risk.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Agentic AI — definition and risk profile:</strong> Agentic AI systems are Large Language Model (LLM)-powered autonomous agents that can decompose a complex goal into sub-tasks, select and use tools (browser, code interpreter, API calls, file systems), and execute actions iteratively without human approval at each step. Examples include Claude Computer Use, AutoGPT, and enterprise "copilots." Key risks: (a) prompt injection — malicious text in observed content (a webpage, a PDF, an email) that overrides the agent's instructions; (b) hallucination-driven errors — the agent confidently executes a wrong action; (c) scope creep — the agent executes actions beyond the intended scope, e.g. sending an email it was not instructed to.</li>
<li><strong>Cyber insurance market — growth and emerging risks:</strong> Global cyber insurance premiums grew from ~$7 billion (2020) to ~$14 billion (2023) and are projected to reach $50 billion by 2030 (Munich Re estimate). India's domestic cyber insurance market is early-stage (estimated ₹1,500-2,000 crore premiums in 2024-25); IRDAI has published guidelines on cyber insurance products. The largest claim drivers globally include ransomware, business email compromise (BEC), and data breaches. Agentic AI adds new claim categories — financial fraud, data exfiltration, and operational disruption triggered by compromised agents.</li>
<li><strong>AI liability — legal gap in India:</strong> India's IT Act, 2000, the DPDP Act, 2023, and tort law under the Indian Contract Act and IPC do not explicitly assign liability for autonomous AI system decisions. There is no "producer liability" framework for AI analogous to product liability under the Consumer Protection Act (which covers "goods" and "services" but has not been tested for AI-caused harm). India's AI governance framework — currently a series of MEITY advisories — does not address civil liability for AI errors. The EU AI Act (2024) is the first comprehensive liability framework, and India's National AI Mission may eventually address this gap.</li>
<li><strong>CERT-In — India's cybersecurity response:</strong> India's Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In), under the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY), functions under the IT Act, 2000 (Section 70B). A 2022 CERT-In directive mandated that all entities (including cloud service providers and virtual asset service providers) report cybersecurity incidents within 6 hours, maintain logs for 180 days, and use NTP servers for timestamping. Any AI-agent-caused cyber incident in India would fall under CERT-In's reporting requirements. CERT-In is also developing advisories on AI-specific threats including adversarial attacks, model theft, and prompt injection.</li>
<li><strong>Systemic / correlated risk in AI — insurance challenge:</strong> Traditional cyber risk assumes that each company is independently targeted — correlation between insured losses is low. AI introduces a new systemic dimension: if a critical vulnerability is found in GPT-4, Claude 3, or any dominant foundation model, all enterprises running agents on that model could simultaneously suffer losses. Insurance models (which depend on uncorrelated loss distributions to price premiums) are not designed for this kind of correlated systemic risk — the same challenge that emerged for pandemic coverage post-COVID, and for ransomware campaigns targeting widely used software (e.g., MOVEit, Log4Shell).</li>
</ul>
</div>

The US State Department designated "Palestine Action" — a UK-based activist organisation that conducts direct-action protests against defence companies supplying arms to Israel (including spray-painting facilities and blockading factory entrances) — as a Foreign Terrorist Organisation (FTO) under the Immigration and Nationality Act. For India, the episode is relevant as an example of how the US "terrorism" designation tool — with significant extraterritorial reach via financial system coercive power (SWIFT, OFAC) — can be used for political purposes, raising questions about the definition and governance of terrorism under international law.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>US Foreign Terrorist Organisation (FTO) designation — legal framework:</strong> Under Section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, the Secretary of State may designate a foreign organisation as an FTO if it engages in terrorism or terrorist activity, or has the capability and intent to do so. Consequences: US financial institutions must freeze FTO assets; it is a federal crime for US persons to provide "material support" to an FTO (18 USC §2339B); FTO members are inadmissible to the US. <li><strong>India's position on Palestine — two-state solution:</strong> India has historically supported a two-state solution to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict (an independent, viable Palestine state coexisting with Israel), a position maintained since Nehru's era. India votes with the UNGA majority on Palestinian rights resolutions. Since 2017, India has also upgraded its strategic relationship with Israel (PM Modi's visit to Israel — the first by an Indian PM), creating a "multi-vector" approach: supporting Palestinian statehood diplomatically while deepening defence, technology and agricultural ties with Israel. India abstained on the 2023 UNGA resolution calling for a humanitarian ceasefire in Gaza.</li>
<li><strong>UN Security Council and Gaza — India's role:</strong> India became a non-permanent member of the UNSC in 2021-22 and participated in multiple Gaza-related resolutions debates. India generally avoids vetoing or co-sponsoring Gaza resolutions, preferring abstentions on contentious ones while supporting humanitarian access resolutions. India's position is to call for "restraint by all parties," humanitarian corridors, and revival of a political process toward two states — a carefully balanced formulation that avoids taking sides while maintaining India's traditional Palestinian solidarity.</li>
<li><strong>OFAC and US extraterritorial sanctions reach:</strong> The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) within the US Treasury administers US sanctions programmes — including the SDN (Specially Designated Nationals) list, which complements FTO designations. Non-US financial institutions that transact with designated entities risk secondary sanctions (losing access to the US financial system). This extraterritorial reach — operating through the dollar-denominated global financial system — gives US designations a coercive power far beyond US territory, raising sovereignty concerns from the EU, India and other major powers.</li>
<li><strong>Terrorism — definition under international law:</strong> There is no universally agreed definition of terrorism in international law. The UN's Comprehensive Convention on International Terrorism (CCIT) has been under negotiation since 1996 but remains unadopted due to disagreements over whether national liberation movements and state terrorism are covered. India has been a vocal advocate for adopting the CCIT, particularly to universalise the criminalisation of cross-border terrorism. The Palestine Action case highlights the definitional dispute — whether property-damage civil disobedience meets the threshold of "terrorism" — at the heart of the unresolved CCIT negotiations.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Human Rights Watch condemned the Taliban government in Afghanistan for enacting a new law explicitly criminalising public protest and demonstrations — the latest in a series of decrees that have dismantled civil liberties since August 2021. The Taliban's Vice and Virtue Ministry (which replaced the Women's Affairs Ministry) has enforced a comprehensive rollback of women's rights: women are banned from appearing in public without a male guardian (mahram), their voices may not be heard in public (including on radio), girls' education above Grade 6 is prohibited, and women are excluded from government employment. For India, Afghanistan's trajectory under the Taliban is directly relevant: Afghanistan shares a border with Pakistan (which India views as a proxy theatre), is a source of drug trafficking routes (through which Afghan heroin transits to India via Pakistan), and its humanitarian crisis generates regional refugee flows.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Taliban takeover — August 2021 and aftermath:</strong> Following the US withdrawal from Afghanistan (completed 31 August 2021), the Taliban rapidly seized control of all major cities and Kabul. No UN member state has recognised the Taliban government as the legitimate government of Afghanistan. The UN maintains a Special Mission (UNAMA) in Kabul for humanitarian coordination. The Taliban-era government (referred to by the UN and most states as the "de facto authorities") has systematically dismantled the Republic's constitution, legal system, and rights frameworks established between 2001 and 2021.</li>
<li><strong>India-Afghanistan relations — historical context:</strong> India invested approximately $3 billion in Afghanistan's development between 2001 and 2021 — the Salma Dam (renamed "Afghanistan-India Friendship Dam"), the Afghan Parliament building, highways, hospitals, and scholarships for 18,000 Afghan students. India evacuated its embassy and consulate staff in 2021. As of 2026, India maintains a low-profile presence through a "technical team" in Kabul but has not formally recognised the Taliban. India's concerns: Afghanistan as a safe haven for Pakistan-based terrorist groups (LeT, JeM training infrastructure in Afghanistan); drug trafficking; and regional stability.</li>
<li><strong>Women's rights under the Taliban — international law dimension:</strong> The UN's Independent International Human Rights Experts designated the Taliban's systematic exclusion of women from public life as "gender apartheid" — a term paralleling "apartheid" under international law but not yet a formalised crime under the Rome Statute. A proposal to add "gender apartheid" to the ICC's jurisdiction is under discussion at the ICJ and ICC preparatory bodies. The comprehensive ban on women's education, employment and public presence violates the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), to which Afghanistan is a signatory.</li>
<li><strong>Afghanistan as a narcotics source:</strong> Afghanistan has historically produced 70-90% of the world's illicit opium supply (UNODC data). Post-2021, the Taliban initially claimed to ban poppy cultivation; enforcement has been selective. Afghan heroin routes to India transit primarily through Pakistan (Golden Crescent → Pakistan → India's northwest border), with seizures of Afghan heroin regularly reported by India's Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB). The drug trade generates revenue for both Pakistani narco-networks and some Taliban factions.</li>
<li><strong>SCO and Afghanistan:</strong> Afghanistan held observer status in the SCO from 2012. Following the Taliban takeover, Afghanistan's SCO engagement is in abeyance — it is neither expelled nor active. India has used SCO forums to raise concerns about Afghanistan-based terrorism and drug trafficking, pressing for SCO's RATS (Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure) mechanism to focus on the Afghan theatre. The Taliban's non-recognition makes formal SCO engagement with Afghanistan diplomatically complex for all members.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Nepal flash flood highlights growing glacier risks in warming Himalayas
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Disaster Management</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Geography → Himalayan glaciology; climate change; glacial lakes; water towers of Asia
- GS Paper III → Disaster Management → GLOF risk assessment; transboundary disaster coordination; climate adaptation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A world-pages feature synthesises the scientific consensus on accelerating glacial lake formation and outburst flood risk in the Hindu Kush-Himalayan (HKH) region in the context of the Nepal GLOF disaster. Global warming is disproportionately intense at high altitudes (amplification factor ~1.5-2× the global mean temperature rise), causing Himalayan glaciers to retreat at unprecedented rates: the HKH region has lost 40% of its glacier area in the 20th century. As glaciers retreat, they leave behind depressions that fill with meltwater to form glacial lakes — the number of large glacial lakes (>0.2 km²) in the Hindu Kush, Karakoram, Himalayas (HKH) region increased by 47% between 1990 and 2018 (ICIMOD study). These lakes are retained by unstable moraine dams — deposits of glacial till — that can fail catastrophically under slope overloading, earthquake triggering, or avalanche impact. ICIMOD's 2023 risk atlas identified 7,500+ glacial lakes in the HKH, of which ~200 are classified as "potentially dangerous." India, China, Nepal, Pakistan and Bhutan all share responsibility for monitoring and managing trans-boundary glacial lake hazards — a governance challenge that no current regional framework fully addresses.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>HKH as the "Third Pole" and water tower:</strong> The Hindu Kush-Himalayan region — sometimes called the "Third Pole" because it holds the largest volume of freshwater ice outside the Arctic and Antarctic — supplies water to 10 major Asian river systems (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra, Yangtze, Yellow, Mekong, Irrawaddy, Salween, Amu Darya, Syr Darya) and is the water source for ~1.9 billion people. Glacial retreat and changes in seasonal snow and ice storage patterns will permanently alter river flow regimes — initially increasing peak flow (more melt), then decreasing baseflow as glaciers are exhausted.</li>
<li><strong>Altitude amplification of warming:</strong> The IPCC AR6 and ICIMOD's HIMAP report confirm that high-altitude regions (above 3,000 m) are warming at rates 1.5-2× the global mean average. The Tibetan Plateau has warmed by ~0.4°C per decade since the 1980s — nearly double the global rate. This amplified warming accelerates both glacier mass loss and permafrost thaw — two primary drivers of slope instability and glacial lake formation in the HKH region.</li>
<li><strong>ICIMOD — International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development:</strong> A regional knowledge centre headquartered in Kathmandu, Nepal, with eight member countries (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, China, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan). ICIMOD conducts scientific monitoring of HKH glaciers, glacial lakes, and cryospheric changes; produces the GLOF risk atlas; and supports national government capacity in early warning systems. India is a member-country and ICIMOD's research directly informs India's NDMA and Ministry of Jal Shakti's GLOF risk assessments.</li>
<li><strong>Transboundary GLOF governance gap:</strong> Most potentially dangerous glacial lakes in the HKH span international borders: a lake in Tibet (China) can outburst and flood Nepal or India; a Bhutanese glacier can flood Assam. There is no treaty-level framework for transboundary GLOF risk management, information sharing, or joint early warning. Existing bilateral water treaties (India-Pakistan Indus Waters Treaty 1960; India-Nepal Kosi Agreement 1954; India-Bangladesh Ganga Treaty 1996) cover water sharing but not hazard management. The Nepal-China "Himalayan Early Warning System" is the only bilateral technical arrangement specifically targeting GLOF early warning.</li>
<li><strong>Paris Agreement and Himalayan glaciers:</strong> The Paris Agreement's 1.5°C temperature target is particularly critical for the HKH — ICIMOD projects that even at 1.5°C, one-third of HKH glaciers will be lost by 2100; at 4°C, two-thirds. India's NDC (Nationally Determined Contribution) under the Paris Agreement includes: 500 GW renewable energy by 2030, 50% non-fossil power capacity, net zero by 2070. India's Himalayan states' vulnerability to glacial loss is a powerful domestic argument for ambitious climate action — glacial retreat is not an abstract future risk but a present-tense disaster driver, as the Nepal GLOF demonstrates.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Ukrainian President Zelenskyy, during a visit to Washington D.C., secured bipartisan backing from the US Senate for the "Graham Sanctions Act" — legislation that would impose sweeping secondary sanctions (tariffs up to 500% on goods from countries purchasing Russian energy or defence equipment) on any country deemed insufficiently supportive of Ukraine's defence effort. The Senate vote was non-binding (a "sense of the Senate" resolution), and the Trump White House indicated it would not rush to sign the legislation, preferring to retain sanctions as a diplomatic lever rather than impose them automatically. For India, this legislation is directly relevant: India is one of the world's largest buyers of Russian crude oil and has been Russia's most important energy market since the G7 sanctions regime, and is also the recipient of Russian S-400 SAMs which triggered CAATSA consideration. If Graham Act-style secondary sanctions were enacted and signed into law, India would be in scope — making the legislation a key external pressure point on India's strategic autonomy doctrine.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Graham Sanctions Act — the legislative proposal:</strong> Bipartisan legislation (Senators Lindsey Graham and others) proposing automatic secondary sanctions (500% tariff on goods from non-compliant countries) on countries that: (a) purchase Russian oil, gas, or LNG above a price cap threshold; (b) provide military equipment to Russia; (c) refuse to join coordinated export controls against Russia. If enacted, it would dramatically expand the extraterritorial reach of US sanctions beyond the existing CAATSA and OFAC frameworks. The legislation has passed in various forms in committee but has not become law as of 2026.</li>
<li><strong>CAATSA — India's existing exposure:</strong> The Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act (CAATSA, 2017) empowers the US President to impose sanctions on any entity making "significant transactions" with Russia's defence, intelligence, or energy sectors. India's purchase of five S-400 Triumf air defence systems from Russia (contract: 2018; delivery: 2021-25) is explicitly a CAATSA-triggering transaction. The US has not imposed CAATSA sanctions on India, citing the "India exception" under the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) — a discretionary presidential waiver provision. The Graham Act would remove presidential discretion, making sanctions automatic.</li>
<li><strong>India-Russia energy relationship — strategic rationale:</strong> India's shift to Russian crude imports (now ~35% of total imports by volume) reflects: (a) cost savings — $8-15 billion per year in import bill savings compared to buying non-sanctioned crude at spot prices; (b) energy security diversification — adding Russia as a supplier reduces dependence on any single Middle East or African source; (c) reciprocal accommodation — India maintains the option of importing from Russia as leverage in its diplomatic relationship with both Russia and the West. India frames this as "strategic autonomy" — a constitutional value articulated in India's 1946 Objectives Resolution and practised consistently since Non-Alignment.</li>
<li><strong>India's strategic autonomy doctrine:</strong> India's foreign policy has historically been characterised by "strategic autonomy" — the right to make independent foreign and security decisions without being bound to any bloc or alliance. Operationally: India is not a member of any military alliance; abstains on UNGA resolutions that would require taking sides in great-power conflicts; maintains simultaneous strategic partnerships with the US (Quad, BECA, GSOMIA, COMCASA defence agreements), Russia (S-400, BrahMos, ongoing defence procurement), and China (BRICS, SCO, bilateral trade). The Graham Act directly tests this doctrine by threatening economic consequences for India's Russia-energy relationship.</li>
<li><strong>G7 Russian oil price cap — India's non-participation:</strong> The G7 (and EU, Australia) imposed a $60/barrel price cap on Russian seaborne crude exports in December 2022, prohibiting western shipping, insurance, and financial services from facilitating Russia crude transactions above the cap. India, China, Turkey and other major Russian energy importers did not join the cap. India has purchased Russian crude at prices ranging from $55 to $75/barrel — sometimes below, sometimes above the cap — using non-western insurance providers (primarily India's GIC Re) and non-western tanker fleets. This has made India the most visible "circumventer" of the G7 price cap regime, drawing US and EU diplomatic pressure.</li>
</ul>
</div>
