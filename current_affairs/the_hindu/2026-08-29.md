<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Nepal Flood Death Toll Rises to 579; Hundreds Still Missing
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Geography → Geophysical phenomena; glacial events; Himalayan river systems; natural disasters
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Nepal bilateral relations; neighbourhood-first policy
- GS Paper III → Disaster Management → GLOF risk; Sendai Framework; India's disaster response mechanisms

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Catastrophic monsoon floods and flash floods triggered by a glacial lake outburst in Nepal's Himalayan belt have killed at least 579 people, with hundreds more still unaccounted for as of 29 August 2026. The flooding, among the worst in Nepal's recorded history, has inundated entire river valleys, destroyed infrastructure, and displaced tens of thousands. India has responded swiftly under its neighbourhood-first policy: the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) teams have been mobilised near the border, the Indian Embassy in Kathmandu has activated a helpline for stranded Indian nationals, and consular teams are coordinating with Nepalese authorities. The disaster has renewed focus on Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs) as a compounding risk in the Hindu Kush-Himalaya system, where accelerated glacial melt due to climate change is creating new, unstable proglacial lakes. For UPSC, this event cuts across disaster management frameworks, India-Nepal bilateral ties (including the Treaty of Peace and Friendship, 1950), and India's obligations under the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030).
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs):</strong> GLOFs occur when the natural dam holding a glacial lake — typically ice, moraine, or debris — fails suddenly, releasing vast volumes of meltwater downstream. The Hindu Kush-Himalaya (HKH) region contains over 15,000 glacial lakes, of which more than 200 in India, Nepal, and Bhutan are classified as potentially dangerous. Climate change is accelerating glacial retreat, enlarging existing lakes and forming new ones, raising GLOF frequency. ICIMOD (International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development), based in Kathmandu, monitors HKH glacial lakes.</li>
<li><strong>Nepal's flood vulnerability:</strong> Nepal's topography — steep gradients, young Himalayan geology, and seasonal South Asian monsoon — makes it one of the world's most disaster-prone nations. The Koshi, Gandaki, and Karnali river systems originate in Nepal and flow into India's Gangetic plain, meaning floods in Nepal directly affect Bihar and UP. Nepal ranks among the top-20 countries most at risk from climate-related disasters globally (ND-GAIN index).</li>
<li><strong>India-Nepal Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1950):</strong> This foundational bilateral treaty allows citizens of each country to reside, own property, and engage in trade in the other's territory. It provides the framework for India's rapid humanitarian deployments across the open border. The treaty is periodically contested in Nepal due to perceived asymmetry, but cooperation on disaster response remains a strong track in bilateral ties.</li>
<li><strong>National Disaster Response Force (NDRF):</strong> Constituted under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, the NDRF comprises 16 battalions drawn from Central Armed Police Forces. It is India's primary agency for disaster response and operates under the NDMA (National Disaster Management Authority), chaired by the Prime Minister. NDRF teams can be pre-positioned on the border in anticipation of cross-border disasters, as happened here.</li>
<li><strong>Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030):</strong> Adopted at the Third UN World Conference in Sendai, Japan, this non-binding agreement sets four priorities: understanding disaster risk; strengthening governance; investing in resilience; and enhancing preparedness. India is a signatory and has aligned its national DM frameworks with Sendai targets. The framework's Target E specifically calls for reducing disaster-affected people globally by 2030 — events like Nepal floods are benchmarked against this.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Sonia Gandhi's Memoir Published by Penguin Sparks Political Controversy
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Freedom of speech and expression; Article 19(1)(a); reasonable restrictions
- GS Paper II → Governance → Political accountability; role of civil society and media

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The release of a memoir by former Congress president and UPA chairperson Sonia Gandhi, published by Penguin India, has generated sharp political reactions. The book covers her decades in Indian public life, including the UPA government's tenure (2004–2014), and has drawn attention for its account of political decisions and personal reflections. Opposition parties and some right-wing groups have raised objections to specific passages, leading to demands for withdrawal or alteration of content. This has triggered a broader debate on publishers' editorial independence, the limits of Article 19(1)(a) (right to free speech), and the extent to which political biographies can face public or legal scrutiny in India. The controversy also highlights the role of the National Advisory Council (NAC), which Sonia Gandhi chaired during UPA, as a non-constitutional advisory body that influenced welfare legislation. For UPSC, the episode is relevant to discussions on freedom of speech, sedition, and the constitutional framework governing expression by public figures.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 19(1)(a) and reasonable restrictions:</strong> The Constitution guarantees freedom of speech and expression to all citizens. Article 19(2) permits the state to impose reasonable restrictions on grounds including sovereignty, security, public order, decency, morality, and contempt of court. Political biographies and memoirs by public figures are generally protected; courts have repeatedly held that the bar for restricting political speech is high.</li>
<li><strong>National Advisory Council (NAC):</strong> The NAC was a civil-society advisory body constituted under UPA-I (2004) and UPA-II (2010), chaired by Sonia Gandhi. It had no constitutional basis — it was a government-constituted body and its members were non-elected. Landmark legislation attributed to NAC's influence includes the Right to Information Act (2005), MGNREGA (2005), and the Right to Food Act (2013). Its legality was debated given the "office of profit" question and the blurring of executive accountability.</li>
<li><strong>Publisher's liability in India:</strong> Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023), publishers can be held liable for seditious, defamatory, or obscene content. However, Indian jurisprudence distinguishes between factual errors in political writing and content that incites violence, setting a high threshold for criminal liability on autobiographical or historical works.</li>
<li><strong>Sedition law evolution (BNS 2023, Section 152):</strong> The colonial sedition law (Section 124A IPC) was suspended pending review after the Supreme Court's 2022 order in S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023) replaced IPC, introducing Section 152 which criminalises acts endangering sovereignty and unity — critics argue this is broader than old sedition. Political memoirs are unlikely to attract this provision but remain subject to defamation suits.</li>
<li><strong>Press freedom in India:</strong> India ranks 159th in the 2026 World Press Freedom Index (Reporters Without Borders), reflecting concerns about publisher intimidation and legal pressure. Publishers sometimes withdraw or alter books pre-emptively under political pressure — a practice courts find difficult to address without a direct legal challenge. This episode illustrates how informal pressure can shape editorial decisions even without formal legal action.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  India's Industrial Output (IIP) Grows 6.7% in July 2026
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Industry</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Indian Economy → Industrial growth; Index of Industrial Production; inclusive growth; economic indicators

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India's Index of Industrial Production (IIP) recorded a growth of 6.7% in July 2026 compared to the same month in the previous year, signalling continued momentum in manufacturing and industrial activity. The data, released by the National Statistical Office (NSO), showed strong performance in manufacturing (which carries a weight of over 77% in the IIP basket), with electricity generation and mining also posting positive numbers. Capital goods output — a proxy for private investment — grew robustly, suggesting that the investment cycle is gaining traction. Consumer durables also registered growth, pointing to improved domestic demand. However, analysts note that the base effect from July 2025 may have flattered the headline number. The IIP print is significant ahead of RBI's monetary policy committee decisions, as sustained industrial growth gives policymakers room to balance growth with price stability. UPSC candidates should note IIP's components, its composition (use-based and sector-based classification), and its relationship with GDP and GVA growth.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Index of Industrial Production (IIP):</strong> The IIP is a composite indicator released monthly by the National Statistical Office (NSO) under MoSPI. It measures the quantum of industrial production in the economy across three sectors: manufacturing (77.6% weight), electricity (7.9%), and mining (14.4%). The base year for IIP is 2011–12. It is one of the key high-frequency indicators watched by the RBI and Finance Ministry alongside PMI, GST collections, and core sector output.</li>
<li><strong>Use-based classification of IIP:</strong> IIP is also classified by use: primary goods, capital goods, intermediate goods, infrastructure/construction goods, and consumer goods (durables and non-durables). Capital goods growth signals investment demand while consumer durables signal household discretionary spending — both are important for tracking the business cycle. Consumer non-durables reflect essential consumption patterns.</li>
<li><strong>Base effect in economic data:</strong> When a prior-period figure was unusually low (e.g. due to a disruption), the year-on-year growth rate in the current period appears inflated even with modest absolute improvement. Conversely, a high base year depresses apparent growth. Analysts adjust for base effects by looking at 2-year compounded annual growth rates (CAGR) to get a truer picture of trend growth.</li>
<li><strong>Core sector industries (Eight Core Industries):</strong> The eight core industries — coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilisers, steel, cement, and electricity — together have a combined weight of 40.27% in IIP. Their monthly output data is released a week before IIP figures, providing a leading indicator. Strong core sector performance generally presages a healthy IIP print.</li>
<li><strong>RBI Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) and IIP:</strong> The MPC, constituted under the RBI Act (amended 2016), uses a flexible inflation-targeting framework (target: 4% CPI ± 2%). IIP data feeds into MPC deliberations on growth outlook. The MPC has six members — three from RBI and three external — and decisions are by majority vote. The Governor casts a deciding vote in case of a tie.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  India Finalises Javelin Anti-Tank Missile Deal with the United States
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Defence</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Foreign Policy</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Industry</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-US strategic partnership; defence cooperation; COMCASA; BECA; LEMOA
- GS Paper III → Security → Internal security; modernisation of armed forces; defence procurement; indigenisation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India has concluded a government-to-government deal with the United States for the procurement of Javelin anti-tank guided missiles (ATGMs) for the Indian Army. The Javelin, jointly produced by Raytheon and Lockheed Martin, is a fire-and-forget ATGM with a range of approximately 2.5–4 km and a top-attack capability designed to defeat heavily armoured main battle tanks. The deal, finalised under the US Foreign Military Sales (FMS) route, marks a continuation of the deepening India-US defence partnership formalised through the COMCASA (2018), BECA (2020), and LEMOA (2016) foundational agreements. Importantly, the deal does not include a Transfer of Technology (ToT) clause, which critics argue limits indigenisation. The procurement comes in the context of India's ongoing border tensions with China along the LAC, where ATGM capability against armoured columns is tactically significant. UPSC candidates should link this to India's defence procurement policy, the shift from Russia to diversified sourcing, and the debate on Make in India versus FMS procurement.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Foundational Defence Agreements (LEMOA, COMCASA, BECA):</strong> The three foundational agreements with the US form the backbone of the defence partnership. LEMOA (Logistics Exchange Memorandum of Agreement, 2016) allows mutual use of military facilities for repair, rest, and refuelling. COMCASA (Communications Compatibility and Security Agreement, 2018) enables encrypted communication equipment interoperability. BECA (Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement, 2020) facilitates sharing of geospatial intelligence and enhances targeting precision. All four were signed by 2020.</li>
<li><strong>Foreign Military Sales (FMS) route:</strong> FMS is a US government-to-government arms transfer mechanism under the Arms Export Control Act. Procurement via FMS bypasses commercial negotiation: the US government acts as the contracting agent, purchases equipment from US manufacturers on India's behalf, and delivers it at cost plus administrative fees. FMS ensures interoperability and post-sale support but offers less room for ToT than Direct Commercial Sales (DCS).</li>
<li><strong>Defence Procurement Policy (DPP) and DAP 2020:</strong> India's Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020 (DAP 2020) categorises procurement into categories (IDDM, Make-I, Make-II, Buy Indian, Buy & Make, Buy Global). The government has progressively raised the FDI limit in defence to 74% under automatic route and 100% under government route, aiming to reduce import dependence. The deal's FMS route implies it falls outside DAP's indigenisation categories.</li>
<li><strong>India-Russia defence dependence and diversification:</strong> Historically, ~60–70% of India's military hardware came from Russia. Post-2022 (Russia-Ukraine war), India has accelerated diversification toward the US, France, and Israel while maintaining Russian platform sustainment. Russia sanctions risk (CAATSA — Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act) is a recurring concern: India received a partial waiver for the S-400 Triumf purchase, but ongoing procurement could be complicated by CAATSA provisions.</li>
<li><strong>Javelin vs. indigenous ATGM programmes:</strong> India's DRDO has developed the Nag (Nag Missile System/NAMICA) and the Man-Portable Anti-Tank Guided Missile (MPATGM). MPATGM is lighter and man-portable, similar to Javelin in role. Critics argue that procuring Javelin without ToT slows down MPATGM induction and undermines Atmanirbhar Bharat in defence. The Army, however, prefers proven platforms for immediate capability gaps along the LAC.</li>
</ul>
</div>

  Suvendu Adhikari Slams Jadavpur University Students Over 'Azadi' Slogans
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Freedom of speech and expression; sedition; student movements; role of universities in democracy
- GS Paper II → Governance → Political accountability; legislature-executive interface; police powers

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
West Bengal Chief Minister Suvendu Adhikari (BJP) has strongly criticised students at Jadavpur University (JU) in Kolkata for raising 'azadi' slogans during a campus event, calling the chants seditious and anti-national. Jadavpur University has historically been a site of student political activism, and the controversy has reignited a national debate on the limits of free expression in university spaces. Adhikari's remarks carry additional weight given his position as CM — the state government's stance directly shapes how police and university administrators respond. The Trinamool Congress, now in opposition, has pushed back, defending students' right to expression. The episode raises constitutional questions under Article 19(1)(a) — the right to free speech and expression — and its interplay with the recently amended laws on sedition and acts endangering national unity. UPSC aspirants should note the tension between campus autonomy, freedom of speech, and the state's powers under Articles 19(2) and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023), especially as student movements across India increasingly intersect with electoral politics.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Jadavpur University and student activism:</strong> JU is a premier state university in West Bengal ranked among India's top research institutions. It has a long tradition of Left-leaning student politics, with the Students' Federation of India (SFI) historically dominant. The campus gained national attention in 2019 when TMC students and BJP supporters clashed during a political rally. University autonomy is protected under the UGC Act (1956) and Supreme Court rulings on academic freedom.</li>
<li><strong>'Azadi' slogans and legal grey area:</strong> The slogan "azadi" (freedom), popularised at JNU in 2016, is interpreted differently: as a demand for freedoms such as caste liberation, gender equality, or economic justice by its proponents, and as a secessionist slogan by critics. Courts have ruled that sloganeering alone — absent a direct incitement to violence — does not constitute sedition (Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar, 1962, remains the landmark precedent).</li>
<li><strong>Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 — Section 152:</strong> This section penalises "acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India" with imprisonment up to 7 years or life. Unlike the old Section 124A IPC (sedition), BNS Section 152 does not require proof of incitement to violence — a broader ambit that has alarmed civil liberties groups. The Supreme Court is yet to definitively rule on its constitutionality.</li>
<li><strong>Chief Minister — constitutional role in a state:</strong> The Chief Minister heads the Council of Ministers in a state, appointed by the Governor under Article 164 after commanding the confidence of the Legislative Assembly. As head of government, the CM chairs Cabinet meetings, advises the Governor on state matters, and is the principal interface between the elected government and the Governor. In West Bengal, where Suvendu Adhikari leads the BJP government, the CM's remarks on campus speech carry executive weight distinct from those of an opposition leader.</li>
<li><strong>University Grants Commission (UGC) and campus autonomy:</strong> The UGC, established under the UGC Act (1956), regulates higher education, grants recognition to universities, and sets academic standards. UGC regulations on campus elections (Lyngdoh Committee recommendations, 2006) govern student union bodies. The MHA and state governments have no direct jurisdiction over academic content or campus speech, making police action against students a constitutionally contested zone requiring state government orders.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Puducherry CM Renews Demand for Full Statehood
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Federalism</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Union Territories; Article 239–241; legislative powers of UTs; Centre-UT relations; federalism
- GS Paper II → Governance → Devolution of powers; democratic accountability in UTs

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Puducherry Chief Minister N. Rangasamy has renewed his government's longstanding demand that Puducherry be granted full statehood, arguing that the current Union Territory status severely limits the elected government's legislative and executive powers. The CM highlighted that the Lt. Governor (LG), appointed by the Centre, retains overriding authority over several subjects including land, public order, and police — areas where the elected government has no effective control. The demand has been raised repeatedly by successive governments in Puducherry regardless of political affiliation, reflecting structural grievances about the UT framework. It parallels ongoing debates in Delhi, where the Supreme Court and Parliament have both been drawn into adjudicating the boundaries of executive power between the elected government and the LG (as seen in the GNCTD Amendment Act, 2021, and subsequent 2023 amendment). UPSC candidates must understand the constitutional distinction between Part C States (historical), current UTs with and without legislature, and the specific legislative scope of Puducherry under Article 239A.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 239A — UTs with Legislature:</strong> Article 239A (inserted by the 14th Constitutional Amendment, 1962) empowers Parliament to create a legislature and Council of Ministers for certain Union Territories. Puducherry was given a legislature under this provision through the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963. However, unlike States, the UT legislature can legislate only on subjects in the State and Concurrent Lists with certain exclusions, and the LG has overriding powers in disputed matters.</li>
<li><strong>Lieutenant Governor's powers in Puducherry:</strong> The LG of Puducherry is appointed by the President under Article 239 and can act independently of the elected Council of Ministers on matters where the LG deems Central government interest is at stake. The Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 (as amended) requires the CM to communicate all Cabinet decisions to the LG, who can refer matters to the President. This creates a structural tension absent in full States governed by Article 163–164.</li>
<li><strong>Delhi precedent — GNCTD Act amendments:</strong> The Government of NCT of Delhi (Amendment) Acts of 2021 and 2023 curtailed the elected Delhi government's powers over the bureaucracy (services), following the Supreme Court's 2018 and 2023 constitution bench judgements. The 2023 Parliament amendment overrode the SC's majority ruling by creating the National Capital Civil Services Authority, tilting power toward the LG. This precedent is directly relevant to Puducherry's statehood demand as it shows Parliament can legislate UT powers.</li>
<li><strong>Statehood vs UT — practical difference:</strong> Full statehood would give Puducherry authority over the State List subjects (including police, land, law & order) and remove the LG's override power. It would also entitle the state to a High Court (Puducherry currently falls under the Madras High Court), a share of central taxes via the Finance Commission, and the ability to form its own Public Service Commission (PPSC, currently shared).</li>
<li><strong>History of Puducherry as a UT:</strong> Puducherry was a French territory (union of Pondicherry, Karikal, Mahé, and Yanam) ceded to India in 1954 and legally merged in 1962 after the Treaty of Cession was ratified by the French Parliament. It became a UT under the 14th Amendment. The unique multicultural heritage — Tamil, French, Telugu, Malayalam — makes it constitutionally complex and politically distinct from other UTs.</li>
</ul>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Popular Front of India (PFI) — ban under UAPA:</strong> The PFI was banned by the Central government under Section 3 of the UAPA in September 2022, for a period of five years, on grounds of links to terrorist organisations including ISIS, involvement in targeted killings, and alleged plans to destabilise the country. **Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The longstanding demand to revise the income ceiling for the OBC "creamy layer" exclusion from reservations has remained unaddressed, with no announcement from the government despite multiple Supreme Court and National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) recommendations to raise the threshold. The creamy layer income limit — currently ₹8 lakh per annum — was last revised in 2017. Since then, inflation has significantly eroded the real value of this threshold, meaning more OBC families are excluded from reservation benefits than originally intended when the limit was set at ₹1 lakh in 1993. Multiple political parties, including those in the ruling coalition, have been reluctant to act, as any revision would either expand the OBC reservation beneficiary pool (politically contentious with general category voters) or leave the limit unchanged (alienating OBC political constituencies). The issue directly affects lakhs of OBC candidates applying for Central government jobs and higher education seats. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Creamy layer concept — Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992):</strong> In the landmark Indra Sawhney (Mandal) case, the Supreme Court (9-judge bench) upheld the Mandal Commission's 27% OBC reservation in Central services but introduced the "creamy layer" exclusion — holding that socially advanced members of OBC communities (above a specified income/status threshold) should not benefit from reservations meant for the genuinely backward. The creamy layer does NOT apply to SC/ST reservations. The initial income limit of ₹1 lakh per annum was set in 1993.</li>
<li><strong>Creamy layer revision history:</strong> The income ceiling has been revised periodically: ₹1 lakh (1993) → ₹2.5 lakh (2004) → ₹4.5 lakh (2008) → ₹6 lakh (2013) → ₹8 lakh (2017). The NCBC and various Parliamentary committees have repeatedly recommended raising it to ₹12–15 lakh to account for inflation (₹8 lakh in 2017 has roughly ₹5.5 lakh purchasing power in 2026). The political sensitivity has kept the revision pending since 2017 — over 9 years.</li>
<li><strong>National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC):</strong> The NCBC was initially a statutory body (under the NCBC Act, 1993). The 102nd Constitutional Amendment (2018) elevated it to a constitutional status under Article 338B, giving it powers to examine requests for inclusion or exclusion in OBC lists and to present reports to the President on safeguards for socially and educationally backward classes. Its recommendations are advisory — the government is not bound to implement them.</li>
<li><strong>Articles 15(4) and 16(4) — reservation provisions:</strong> Article 15(4) (added by the 1st Amendment, 1951) allows special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes or SCs/STs. Article 16(4) allows reservations in public employment for "any backward class of citizens which, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in the services." These are enabling provisions (not mandatory rights), and the extent of reservation is subject to the Indra Sawhney 50% ceiling (with some exceptions for extraordinary circumstances like Tamil Nadu's 69% reservation).</li>
<li><strong>OBC sub-classification — SC ruling (2024):</strong> In August 2024, a 7-judge Constitution Bench in State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh overruled the earlier E.V. Chinnaiah (2004) judgment and held that states CAN sub-classify OBC communities to create internal quotas for the most backward among OBCs. This opened the door for "OBC within OBC" reservations, allowing states to prioritise the most under-represented sub-groups. The creamy layer issue intersects with sub-classification — defining which families within each sub-group qualify.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways has announced the PARIVARTAN (Promoting Advancement of Rapid Integrated Vehicular Transport and National Transition) scheme to deploy over two lakh electric buses in the Delhi-NCR region by 2028, as part of India's National Electric Mobility Mission Plan (NEMMP) and the Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of (Hybrid &) Electric Vehicles (FAME) policy successor. The scheme targets a complete transition of the Delhi Transport Corporation (DTC) and National Capital Region Transport Corporation (NCRTC) bus fleets to electric, alongside subsidising private bus operators willing to convert. Delhi is India's most polluted major city and vehicular emissions from buses (predominantly diesel) are a significant contributor to PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations. The PARIVARTAN scheme integrates with the PM e-Bus Sewa programme (launched 2023, targeting 10,000 e-buses in 169 cities) and aims at a 30% EV penetration in public transport by 2028. UPSC candidates should link this to GRADED Response Action Plan (GRAP), Delhi's air quality management, and the constitutional dimension of the right to a clean environment.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Delhi's air pollution crisis:</strong> Delhi regularly records AQI (Air Quality Index) in the "Severe" category (AQI > 400) during winters (October–February), driven by stubble burning in Punjab/Haryana, vehicular emissions, industrial and construction dust, and unfavourable meteorological conditions (low wind speed, temperature inversion). Delhi's PM2.5 average annual level is 10–15 times the WHO safe limit (5 µg/m³). The Supreme Court-mandated CAQM (Commission for Air Quality Management in NCR and Adjoining Areas) replaced multiple oversight bodies under the CAQM Act, 2021.</li>
<li><strong>FAME India Scheme and EV transition:</strong> FAME (Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Electric Vehicles) Phase II (2019–2024, ₹10,000 crore) incentivised EV purchases through demand-side subsidies, especially for two-wheelers, three-wheelers, buses, and charging infrastructure. FAME III is under development as the successor. The PM e-Bus Sewa scheme (2023, ₹57,613 crore, targeting 10,000 e-buses in cities with population 3+ lakh) uses a PPP model where the government pays operators a "gross cost contract" for running electric buses.</li>
<li><strong>GRADED Response Action Plan (GRAP):</strong> GRAP was formulated by the Ministry of Environment and operationalised by CAQM to impose stage-wise emergency restrictions on Delhi-NCR as AQI worsens. GRAP I (AQI 201–300): dust control, no open burning. GRAP II (AQI 301–400): additional restrictions. GRAP III (AQI 401–450): closure of schools, ban on construction. GRAP IV (AQI > 450): ban on entry of non-essential trucks, consideration of lockdown. Supreme Court supervises GRAP implementation through regular hearings.</li>
<li><strong>Right to clean environment — constitutional basis:</strong> The Supreme Court in MC Mehta v. Union of India (1987 and subsequent orders) and Virender Gaur v. State of Haryana (1995) held that Article 21 (right to life) encompasses the right to a clean and healthy environment. The Constitution's Directive Principle Article 48A also mandates the State to protect and improve the environment. This constitutional framework gives the SC authority to issue executive-style directions on pollution control, including GRAP enforcement and stubble-burning management.</li>
<li><strong>Delhi Transport Corporation (DTC) fleet transition:</strong> DTC is a public sector undertaking owned by the Delhi government and is the largest public bus operator in India with approximately 7,000 buses. The transition to electric buses requires not just vehicle procurement but charging infrastructure buildout (grid capacity, charging depots), driver retraining, and maintenance ecosystem development. BYD (China), Tata Motors, and Olectra are key electric bus suppliers in India; procurement under government contracts is subject to the "Make in India" Public Procurement Order (PPO) favouring domestic manufacturers.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Natural gas pricing; energy policy; fuel subsidy reform; inflation impact

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Indraprastha Gas Limited (IGL) has hiked Compressed Natural Gas (CNG) prices by ₹3.89 per kg in Delhi-NCR and simultaneously raised piped natural gas (PNG) prices for households, citing increased natural gas procurement costs following the revision in domestic gas allocation prices under the government's new pricing formula. The hike will directly affect auto-rickshaws, taxis, DTC buses, and private CNG vehicles — and has sparked protests from transporters. CNG is promoted as a cleaner alternative to petrol/diesel (lower PM2.5 and NOx emissions) and has been mandated for commercial vehicles in Delhi since a Supreme Court order in 1998. However, the recurring price hikes are blunting CNG's cost advantage over petrol in a context where fuel prices are market-linked. The episode also highlights the structural challenge of gas pricing in India — domestic production has declined while LNG import prices remain volatile. UPSC candidates should understand the Administered Price Mechanism (APM) for gas, the role of PNGRB, and India's city gas distribution (CGD) network expansion.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>City Gas Distribution (CGD) network:</strong> The CGD network supplies piped natural gas (PNG) to homes and establishments, and CNG to vehicles through retail outlets. It is regulated by the Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB), established under the PNGRB Act, 2006. CGD geographic areas (GAs) are awarded through competitive bidding. As of 2026, India has authorised CGD networks across over 300 GAs, covering approximately 70% of the country's population. IGL (Delhi-NCR), MGL (Mumbai), GAIL Gas, and Adani Total Gas are major operators.</li>
<li><strong>Domestic gas pricing — APM and revised formula:</strong> India's domestic natural gas price was historically set through the Administered Price Mechanism (APM). Post-Rangarajan Committee (2012), the government moved to a formula-linked price linked to global gas benchmarks. Under the current formula (revised 2022), gas from "nomination fields" (ONGC/Oil India legacy fields) is priced at a weighted average of Henry Hub, NBP, Russia, and Alberta prices — reviewed every 6 months. APM gas feeds city gas distribution at a lower price than open-market LNG, making CGD economics sensitive to APM revisions.</li>
<li><strong>Supreme Court CNG mandate for Delhi (1998):</strong> In MC Mehta v. Union of India, the Supreme Court ordered in 1998 that all commercial vehicles (buses, autos, taxis) operating in Delhi must convert to CNG by April 2001. This transformed Delhi's public transport fuel mix and significantly improved air quality in the early 2000s. The SC's environmental jurisprudence under Article 21 continues to drive city-level clean fuel mandates across Indian metros.</li>
<li><strong>PNGRB — Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board:</strong> PNGRB (established 2006) regulates the refining, processing, storage, transportation, distribution, marketing, and sale of petroleum, petroleum products, and natural gas, excluding production. It monitors CGD tariffs, approves expansion plans, and adjudicates disputes between CGD operators and consumers. PNGRB has quasi-judicial powers and its orders are appealable to the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL).</li>
<li><strong>LNG imports and India's gas import dependence:</strong> India imports approximately 45–50% of its natural gas requirements as LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas), primarily from Qatar (under a 25-year deal with Petronet LNG), Australia, and spot market. India's domestic production from KG-D6 (Reliance), ONGC Mumbai High, and other fields has declined over the last decade. Expanding domestic gas output — including from unconventional sources like coal bed methane (CBM) and shale gas — is a key energy security priority, reducing vulnerability to LNG price spikes.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Editorial: ECI's Special Intensive Revision — 'Unkind Cuts' to Electoral Rolls
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 6 · Editorial</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Election Commission of India; voter registration; RPA 1950; electoral rolls; free and fair elections
- GS Paper II → Governance → Accountability of constitutional bodies; transparency; RTI

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Hindu editorial criticises the Election Commission of India's (ECI) conduct of the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, arguing that the process has resulted in the deletion of a disproportionately large number of voter names — raising concerns about voter suppression affecting marginalised communities. The editorial points out that the SIR — which involves door-to-door verification and deletion of "duplicate or untraceable" voters — has been challenged in the Supreme Court after civil society groups documented systematic deletion of voters in urban slums, migrant worker localities, and minority-concentrated areas. The editorial questions ECI's accountability mechanism and transparency in the deletion process, noting that affected voters are often unaware of their names being removed until election day. The piece argues that the right to vote — while not explicitly stated in the Constitution — is a constitutional and statutory right under the Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1950, and that any mass deletion without due process violates the right to equality and equal protection under Article 14. UPSC candidates should understand the ECI's powers over electoral rolls, the RPA 1950 framework, and safeguards against arbitrary deletion.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls:</strong> Under the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960, the ECI conducts periodic revisions of electoral rolls. An "intensive revision" involves booth-level officers (BLOs) visiting every house to verify entries. Deletions can be made on grounds of death, shift in residence, or non-traceability. Critics argue that non-traceability — often because migrant workers are absent on verification day — is being used to delete valid voters, amounting to disenfranchisement.</li>
<li><strong>Election Commission of India — constitutional status:</strong> The ECI is a constitutional body under Article 324, which vests superintendence, direction, and control of elections to Parliament, state legislatures, the President, and the Vice-President in the ECI. The Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and other Election Commissioners can be removed only through a process akin to impeachment of a Supreme Court judge. The Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023 changed the appointment committee to exclude the CJI.</li>
<li><strong>Right to vote — constitutional status:</strong> Unlike many constitutional rights, the right to vote is not explicitly listed in the Fundamental Rights chapter. It is a statutory right under the Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1950 and 1951. However, the Supreme Court in Javed v. State of Haryana (2003) held that the right to vote is an important democratic right and in PUCL v. Union of India (2003) held that voters have the right to know about candidates' backgrounds — progressively strengthening the democratic right to vote even without explicit constitutional text.</li>
<li><strong>Voter deletion safeguards under RPA 1950:</strong> Section 22 of the RPA 1950 allows an electoral registration officer to delete a name from the roll if the person is not entitled to be registered, but requires prior opportunity to the person concerned. Rule 18 of the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 provides for a hearing. Critics argue these procedural safeguards are routinely bypassed in mass revision exercises, with deletion notices not reaching the concerned voters — especially in informal urban settlements.</li>
<li><strong>ECI accountability and judicial review:</strong> While Article 329(b) bars courts from questioning election-related matters once the election process has begun (election petitions are the remedy post-election), the Supreme Court and High Courts can judicially review pre-election administrative decisions of the ECI — including voter roll revisions — if fundamental rights are violated. The SC has reviewed ECI decisions in cases like Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India (2006) and S. Subramaniam Balaji v. Union of India (2013).</li>
</ul>
</div>

</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Afghanistan relations; Taliban; SAARC; regional security architecture; Pakistan's role
- GS Paper III → Internal Security → Cross-border terrorism; Taliban's regional impact; ISIS-K

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
August 2026 marks five years since the Taliban's takeover of Afghanistan (August 2021). This Op-Ed assesses the humanitarian, strategic, and diplomatic landscape five years on. Afghanistan today is experiencing one of the world's worst humanitarian crises: over 23 million people face acute food insecurity (nearly half the population), girls above Class 6 are banned from school, women are barred from most public employment, and the formal economy has largely collapsed since the US-led withdrawal and freeze of Afghan central bank reserves ($7 billion frozen by the US). The Taliban has not been recognised by any government internationally. For India, the Op-Ed argues that Afghanistan represents a complex strategic challenge — the Taliban has maintained operational links with anti-India militant groups through Pakistani patrons, yet India has quietly reopened diplomatic contact through its embassy in Kabul to protect its considerable infrastructure investments (Salma Dam, Shahtoot Dam, Zaranj-Delaram Highway, Afghan Parliament building). India's engagement is characterised as "strategic pragmatism without recognition." UPSC candidates must know India's historical role in Afghanistan and the China-Pakistan-Taliban triangulation.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India's investment and historical ties with Afghanistan:</strong> India has invested approximately $3 billion in Afghanistan in infrastructure — including the Salma Dam (Afghan-India Friendship Dam, Herat), the Shahtoot Dam (under construction, Kabul water supply), the 218-km Zaranj-Delaram Highway (connecting the Iranian border to Afghan road network), and the new Afghan Parliament building in Kabul. India also trained thousands of Afghan civil servants, police, and military personnel. These investments represent India's strategic interest in a friendly, stable Afghanistan and access to Central Asia bypassing Pakistan.</li>
<li><strong>Taliban's return and Pakistan's role:</strong> The Taliban emerged from Pakistan's Pashtun tribal belt in the 1990s, with documented support from Pakistan's ISI. Pakistan provided sanctuary, logistics, and political support to the Taliban throughout the 2001–2021 US occupation. The Haqqani Network — a Taliban faction — has been designated a terrorist organisation by the US and has historically targeted Indian interests in Afghanistan. Post-2021, Pakistan's "strategic asset" narrative on the Taliban has complicated, as the Taliban's own Tehrik-e-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) has turned against Islamabad.</li>
<li><strong>ISIS-K (Islamic State Khorasan Province):</strong> ISIS-K is the Afghan affiliate of the Islamic State, formed in 2015. It has carried out spectacular attacks inside Afghanistan (including a 2021 Kabul airport bombing killing 13 US soldiers and 170 Afghans). ISIS-K views the Taliban as insufficiently radical. It has also shown capability for attacks beyond Afghanistan — including a claimed attack in Moscow (2024). ISIS-K's presence in Afghanistan creates a security threat to India (through radicalised networks in South Asia) and regional stability.</li>
<li><strong>Doha Agreement (2020) and its aftermath:</strong> The Doha Agreement between the US and the Taliban (February 2020) committed US forces to withdraw in exchange for Taliban guarantees not to harbour Al-Qaeda or allow territory to be used against the US. The agreement excluded the Afghan government, effectively delegitimising it. The Taliban completed their takeover by August 15, 2021, within 10 days of the US forces' completion of withdrawal. India did not participate in the Doha process — a diplomatic oversight that left India with limited post-2021 leverage.</li>
<li><strong>India-Afghanistan diplomatic status:</strong> India's embassy in Kabul was shut in August 2021 during the chaotic evacuation. India sent a "technical team" to Kabul in June 2022 — effectively a quasi-diplomatic mission — to assess the ground situation and protect assets. India has since quietly expanded this engagement. The MEA's position is that India supports an "Afghan-led, Afghan-owned, Afghan-controlled" peace process and has not recognised the Taliban government. China and Russia have maintained working-level diplomatic contacts while also not formally recognising Taliban rule.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
This Op-Ed argues that Nepal's catastrophic floods are not merely a humanitarian event but a geopolitical test of the India-Nepal-China strategic triangle. India has moved fastest with NDRF teams, relief materials, and consular outreach — underscoring its neighbourhood-first policy and its legacy as Nepal's traditional first responder. The piece advocates for a permanent India-Nepal humanitarian pre-positioning arrangement and a shared GLOF early warning system. For UPSC, this is essential reading on humanitarian diplomacy and how disaster response shapes bilateral relationships.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India's Operation Maitri (Nepal, 2015):</strong> Following the devastating 7.8 Richter earthquake in Nepal (April 2015) that killed over 9,000 people, India launched Operation Maitri — the largest-ever humanitarian rescue operation in its history. India deployed 16 NDRF teams, 14 Army columns, 7 Navy diving teams, and flew 250+ IAF sorties delivering 500+ tonnes of relief material. This operation significantly boosted India's soft power in Nepal and remains the benchmark for Indian humanitarian diplomacy in the neighbourhood.</li>
<li><strong>China's BRI investments in Nepal:</strong> Nepal joined China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) in 2017. Key Chinese projects include the Pokhara Regional International Airport (completed 2023, but financially struggling with low international flight traffic), and the planned Kathmandu-Rasuwagadhi Railway (connecting Kathmandu to the Tibetan border via Kerung). Nepal's BRI debt burden is a concern — particularly the Pokhara Airport, which Nepal has sought to renegotiate. China's BRI connectivity gives it economic leverage but cannot replicate India's open-border humanitarian access.</li>
<li><strong>SAARC disaster management framework:</strong> SAARC (South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation) has a Comprehensive Framework on Disaster Management (adopted 2006) and a SAARC Disaster Management Centre (SDMC, based in New Delhi, reconstituted in 2016 as the Asian Disaster Preparedness Center regional office). However, India-Pakistan tensions have effectively paralysed SAARC as a functional body since 2016, limiting regional disaster cooperation to bilateral channels. A functional SAARC with China included (as observer) could transform regional disaster response.</li>
<li><strong>Humanitarian diplomacy and soft power:</strong> Humanitarian diplomacy refers to the use of disaster relief, development assistance, and crisis response as instruments of foreign policy influence. India has used it effectively in its neighbourhood (Sri Lanka 2004 tsunami, Maldives 2004–05, Nepal 2015, Bangladesh cyclone). Joseph Nye's concept of "soft power" (attraction through culture, values, and foreign policy) is often measured partly through humanitarian reputation. UPSC candidates should understand how disaster response links to strategic depth in India's foreign policy.</li>
<li><strong>India-Nepal-China strategic triangle — key dynamics:</strong> Nepal is constitutionally a federal secular republic (2015 Constitution) after abolishing its Hindu monarchy. It maintains equidistance between India and China as an official policy but leans toward India economically (80% of trade, energy dependence, open border). China offers Nepal an alternative trade route and infrastructure but lacks India's cultural and demographic ties. Occasional friction points — including Nepal's Lipulekh, Kalapani, Limpiyadhura territorial claims with India — complicate the bilateral but are secondary to the overwhelming depth of India-Nepal interconnection.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Haryana's Gen Z Pushes Back Against Khap Panchayats in Romance and Marriage Choices

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>

  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 7 · Ground Zero</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Indian Society → Caste; rural social institutions; changing social values; women's rights
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Rights of women; honour killing; protection from social ostracism; SC judgements on khap panchayats

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A ground-level reportage from Haryana's Jat-dominated districts documents a generational shift: young people (the "Gen Z" cohort, born after 1997) are increasingly challenging the authority of khap panchayats over marriage and romantic choices. Young couples from the same gotra (clan) or same village — traditionally banned from marrying by khap dictates — are defying orders and sometimes seeking police protection under SC guidelines. Social media and mobile phones are cited as enablers: young people are forming relationships beyond village boundaries, accessing legal information online, and documenting khap threats. While honour killings and social ostracism remain a real risk, the reportage notes that young Haryanvi women in particular are more assertive about their rights than previous generations. The Supreme Court in Shakti Vahini v. Union of India (2018) directed states to set up special cells to protect inter-caste and inter-faith couples. The reportage contextualises this as India's silent social revolution — urbanisation, internet access, and economic independence slowly eroding patriarchal and caste-based social control.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Khap panchayats — nature and legal status:</strong> Khap panchayats are caste-based community councils, particularly prevalent in Haryana, western UP, and Rajasthan among Jat and other agrarian communities. They are entirely extra-constitutional — they have no legal basis or formal recognition under the Panchayati Raj system (73rd Amendment, 1992). Their decisions are non-binding but are often enforced through social pressure, economic boycott, and violence. Courts have repeatedly held that khap dictates carry no legal weight and can be challenged.</li>
<li><strong>Honour killing — legal framework:</strong> "Honour killing" — murder of a family member perceived to have brought shame through a relationship or marriage outside community norms — is punishable under IPC (now BNS) sections for murder, abetment to murder, and criminal conspiracy. The Law Commission's 242nd Report (2012) recommended specific legislation against khap interference in adult marriages. The Shakti Vahini v. Union of India (2018) SC ruling directed states to create "special cells" and a "safe house" network for threatened inter-caste couples, and held that adult individuals' right to marry a person of their choice is an intrinsic part of Article 21.</li>
<li><strong>Gotra and caste endogamy/exogamy:</strong> In Hindu social structure, gotra (patrilineal clan) defines exogamy rules — members of the same gotra are considered siblings and cannot marry. Khaps extend this to all members of the same village (sagotra ban) or even same sub-caste. The Hindu Marriage Act (1955) prohibits marriages within certain degrees of prohibited relationship but does not codify gotra-based prohibitions as law. Inter-caste marriages are protected by the Special Marriage Act (1954) and promoted through Dr Ambedkar Schemes for Social Integration through Inter-Caste Marriages.</li>
<li><strong>Haryana's social indicators — context:</strong> Haryana has India's worst sex ratio at birth (SSB) — historically below 900 girls per 1,000 boys in many districts due to sex-selective abortion, reflecting deeply patriarchal norms. Despite being one of India's wealthier states (high per-capita income, agriculture prosperity), its gender indicators lag behind. The Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (BBBP) campaign was launched in Haryana's Panipat in January 2015 specifically to address Haryana's poor SSB as a pilot district.</li>
<li><strong>Social change drivers — internet and urbanisation:</strong> India's internet penetration reached over 900 million users by 2026, with rural India's share growing rapidly through Jio's 4G rollout (post-2016). Digital connectivity is enabling young people in conservative rural settings to access alternative lifestyles, legal information, and peer communities challenging traditional social controls. Sociologists studying Indian social change note that mobile internet access is compressing the timeline of social transformation that took generations in the West, accelerating attitudinal change on gender roles, caste, and marriage within a single generation.</li>
</ul>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 46 — DPSP for weaker sections:</strong> Article 46 is a Directive Principle of State Policy that directs the state to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, in particular Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and to protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation. While not directly enforceable in court, it provides the constitutional basis for tribal welfare schemes and has been cited in SC judgements on ST rights.</li>
<li><strong>Fifth Schedule and Tribal Areas:</strong> The Fifth Schedule of the Constitution (Article 244) provides for administration and control of Scheduled Areas in states other than the North-East. Scheduled Areas are declared by the President after consultation with the Governor and are areas with significant tribal population requiring special administrative attention. The Governor has special powers (submit reports on ST administration to the President; Tribes Advisory Council) in Fifth Schedule states. Maharashtra has Scheduled Areas in its tribal districts.</li>
<li><strong>Tribal Sub-Plan (now ST Component):</strong> The Tribal Sub-Plan (TSP), introduced in the 5th Five Year Plan (1974–79), mandates that state governments earmark plan funds proportional to the ST population share for their welfare. Renamed "Scheduled Tribe Component" (STC) in 2013. Maharashtra has an ST population of approximately 11% — meaning 11% of plan funds should go to ST welfare. CAG audits consistently show that states divert TSP funds to non-tribal purposes, and that maintenance grants for tribal hostels are chronically underfunded.</li>
<li><strong>Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS):</strong> EMRS are central government-funded residential schools for tribal children (Classes VI–XII) in Scheduled Areas, analogous to Navodaya Vidyalayas for tribal areas. The government in 2018 committed to opening EMRS in every block with 50%+ ST population. Maharashtra has a large EMRS network. These schools receive central funds for construction and teacher salaries, while state governments are responsible for ongoing maintenance — the maintenance funding gap is a recurring problem reflected in the Pune students' demands.</li>
<li><strong>Post-Matric Scholarship (PMS) for STs:</strong> The PMS scheme for Scheduled Tribes provides scholarships to ST students pursuing education beyond Class X. It covers tuition fees, maintenance allowance, and study materials. The scheme is a centrally sponsored scheme (60:40 Centre:State ratio, 90:10 for NE and hilly states). Maharashtra has historically accumulated arrears in releasing PMS disbursements — a major grievance of tribal student communities and one of the 14 demands in the Pune strike.</li>
</ul>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Rights of persons with disabilities; RPwD Act 2016; caregiver support

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A feature story from Kerala documents the growing crisis faced by ageing parents who are the sole caregivers of adult children with severe intellectual or physical disabilities — autism, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability — with no institutional or community support systems to take over when parents become too old or die. Kerala has India's most aged population (highest proportion of people above 60) alongside a high prevalence of reported disability — a combination creating a distinctive "double ageing" caregiving challenge. The story profiles families where elderly parents (70s–80s) are still bathing, feeding, and managing adult children with disabilities while worrying about what will happen after their death. Kerala's Kudumbashree network has attempted some day-care programmes, but residential care capacity for disabled adults remains woefully short. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act (RPwD) 2016 mandates community inclusion and independent living support but implementation — particularly for those with severe disabilities requiring constant care — remains thin. UPSC candidates should connect this to the RPwD Act, the UN Convention on Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD), Kerala's development model, and the demographic transition.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016:</strong> The RPwD Act replaced the Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act, 1995 to align with the UN CRPD (ratified by India in 2007). It expands disability categories from 7 to 21, mandates 5% reservation (up from 3%) in government jobs, 5% seats in government higher education institutions, and introduces new obligations on the state — including accessible infrastructure, supported decision-making, and independent living support. A "benchmark disability" of 40% is required for most benefits.</li>
<li><strong>UN Convention on Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD):</strong> Adopted in 2006, the UNCRPD shifts the conceptual framework from a medical model (disability as individual impairment to be cured) to a social model (disability arising from society's failure to accommodate diverse needs). India ratified the UNCRPD in 2007. Article 19 of UNCRPD mandates states to ensure that persons with disabilities can live independently and are included in the community — a provision that directly applies to the Kerala story's subjects.</li>
<li><strong>Kerala's demographic profile:</strong> Kerala's demographic transition is the most advanced in India. The state has India's lowest total fertility rate (TFR) at 1.7 (below replacement level of 2.1), an aged sex ratio skewed by male out-migration (Gulf NRKs), and an old-age dependency ratio significantly higher than the national average. The percentage of population above 60 is approximately 16% in Kerala vs. 10% nationally, making elder care, including care of disabled elders and care by elderly parents, a pressing state issue.</li>
<li><strong>Kudumbashree — Kerala's community network:</strong> Kudumbashree is Kerala's state poverty eradication and women's empowerment programme, launched in 1998. It operates through a three-tier structure: Neighbourhood Groups (NHGs) → Area Development Societies (ADS) → Community Development Societies (CDS). Kudumbashree has diversified into childcare (Balasabha), elder care, and disability day-care (Ashraya programme) but lacks the scale and specialised capacity to address severe disability care. It nonetheless represents Kerala's distinctive model of community-managed welfare.</li>
<li><strong>Disability and caregiving — economic dimension:</strong> The WHO estimates that caring for a person with severe disability costs 2.5–4x the typical household per-capita expenditure in developing countries. In India, caregiving is almost entirely borne by female family members (mothers and daughters) without payment, social security, or respite support — making disability care a women's rights and labour issue as much as a disability rights issue. The RPwD Act's provision for "caregiver allowances" exists in principle but has been implemented unevenly across states.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Several of Andhra Pradesh's key agricultural districts — including Prakasam, Kurnool, Anantapur, and parts of Krishna delta — have recorded a significant deficit in south-west monsoon rainfall this kharif season (June–September 2026), threatening standing crops of paddy, groundnut, cotton, and pulses. The IMD (India Meteorological Department) has classified these districts as "deficient" (receiving less than 80% of normal rainfall). Farmers who had planted on credit are facing potential crop loss without commensurate insurance cover — particularly groundnut and cotton farmers in Rayalaseema who are not covered under PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana) because their land is uninsured or because insurance companies have restricted cover in drought-prone districts. The AP government has sought Central assistance under the SDRF (State Disaster Response Fund) and invoked the NDRF (National Disaster Response Fund) mechanism for drought relief. UPSC candidates should link this to India's monsoon dependence, PMFBY, the SDRF/NDRF framework, and the challenges of agricultural risk management.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Kharif cropping season — India:</strong> The kharif season runs from June to September/October, coinciding with the south-west monsoon. Major kharif crops include paddy (rice), sorghum (jowar), bajra (pearl millet), maize, cotton, groundnut, soybean, sugarcane, and pulses like arhar/tur. Andhra Pradesh's kharif output is dominated by paddy in the Krishna-Godavari delta and groundnut/cotton in Rayalaseema. A deficient monsoon can reduce area sown, increase pest/disease incidence, and trigger distress migration.</li>
<li><strong>IMD rainfall classification:</strong> The IMD classifies seasonal rainfall deviation from long-period average (LPA) as: Excess (>119% of LPA), Normal (96–119%), Below Normal (90–95%), Deficient (80–89%), Large Deficient (<80%). Districts in the "deficient" and "large deficient" categories are eligible for drought declaration by the state, enabling access to relief funds. The NDMA's Drought Management Manual (2009) provides the protocol for drought assessment (Remote Sensing, soil moisture, crop condition surveys).</li>
<li><strong>Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY):</strong> Launched in 2016, PMFBY provides comprehensive crop insurance covering pre-sowing to post-harvest losses including natural calamities, pests, and diseases. Premium is capped at 2% for kharif, 1.5% for rabi, and 5% for commercial/horticultural crops — remaining premium is shared between Centre and states (50:50 or as negotiated). Despite being the world's largest crop insurance programme by enrolled farmers, PMFBY has faced criticism for low claim settlement ratios, enrolment gaps in marginal farmers, and insurance company exits from drought-prone districts.</li>
<li><strong>SDRF and NDRF — drought relief mechanism:</strong> The State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) is constituted from Centre's grants (75% Centre, 25% State for general states) and is the primary funding for disaster relief. NDRF (National Disaster Response Fund) provides additional assistance beyond SDRF capacity, subject to a joint central team assessment (Inter-Ministerial Central Team, IMCT). Drought is a "notified disaster" under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, but the NDRF norms restrict drought relief to specific items (seeds, food grain, fodder), leaving a large share of farmer loss uncompensated.</li>
<li><strong>Rayalaseema — chronic drought geography:</strong> Rayalaseema (comprising Kurnool, Nandyal, Anantapur, YSR Kadapa, and Chittoor districts) is one of India's most drought-prone regions. It receives average annual rainfall of 500–700 mm — significantly below the national average of ~1,170 mm — and is heavily dependent on Krishna river irrigation (Tungabhadra reservoir, Srisailam dam). Anantapur consistently records among India's lowest annual rainfall. Farmer distress (including debt-driven suicides) in Rayalaseema during drought years has been a recurrent policy and political crisis.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India and Canada have resumed Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) negotiations, targeting a conclusion by end-2026, following a period of severe bilateral tension after the Hardeep Singh Nijjar killing in Surrey, British Columbia (June 2023), which Canada attributed to Indian government agents and India denied. Both sides acknowledge that trade and economic interests are too large to be indefinitely frozen by diplomatic fallout — bilateral trade stands at approximately $10 billion annually, with Canada a source of pulses, potash (critical for India's fertiliser sector), and education services (over 3.5 lakh Indian students study in Canada). UPSC candidates should understand India's FTA/CEPA policy history and the interplay between diplomatic tensions and trade negotiations.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>CEPA vs FTA — structural difference:</strong> A Free Trade Agreement (FTA) primarily reduces tariffs on goods. A Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) is broader — covering goods, services, investment, intellectual property, government procurement, and sometimes labour standards and e-commerce. India's CEPAs include those with Japan (2011), South Korea (2010), UAE (2022), and Australia (interim, 2022). A CEPA with Canada — a G7 country with large Indian diaspora and student population — would significantly upgrade the economic relationship.</li>
<li><strong>India-Canada Nijjar crisis (2023):</strong> Hardeep Singh Nijjar, a Khalistani separatist leader designated a terrorist by India, was shot dead in Surrey, BC in June 2023. Canadian PM Justin Trudeau alleged "credible allegations" of Indian government involvement, which India denied, calling Nijjar a terrorist and Canada a safe harbour for Khalistani extremism. Both countries expelled senior diplomats. Canada subsequently recalled its High Commissioner from India. New talks under Canada's new government (post-Trudeau Liberal government) have cautiously reopened trade channels.</li>
<li><strong>India-Canada economic interdependence:</strong> Canada is India's 18th largest trade partner (bilateral trade ~$10 billion). Key Canadian exports to India: pulses (Canada supplies ~70% of India's imported yellow peas and lentils, critical for dal production), potash (used in fertilisers — India imports ~4 million tonnes annually, Canada has world's largest reserves), and timber. India's exports to Canada: pharmaceuticals, textiles, gems. Indian students in Canada (3.5 lakh+) generate approximately CAD 2 billion in education revenue for Canada annually.</li>
<li><strong>Khalistan and India's national security concerns:</strong> India has consistently raised concerns with Canada about Khalistani separatist activities on Canadian soil — rallies calling for a Sikh homeland separate from India ("Khalistan"), vandalism of Hindu temples, and alleged fundraising for militant activities in Punjab. Canada, with its large Sikh diaspora (approximately 7.7 lakh Sikh-Canadians, a significant political constituency), has been reluctant to designate Khalistani groups as terrorist organisations. India lists several Khalistani groups under UAPA's designated terrorist organisation schedule.</li>
<li><strong>India's FTA/CEPA strategy — recent direction:</strong> After a decade of minimal FTA activity (post-2011), India resumed active FTA negotiations post-2021 under a recalibrated strategy: prioritising agreements that offer substantial market access for Indian goods (especially in manufacturing and services) while protecting sensitive sectors (agriculture, dairy). India concluded CEPAs with UAE and Australia (interim ECTA) in 2022 and is in advanced talks with the EU, UK, and Canada. India withdrew from RCEP in 2019 citing concerns about market access for Chinese goods and agricultural imports from ASEAN.</li>
</ul>

India's 5G spectrum was auctioned in 2022 (Jio, Airtel, and Vodafone-Idea were bidders); Jio and Airtel have commercially launched 5G in major cities. <p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Agriculture</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Agriculture → Sugar sector; MSP; Fair and Remunerative Price; essential commodities; price stabilisation
- GS Paper III → Economy → Food inflation management; Essential Commodities Act; buffer stocks

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution has decided to shift from monthly to fortnightly sugar release quota instructions to sugar mills, citing the need for more responsive monitoring of retail sugar prices amid inflationary pressures. The government uses its powers under the Essential Commodities Act (ECA), 1955 to regulate the quantity of sugar that mills can release into the market each month — controlling supply to manage retail prices. The change to fortnightly releases allows the government to adjust supply more nimbly in response to festival-season demand spikes. India is the world's largest consumer and second-largest producer of sugar (after Brazil). Domestic sugar production in 2025–26 has been affected by below-normal sugarcane acreage in Maharashtra and Karnataka — the southern states that produce the highest-quality cane for white sugar. The government has also restricted sugar exports to maintain domestic supply adequacy. UPSC candidates should understand the Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) mechanism, the Sugar Development Fund, and India's sugar export-import dynamics.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Essential Commodities Act (ECA), 1955:</strong> The ECA empowers the Central government to designate any commodity as an "essential commodity" and regulate its production, supply, and distribution including setting price controls, restricting stock holding, and mandating sales — to ensure equitable distribution and price stability. Sugar, edible oils, pulses, and petroleum products are among regularly notified commodities. The ECA has been amended multiple times; the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020 raised the threshold for invoking stock limit orders (requiring extraordinary price rise), but the 2020 amendment's provisions have since been modified in response to price spikes.</li>
<li><strong>Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) for sugarcane:</strong> The FRP is the minimum price that sugar mills must pay farmers for sugarcane, fixed by the CCEA (Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs) annually on the recommendation of CACP (Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices). FRP replaced the Statutory Minimum Price (SMP) in 2009 following the Rangarajan Committee report. FRP is linked to recovery rate (sucrose content per tonne of cane) — mills pay higher if recovery exceeds the base rate. Delayed payments by mills are a persistent problem, creating farmer-mill disputes especially in UP and Maharashtra.</li>
<li><strong>India's sugar economy — production and export:</strong> India produces 30–35 million tonnes of sugar annually and exports 5–10 million tonnes in surplus years — making it a significant player in global sugar markets (world production ~180–185 million tonnes). Brazil is the dominant global exporter. India's sugar exports have been linked to subsidies (Production Assistance, Buffer Stock subsidy) that have drawn countervailing duty challenges from Australia, Brazil, and Guatemala at the WTO. The SCM Agreement (Subsidies and Countervailing Measures) limits production-linked export subsidies.</li>
<li><strong>Sugarcane and ethanol blending — energy link:</strong> India's National Biofuel Policy (2018, revised 2022) targets 20% ethanol blending in petrol by 2025. Sugarcane-based ethanol is a primary feedstock. The government has created a separate ethanol procurement mechanism allowing mills to divert B-heavy molasses and sugarcane juice to ethanol production, reducing sugar supply and supporting cane prices simultaneously. This creates a direct link between India's sugar policy and its energy transition strategy — when ethanol blending ramps up, less sugarcane converts to sugar, tightening the domestic sugar balance.</li>
<li><strong>Sugar price regulation mechanism:</strong> The government sets monthly release quotas for each sugar mill (the "Levy Sugar" system was abolished in 2013; the current system uses "ex-mill price" monitoring and release quota controls). DFPD (Department of Food and Public Distribution) monitors retail prices daily in major consuming centres and adjusts release quotas or open market sales from buffer stocks to dampen price spikes. The National Cooperative Exports Ltd (NCEL) coordinates state-level interventions. During festival seasons (Diwali, Dussehra), demand spikes require proactive supply management.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  ECLGS 5.0 Enhances MSME Credit Limit; Scheme Extended Through March 2027
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Industry</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 11 · Business & Economy</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → MSME sector; credit guarantee schemes; working capital; financial inclusion

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The government has launched the Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme (ECLGS) 5.0, enhancing the credit guarantee limit for MSMEs and extending the scheme through March 2027. The ECLGS was originally launched in May 2020 as part of the Atmanirbhar Bharat package to provide collateral-free, government-guaranteed loans to COVID-affected MSMEs. Subsequent iterations (ECLGS 2.0, 3.0, 4.0) expanded the scheme to cover hospitals, healthcare, and stressed sectors. ECLGS 5.0 focuses on manufacturing MSMEs seeking growth capital post-COVID recovery, with enhanced per-borrower limits and a dedicated tranche for women-owned MSMEs. As of March 2026, ECLGS had provided approximately ₹3.7 lakh crore in guaranteed credit to 1.3+ crore MSME accounts, making it the world's largest credit guarantee scheme by disbursement in its first five years. India's MSME sector employs approximately 11 crore people and contributes about 30% of GDP and 45% of exports — its credit access remains a persistent challenge. UPSC candidates should understand the MSME definition, CGTMSE, and India's MSME policy ecosystem.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>ECLGS — Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme:</strong> Launched under Atmanirbhar Bharat (May 2020), ECLGS provided 100% government guarantee (through NCGTC — National Credit Guarantee Trustee Company) on additional working capital term loans to eligible businesses, capped at 20% of outstanding credit or ₹5 crore (later enhanced). Interest was capped, and borrowers got a 12-month principal moratorium. No collateral was required. Banks and NBFCs extended loans while NCGTC covered NPA risk — enabling risk-averse lenders to reach MSMEs they would otherwise decline.</li>
<li><strong>MSME definition (Udyam Registration):</strong> India revised MSME definitions in 2020. Micro enterprise: investment up to ₹1 crore, turnover up to ₹5 crore. Small: investment up to ₹10 crore, turnover up to ₹50 crore. Medium: investment up to ₹50 crore, turnover up to ₹250 crore. The Udyam Registration Portal (replacing Udyog Aadhaar) is the mandatory registration platform. As of 2026, over 5 crore MSMEs are registered on Udyam — though the actual MSME universe is estimated at 6–7 crore enterprises.</li>
<li><strong>Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE):</strong> CGTMSE was established in 2000 by SIDBI and the Ministry of MSME to provide guarantee cover to banks and MFIs for collateral-free loans to MSMEs up to ₹5 crore (recently enhanced). CGTMSE is distinct from ECLGS's NCGTC vehicle — CGTMSE is the regular, peacetime guarantee scheme; ECLGS was an emergency scheme. Both aim to address India's persistent MSME credit gap (estimated at ₹20–25 lakh crore — the difference between formal credit demand and supply).</li>
<li><strong>MSME sector in India — economic significance:</strong> MSMEs contribute approximately 30% of GDP (GVA), 45% of total exports, and employ 11 crore people — the second-largest employment segment after agriculture. The sector is characterised by high informality, limited access to formal credit, technology gaps, and supply chain integration challenges. Key MSME-centric policies include the Public Procurement Policy for MSEs (mandating 25% government procurement from MSMEs, with 4% from SC/ST MSEs), and GeM (Government e-Marketplace) which has channelled ₹5+ lakh crore in orders to MSMEs.</li>
<li><strong>Women MSMEs — policy focus:</strong> Women-owned MSMEs account for approximately 20% of registered MSMEs in India but face compounded access barriers (collateral, social capital, mobility). ECLGS 5.0's dedicated tranche follows earlier targeted interventions: Mahila Udyam Nidhi (SIDBI scheme for women entrepreneurs), Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana's Kishore and Tarun loan tiers for women, and Stand-Up India (2016) which mandates one SC/ST and one woman entrepreneur loan per bank branch. The Niti Aayog's WEE (Women Entrepreneurship Platform) provides advisory and market linkage support.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  FSSAI Mandates Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labels on Packaged Foods from 2027
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Health</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Industry</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 11 · Business & Economy</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Public health; food safety; consumer rights; non-communicable diseases
- GS Paper III → Economy → Food processing industry; FSSAI regulations; packaging norms

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) has finalised regulations mandating Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labels (FOPNL) on all packaged food products with effect from January 2027, following years of industry lobbying and regulatory deliberation. The labels will flag foods "high in" sugar, salt, or saturated fat using a standardised warning symbol — similar to Chile's black octagon warning label model, which is regarded as one of the world's most effective food labelling systems. The regulation is aimed at addressing India's rapid increase in non-communicable diseases (NCDs) — obesity, type-2 diabetes, and hypertension — linked to ultra-processed food consumption. India's packaged food market is estimated at ₹5 lakh crore and growing at 12–15% annually. Food industry lobbies had pushed for a "traffic light" or "star rating" system that is less discouraging of purchase — FSSAI's choice of warning labels represents a public health-first approach. UPSC candidates should know FSSAI's statutory mandate, the NCD burden in India, and the consumer rights framework.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>FSSAI — Food Safety and Standards Authority of India:</strong> FSSAI is a statutory body under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (FSS Act), under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. It sets standards for food products, regulates food businesses, grants licenses, and enforces food safety laws. FSSAI's Chairperson and CEO are appointed by the Central government. It has jurisdiction over all food sold in India — manufactured, imported, or processed. FSSAI has been progressive on public health labelling, having earlier mandated calorie and nutrient declarations on back labels.</li>
<li><strong>Front-of-Pack Labelling (FOPL) — global context:</strong> Chile pioneered black octagonal warning labels in 2016 for foods exceeding thresholds for sugar, saturated fat, sodium, and calories. Studies show that warning labels (negative framing) are more effective at reducing purchase intent than positive "star rating" labels. Mexico, Peru, and Uruguay have adopted similar systems. WHO recommends "interpretive" FOPL (warning or traffic light) over "reductive" nutrient information tables for public health effectiveness in low-and-middle-income countries.</li>
<li><strong>India's NCD burden:</strong> Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) — cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases — account for approximately 65% of deaths in India (WHO 2025). Diabetes prevalence has crossed 100 million adults in India (ICMR-INDIAB study 2023), making India the "diabetes capital" of the world. Ultra-processed food (UPF) consumption is associated with increased NCD risk — Indian children and adolescents are among the fastest-growing consumers of packaged snacks, beverages, and instant noodles.</li>
<li><strong>Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and food labelling:</strong> The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 strengthened consumer rights in India, including the right to information — the right to be informed about the quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard, and price of goods. Misleading food labels (e.g. "natural," "sugar-free" when not technically accurate) are actionable under both the FSS Act and the Consumer Protection Act. The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), established under the 2019 Act, can take suo motu cognisance of food labelling violations.</li>
<li><strong>Food processing industry and labelling compliance costs:</strong> India's food processing sector is the 6th largest in the world and is a priority sector under the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme (₹10,900 crore allocated for Food Processing PLI, 2021–27). New FOPNL regulations require reformulation of existing products (to stay below "high in" thresholds) or relabelling. Small and medium food businesses (registered under FSSAI as "basic" or "state licence" holders) face compliance challenges — FSSAI has typically provided phased timelines for smaller manufacturers.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Major political parties and factions in Bangladesh — including representatives of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) and several Islamist groupings — have signed a political reconciliation charter in Mecca, Saudi Arabia, brokered under OIC (Organisation of Islamic Cooperation) facilitation. The charter, dubbed the "Mecca Charter," commits the signatories to a shared democratic roadmap for Bangladesh ahead of elections expected in early 2027, following the mass uprising of August 2024 that ended Sheikh Hasina's government and led to her exile in India. The BNP — which boycotted elections in 2014 and 2018 under Hasina's Awami League — is seeking a return to electoral politics through this cross-party agreement. For India, the Bangladesh political transition is consequential: India had a strong relationship with Sheikh Hasina's Awami League government and has concerns about Bangladesh's new political direction — particularly regarding the role of Islamist parties, relations with China, and border security including the Rohingya situation and cattle smuggling routes. UPSC candidates must understand India-Bangladesh bilateral dimensions comprehensively.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Bangladesh's August 2024 political transition:</strong> Sheikh Hasina's Awami League government, in power since 2009, faced mass student-led protests against a quota system for government jobs in July–August 2024. The protests evolved into a broader uprising against authoritarian governance. On August 5, 2024, Hasina resigned and fled to India, where she remains in exile. An interim government led by Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus was installed, tasked with constitutional reform and organising free elections. The political landscape remains polarised between Awami League supporters and the opposition BNP-Jamaat alliance.</li>
<li><strong>India-Bangladesh relations — key dimensions:</strong> India and Bangladesh share a 4,156 km border, making it India's longest land border with any country. Key bilateral issues include: the Teesta river water-sharing dispute (unresolved since 2011); Rohingya refugee management (Bangladesh hosts ~1 million Rohingya); transit and connectivity (Bangladeshi goods use Indian railways to reach NE India); illegal immigration; Border haats; and strategic concerns about Chinese presence in Bangladesh (Chittagong port, infrastructure projects). The Hasina government was pro-India; the post-Hasina situation is more uncertain.</li>
<li><strong>BNP (Bangladesh Nationalist Party) — history:</strong> BNP was founded by Ziaur Rahman in 1978. It has alternated in power with the Awami League in Bangladesh's post-1991 democratic era. BNP traditionally has a closer relationship with Pakistan and China compared to the Awami League (which maintained strong ties with India and has secular-nationalist roots). Khaleda Zia, BNP's chairperson, was imprisoned for corruption under Hasina's government. BNP's return to mainstream politics has significant implications for India's strategic interests in Bangladesh.</li>
<li><strong>Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC):</strong> The OIC is the second-largest intergovernmental organisation after the UN, with 57 member states across four continents, headquartered in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. It was established in 1969 following the arson attack on Al-Aqsa mosque. Saudi Arabia plays a leading role in OIC. India is not a member (was offered observer status but has not accepted due to Pakistan's presence). OIC has occasionally made statements on the Kashmir issue that India strongly objects to.</li>
<li><strong>Teesta river dispute — India-Bangladesh:</strong> The Teesta originates in Sikkim, flows through West Bengal, and enters Bangladesh. Bangladesh needs Teesta water for kharif irrigation in its northern districts. A sharing agreement (50:50) was almost finalised in 2011 but West Bengal CM Mamata Banerjee withheld consent, citing Bengal's own irrigation needs. The dispute has remained unresolved — a significant irritant in India-Bangladesh ties. China has offered Bangladesh a Teesta River Management and Restoration Project — a strategic investment India views with concern.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
US President Donald Trump's self-imposed 6-month deadline for Iran to agree to a new nuclear deal has expired without an agreement, prompting a shift in US rhetoric toward hinting at military options while simultaneously keeping diplomatic channels open through Oman's mediation. Iran has accelerated uranium enrichment to 60% purity (close to weapons-grade 90%), responding to Trump's "maximum pressure" sanctions policy reimposed after the US withdrew from JCPOA in 2018. Europe, Russia, and China — the other JCPOA signatories — have called for restraint. For India, an Iran confrontation carries significant energy security risks: India has historically imported up to 10–12% of its crude oil from Iran (Chabahar port is also a key strategic investment). US sanctions have already curtailed India-Iran oil trade. An escalation could destabilise energy markets and directly affect India's oil import bill and rupee-dollar management. UPSC candidates must understand the JCPOA framework, India's Iran-balancing act, and the Chabahar port's strategic importance.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>JCPOA (Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action), 2015:</strong> The JCPOA was signed between Iran and the P5+1 (US, UK, France, Russia, China, and Germany) in Vienna, July 2015. Iran agreed to limit uranium enrichment to 3.67%, reduce centrifuge numbers, allow IAEA inspections, and cap its heavy water stockpile — in exchange for sanctions relief. The US unilaterally withdrew from JCPOA under Trump (May 2018) and reimposed maximum pressure sanctions. Iran gradually violated JCPOA limits in response. Biden's attempt to revive JCPOA (Vienna talks 2021–22) stalled. Trump's second term has seen no new deal.</li>
<li><strong>Iran's nuclear programme — current status:</strong> Iran's uranium enrichment has reached 60% purity in hexafluoride form at Fordow and Natanz facilities — far above the JCPOA limit of 3.67% but below weapons-grade (90%). The IAEA (International Atomic Energy Agency) — the UN's nuclear watchdog — has limited inspection access as Iran has suspended cooperation with the IAEA Additional Protocol. Experts estimate Iran is within weeks of producing sufficient weapons-grade material for one bomb if it chose to, though weaponisation would take additional months.</li>
<li><strong>India-Iran Chabahar Port:</strong> India has invested in developing the Shahid Beheshti terminal at Chabahar Port in Iran's Sistan-Baluchistan province. Chabahar gives India overland access to Afghanistan and Central Asia bypassing Pakistan. India signed a 10-year contract in 2024 to develop and operate the port. The US has granted India a sanctions waiver for Chabahar specifically, recognising its humanitarian and connectivity value. However, US-Iran tensions threaten to complicate this arrangement — a military strike on Iran would halt Chabahar operations entirely.</li>
<li><strong>India-Iran oil trade and sanctions:</strong> India was Iran's second-largest oil customer before US sanctions were reimposed in 2019. India imported ~23 million tonnes of Iranian oil in 2018–19 at significantly discounted prices (sanctioned oil trades at a discount). After 2019, Indian refiners (IOC, BPCL, MRPL) largely halted Iranian purchases to avoid CAATSA (Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act) penalties on companies doing business with Iran. India's energy mix shifted to Saudi Arabia, UAE, Iraq, and Russia as alternatives.</li>
<li><strong>Oman's mediation role:</strong> Oman has historically served as a secret channel between the US and Iran, including facilitating the preliminary nuclear talks that led to the 2015 JCPOA. Oman's unique position — maintaining diplomatic relations with both the US and Iran, and with a tradition of neutrality in Gulf conflicts — makes it the go-to mediator. The current Omani-facilitated talks follow in this tradition. India also maintains close ties with Oman (a significant remittance source for Kerala and Goa), making the Oman channel geopolitically relevant to India's diplomatic interests.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
China has announced the removal of several senior People's Liberation Army (PLA) officers from the Central Military Commission (CMC) — the supreme military body chaired by Xi Jinping — amid an anti-corruption investigation into procurement irregularities in the Rocket Force (formerly the Second Artillery Corps, which controls China's nuclear and conventional missile arsenal). For India, which faces ongoing tensions with China along the LAC, the internal dynamics of the PLA are strategically significant. A weakened or reorganised Rocket Force could affect the credibility of China's missile deterrence posture — relevant given the Rocket Force's role in China's theatre missile commands aimed at India. UPSC candidates should understand China's civil-military structure, the CMC's role, and the strategic implications of PLA's internal churn.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Central Military Commission (CMC) — China:</strong> The CMC is China's highest military decision-making body. It has constitutional status under both the PRC State Constitution and the CCP Constitution. The CMC Chairmanship is the most powerful military position in China — Xi Jinping has held it since 2012. CMC members include the Chief of the Joint Staff Department, heads of the four PLA service branches (Army, Navy, Air Force, Rocket Force), the CMC Equipment Development Department head, and the Logistics Support Department head. CMC members are elected at the National Party Congress.</li>
<li><strong>PLA Rocket Force — role and structure:</strong> The PLA Rocket Force (PLARF), elevated to full service branch status in 2015 (formerly Second Artillery Corps, est. 1966), controls China's land-based nuclear and conventional ballistic and cruise missiles. It operates China's ICBMs, IRBMs (like the DF-26, capable of reaching Guam), and short-range tactical missiles. The PLARF's "theatre missile" capability — particularly DF-26 and DF-21 anti-ship ballistic missiles — is central to China's anti-access/area denial (A2/AD) strategy in the Pacific and Indian Ocean regions.</li>
<li><strong>Xi Jinping's anti-corruption campaign:</strong> Since 2012, Xi's Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI) has investigated over 4 million officials, including Politburo Standing Committee member Zhou Yongkang and dozens of PLA general officers. The campaign serves dual purposes: genuine corruption control and political consolidation by removing rivals. The removal of defence ministers Li Shangfu (appointed 2023, removed same year) and Wei Fenghe (2022–23) within the same procurement corruption probe suggests systemic rot in PLA arms acquisition — ironically at a time of record defence budget growth.</li>
<li><strong>China's defence budget and PLA modernisation:</strong> China's official defence budget for 2026 is approximately $250 billion (RMB 1.78 trillion) — the world's second-largest after the US. The actual defence spend is estimated by Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) to be 1.5–2x the official figure when R&D and paramilitary costs are included. China's defence spending has grown at 6–8% annually for over a decade, funding rapid modernisation across the PLAN (Navy), PLAAF (Air Force), and PLARF — directly challenging India's defence planning assumptions.</li>
<li><strong>India-China LAC and PLA deployment:</strong> China's PLA Western Theatre Command, based in Chengdu, is responsible for the Sino-Indian border along the LAC. Following the Galwan Valley clash (June 2020, 20 Indian soldiers killed), both sides have built permanent infrastructure along the LAC — roads, helipads, forward bases, and hardened shelters. China has deployed multiple mountain brigades with specialised high-altitude warfare equipment. India has responded with accelerated border road construction (BRO), forward positioning of the Mountain Strike Corps, and additional IAF assets at Leh and eastern sector bases.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Syria's New Government Appoints Kurdish Adviser Amid Northern Territory Tensions
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Security</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 13 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → Middle East geopolitics; Syria post-Assad; Kurdish question; Turkey-US-Syria dynamics

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Syria's new transitional government (established after Bashar al-Assad's fall in December 2024, following the HTS-led rebel advance) has appointed a Kurdish political figure as a senior adviser for northern Syria affairs, in what is seen as a conciliatory gesture toward the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) and the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (AANES), popularly known as Rojava. The SDF, dominated by the Kurdish YPG militia and backed by the US, controls approximately one-third of Syria's territory including much of its oil-producing northeast. Turkey views the YPG as synonymous with the PKK (Kurdistan Workers' Party), designated a terrorist organisation by Turkey, the US, and the EU. The appointment signals the Syrian government's attempt to integrate Kurdish-held territory into a future constitutional framework — but Turkey has threatened military action if Kurdish autonomous governance is formalised. India's interest in Syria is primarily through energy (Syria was a minor oil source pre-war), diaspora welfare, and the broader Middle East stability calculus that affects India's energy security, remittances, and regional trade routes.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Syria's political transition (2024–25):</strong> Bashar al-Assad's government collapsed in December 2024 as the Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS) — a jihadist coalition formerly linked to Al-Qaeda — led a rapid military offensive from Idlib. Assad fled to Russia. A transitional government under HTS leader Ahmed al-Sharaa (Abu Mohammed al-Jolani) has sought international recognition and has made pragmatic overtures to Western governments and regional powers including Saudi Arabia and Turkey. The transitional government controls most of western and central Syria; the SDF controls the northeast.</li>
<li><strong>Kurdish question in Syria — AANES (Rojava):</strong> The Syrian civil war enabled the Kurdish YPG/SDFto establish de facto autonomous governance in northeastern Syria (2013 onwards), which they call "Rojava" or AANES. AANES operates its own administration, military, and educational system based on the philosophy of Abdullah Öcalan (imprisoned PKK founder). The SDF partnered with the US to defeat ISIS in eastern Syria, giving it US military backing and international legitimacy — but also making it a target for Turkey.</li>
<li><strong>Turkey's regional policy and Kurdish issue:</strong> Turkey has conducted three major military operations in northern Syria (Operation Olive Branch 2018, Peace Spring 2019, and subsequent operations) targeting YPG/SDF positions, which Turkey equates with PKK terrorism. Turkey is a NATO member and its operations have complicated US-Turkey relations within the Alliance. Any post-Assad constitutional settlement that formally recognises Kurdish autonomy in Syria would be seen by Turkey as a direct threat to its territorial integrity given Turkey's own large Kurdish population.</li>
<li><strong>India-Syria relations:</strong> India maintained diplomatic relations with the Assad government throughout the Syrian civil war — one of the few democracies to do so. India abstained on key UN resolutions calling for Assad to step down. India's embassy in Damascus was suspended during the worst fighting but India evacuated over 4,000 Indian nationals through Operation Rahat (2015). India's interest in Syria is primarily strategic: it opposes externally imposed regime changes and values Syria as part of the broader West Asian connectivity for trade and remittances (over 8 million Indians in the Gulf).</li>
<li><strong>ISIS/ISIL — current status:</strong> The Islamic State (ISIS/ISIL) was territorially defeated in Syria and Iraq by 2019 (fall of Baghouz, its last territorial stronghold). However, ISIS continues as an underground insurgency — conducting guerrilla attacks in Syria's Deir ez-Zor, Iraq's Anbar and Diyala provinces, and increasingly in the Sahel (ISIS-Sahel) and South/Southeast Asia (ISIS-K in Afghanistan, ISIS-affiliated cells in India). The SDF's holding of thousands of ISIS prisoners in camps like al-Hol (70,000+ inmates including women and children) represents a continuing security risk.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Iran Signals Readiness for 'Conditional' Nuclear Talks; Diplomacy Enters New Phase
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Diplomacy</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 13 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → Nuclear non-proliferation; NPT; JCPOA revival; US-Iran-India triangular dynamics
- GS Paper III → Economy → Oil markets; energy security; India's crude oil import strategy

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Separately from the Trump deadline story, Iran's Foreign Ministry has issued a statement indicating that Tehran is open to a new nuclear dialogue with the P5+1 if the US first provides written "guarantees of good faith" — including a partial sanctions rollback and a commitment not to withdraw from any future agreement. Iran's Supreme Leader Khamenei has privately indicated to European diplomats through Oman's channel that Iran's "red line" is maintaining the right to civilian uranium enrichment, but it is willing to cap enrichment at 20% and allow enhanced IAEA inspections. The distinction from the earlier Trump deadline story is that this is a detailed diplomatic signalling piece, covering Iran's internal political consensus-building process (Revolutionary Guard vs. Reformist camps) and the EU's role as intermediary. For India, the diplomatic dynamics matter: if sanctions are relaxed, India could resume discounted Iranian oil imports, reducing its import bill. India has also been informally exploring an Indian Rupee–Iranian Rial trade settlement mechanism to bypass dollar-clearing sanctions. UPSC candidates should understand Iran's nuclear programme governance, NPT obligations, and India's multilateral nuclear engagement.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Iran's political structure — Supreme Leader vs. President:</strong> Iran's theocratic governance structure (Velayat-e Faqih — guardianship of the jurist) vests ultimate authority in the Supreme Leader (currently Ali Khamenei, since 1989). The elected President handles day-to-day governance but nuclear and foreign policy decisions require Supreme Leader approval. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) is directly answerable to the Supreme Leader and has its own nuclear/missile programme parallel to the civilian Atomic Energy Organization of Iran (AEOI). The IRGC's economic interests (vast business empire) make sanctions complicated to implement — targeting the IRGC without damaging the civilian economy.</li>
<li><strong>Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT):</strong> The NPT (1968, entered force 1970) is the cornerstone of the global nuclear non-proliferation regime. It has three pillars: non-proliferation (non-nuclear states cannot acquire nuclear weapons), disarmament (nuclear states must work toward disarmament), and peaceful use of nuclear energy. Iran is an NPT signatory and claims its enrichment programme is for peaceful civilian nuclear power (Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant). The IAEA monitors NPT compliance; Iran has periodically restricted IAEA inspector access — a key sticking point in nuclear diplomacy.</li>
<li><strong>India and Iran — Rupee-Rial trade settlement:</strong> India has explored bilateral currency trade mechanisms with sanctioned countries to avoid dollar clearing systems. With Iran, India previously settled about 55% of oil payments through UCO Bank (India) and Parsian Bank (Iran) in rupees — a mechanism that broke down after 2019 sanctions. Any revival would require a legal framework under FEMA (Foreign Exchange Management Act) and coordinated diplomatic effort to avoid CAATSA secondary sanctions on Indian banks engaging with designated Iranian entities.</li>
<li><strong>IAEA — International Atomic Energy Agency:</strong> The IAEA is a UN intergovernmental organisation (established 1957, Vienna) that promotes peaceful nuclear energy use and verifies nuclear non-proliferation commitments through safeguards (inspection and monitoring of nuclear materials and facilities). The IAEA Board of Governors (35 members) and the Director-General (currently Rafael Grossi) manage the organisation. Non-compliance cases are referred to the UN Security Council — Iran has been referred three times. IAEA's Additional Protocol (1997) provides more intrusive inspection rights, which Iran suspended in 2021.</li>
<li><strong>India's nuclear deal — India-US Civil Nuclear Agreement (123 Agreement, 2008):</strong> India is not a signatory to the NPT (along with Pakistan, Israel, and South Sudan). India conducted nuclear tests in 1974 (Pokhran-I) and 1998 (Pokhran-II). The India-US Civil Nuclear Agreement (2008) gave India access to civilian nuclear trade while maintaining its nuclear weapons programme outside IAEA safeguards — a unique "exception" to the global non-proliferation regime. India's N-deal required a special waiver from the 45-member Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG). India's bid for NSG membership has been blocked by China's objections on the grounds that India is not an NPT signatory.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Hong Kong Court Convicts Civil Society Leaders for Organising Tiananmen Vigil
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 13 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → China-Hong Kong; One Country Two Systems; democracy and human rights in international relations; India's China policy
- GS Paper II → Governance → Civil liberties; freedom of assembly; authoritarian governance models

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A Hong Kong court has convicted several former leaders of the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements of China — the organisation that organised the annual candlelight vigil commemorating the Tiananmen Square massacre (June 4, 1989) for over 30 years — under the National Security Law (NSL) imposed by Beijing in June 2020. The defendants were charged with acting as agents of foreign forces. The convictions mark another milestone in the dismantling of Hong Kong's civil society post-NSL. The annual June 4 vigil, once attended by tens of thousands and the largest Tiananmen commemoration outside mainland China, has been banned since 2020 (initially on COVID grounds, subsequently on security grounds). The convictions have drawn strong condemnation from the US, EU, and UK — which point to violations of the "One Country, Two Systems" framework guaranteed under the Sino-British Joint Declaration (1984) until 2047. For UPSC, this is relevant to discussions on democratic backsliding, the legal evolution of Hong Kong's status, and India's careful calibration of China criticism given the LAC standoff context.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Tiananmen Square massacre (June 4, 1989):</strong> On June 4, 1989, the Chinese People's Liberation Army violently suppressed pro-democracy protests in Beijing's Tiananmen Square, killing an estimated 1,000–10,000 people (the exact figure remains contested and classified by China). The protests had begun in April 1989 as student-led demonstrations calling for political reform. The event is one of the most significant moments in modern Chinese political history — commemorated globally but banned from public memory on mainland China. Hong Kong was the last place under Chinese sovereignty where June 4 commemorations were permitted.</li>
<li><strong>Hong Kong National Security Law (NSL), 2020:</strong> Beijing enacted the NSL in June 2020 after a year of Hong Kong protests (2019 extradition bill protests). The NSL criminalises secession, subversion, terrorism, and collusion with foreign forces — with maximum penalties of life imprisonment and trials that can be held on the mainland. The NSL was enacted by the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (NPC) and inserted directly into Hong Kong's Basic Law, bypassing Hong Kong's legislature — an action that legal experts say violated the Joint Declaration's guarantee of Hong Kong's autonomy.</li>
<li><strong>One Country, Two Systems — legal basis:</strong> "One Country, Two Systems" (OCTS) is the constitutional framework under which Hong Kong and Macau were returned to China. It was conceived by Deng Xiaoping and codified in the Sino-British Joint Declaration (1984) — a binding international treaty — and Hong Kong's Basic Law (1990). Under OCTS, Hong Kong was to retain its common law legal system, free press, freedom of assembly, and separate economic system until at least 2047. Critics argue the NSL has fundamentally undermined OCTS a full 22 years before the promised deadline.</li>
<li><strong>Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements:</strong> Founded in 1989 in response to the Tiananmen massacre, the Hong Kong Alliance organised the annual June 4 vigil in Victoria Park for 32 consecutive years (1990–2020, cancelled COVID 2020–21, banned from 2021). At its peak, the vigil attracted 180,000 people. The Alliance was disbanded in September 2021 after its leaders were arrested under the NSL. Its demands — accountability for Tiananmen, democratic reform in China, one person one vote for Hong Kong — were considered legal free speech under Hong Kong's pre-NSL legal framework.</li>
<li><strong>India's position on Hong Kong and human rights in China:</strong> India has generally avoided direct criticism of China's Hong Kong policies, balancing its own sensitivity to external interference in domestic affairs (Kashmir), its economic interdependence with China, and the LAC security context. India abstained on the 2022 UN Human Rights Council vote on whether to debate China's treatment of Uyghurs in Xinjiang. India's China policy follows a "calibrated engagement" approach — maintaining diplomatic contact while firmness on boundary issues — rather than a human rights-first approach, distinguishing it from Western democracies.</li>
</ul>
</div>
