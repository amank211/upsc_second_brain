  157 killed in Nepal ash flood, 133 Indians among hundreds missing

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Geography → Natural disasters; glacial lake outburst floods; Himalayan hydrology
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Nepal bilateral; consular assistance; diaspora protection

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A devastating rock-and-ice landslide on the Nepal-China border triggered a catastrophic flood along the Bhotekoshi River system, killing at least 157 people and leaving hundreds missing, including 133 Indian nationals. The landslide, described as an ash-type glacial event, dammed the Lhende River tributary before the natural dam burst, sending a massive surge downstream through populated valleys in Nepal's Sindhupalchok district. The incident highlights the extreme glacial hazard zone in the high Himalayas, where climate change is accelerating permafrost thaw and glacier retreat. The large number of missing Indians underscores the scale of cross-border labour migration from northern India into Nepal and onward. India's Ministry of External Affairs activated consular channels to coordinate with Nepali authorities on search, rescue, and identification. The disaster has renewed focus on early-warning systems for glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs) in the Hindu Kush Himalayan region.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF):</strong> A sudden release of water from a glacial lake formed by a natural dam of ice, moraine, or landslide debris. GLOFs are among the most destructive geophysical hazards in the Himalayan region; India's National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) has designated them a priority risk under the National Disaster Management Plan 2019.</li>
<li><strong>Bhotekoshi River:</strong> A trans-boundary Himalayan river rising in the Tibet Autonomous Region and entering Nepal before joining the Sun Koshi. The Bhotekoshi corridor is a critical route for the China-Nepal Arniko Highway and is historically prone to landslide-dam events.</li>
<li><strong>Hindu Kush Himalaya Assessment (ICIMOD, 2019):</strong> A landmark scientific report projected that even if global warming is limited to 1.5°C, the Hindu Kush Himalaya could lose one-third of its glaciers by 2100; at current trajectories (3–4°C), two-thirds would disappear, dramatically increasing GLOF frequency and intensity.</li>
<li><strong>India-Nepal Friendship Treaty (1950) and consular relations:</strong> Indian nationals in Nepal enjoy a special status under the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, which permits free movement across the open border. This open-border arrangement, while facilitating livelihood migration, also complicates disaster-response accounting when Indian workers are caught in natural calamities.</li>
<li><strong>National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP) 2019:</strong> India's comprehensive plan under the Disaster Management Act 2005, mandating state governments to develop Sub-Divisional Disaster Management Plans and to integrate glaciological hazard mapping — particularly for Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh that share the same high-altitude tectonically active terrain.</li>
</ul>
</div>

  Meta agrees to $17-billion settlement in teen addiction case

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Mental health; child welfare; digital rights of minors
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Social media regulation; algorithmic harm; platform accountability

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Meta Platforms agreed to a landmark $17 billion settlement with 47 U.S. states over allegations that Facebook and Instagram were deliberately designed to addict teenage users, harming their mental health. Attorneys general from across the United States alleged that Meta's algorithmic recommendation systems and product features — including infinite scroll, notification engineering, and like counts — were knowingly engineered to maximise engagement among minors at the cost of their psychological wellbeing. The settlement, one of the largest tech-liability payouts in history, does not require Meta to admit liability but commits the company to age-verification measures and design changes. For India, the case carries significant policy implications: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 restricts processing of data of children under 18 and mandates verifiable parental consent, and pending subordinate legislation is expected to operationalise these provisions. Globally, it sets a precedent for state-level enforcement against platform design choices.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDPA):</strong> India's first comprehensive data-protection law, enacted in August 2023. It designates children (persons under 18) as a protected category, requires verifiable parental consent before processing their data, and prohibits tracking or behavioural monitoring of minors. Rules under the Act are pending notification and will specify consent-verification mechanisms.</li>
<li><strong>Algorithmic amplification and mental health:</strong> Peer-reviewed research (e.g., Twenge & Haidt, 2021) has documented a correlation between heavy social-media use by adolescents and increased rates of anxiety, depression, and self-harm. The U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory in 2023 warning of social-media harms to youth mental health, calling for legislative guardrails.</li>
<li><strong>Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA, 1998):</strong> U.S. federal law restricting collection of personal data from children under 13 without parental consent. Critics argue COPPA is outdated and under-enforced for the modern algorithmic era; the Meta settlement reflects states' use of consumer-protection laws to fill this gap.</li>
<li><strong>Product liability for digital platforms:</strong> Traditional product-liability law governs physical goods; digital platforms have historically been shielded in the U.S. by Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act 1996, which grants immunity for third-party content. The Meta litigation bypassed this shield by targeting the platform's own design choices (infinite scroll, notifications) rather than user-generated content — a legally significant distinction.</li>
<li><strong>India's emerging framework for minors online:</strong> Beyond DPDPA, the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules 2021 require significant social media intermediaries to not publish content that could harm minors. India's Parliamentary Standing Committee on Communications (2024) recommended a minimum age of 13 for social media access, mirroring global trends toward age-gating.</li>
</ul>
</div>

  Amend Aadhaar Act to help trace, deport illegals, says Bombay HC
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Judicial review; High Court directions; parliamentary legislation
- GS Paper II → Governance → Aadhaar; biometric identification; UIDAI; illegal immigration

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Bombay High Court, hearing a case involving Afghan national Majid Khan Shah who allegedly obtained Aadhaar using fraudulent documents, directed the Centre and the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) to consider amending the Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act, 2016 to enable tracing and deportation of illegal foreign nationals who have enrolled in the Aadhaar system. The court noted that Section 29 of the Aadhaar Act prohibits sharing of Aadhaar data except in limited circumstances, creating a legal barrier to using the database for immigration enforcement. The judgment raises a constitutional tension: Aadhaar was designed as a resident-based (not citizen-based) identity system to ensure welfare delivery, yet the same universality that enables inclusive governance can be exploited. The court's direction does not immediately change the law; it places a legislative recommendation before Parliament.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Aadhaar Act 2016 — Section 29 (data sharing restriction):</strong> Prohibits UIDAI from sharing an individual's Aadhaar number, biometrics, or demographic information with any agency except in prescribed circumstances (national security, High Court/Supreme Court order under exceptional circumstances). This creates a legal firewall between the welfare-identification function and law-enforcement or immigration-enforcement uses.</li>
<li><strong>Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2018):</strong> The Supreme Court's nine-judge constitutional bench upheld Aadhaar as constitutional but struck down several provisions, including mandatory Aadhaar for private entities. The judgment emphasised that Aadhaar data must be protected by proportionality standards — any data sharing for enforcement must clear a stringent proportionality test.</li>
<li><strong>Resident vs Citizen basis of Aadhaar:</strong> Unlike voter IDs or passports, Aadhaar is issued to all residents (including foreign nationals legally residing in India for 182 days). This design choice was intentional — to cover migrants, refugees, and stateless persons in welfare delivery — but creates ambiguity when illegal foreign nationals enroll using fraudulent documents.</li>
<li><strong>Foreigners Act 1946 and Citizenship Act 1955:</strong> The principal statutory framework for immigration enforcement. Detection, detention, and deportation of illegal foreigners is governed primarily by the Foreigners Act 1946 and the Foreigners Order 1948; National Register of Citizens (NRC) exercises conducted under the Citizenship Act 1955. Neither statute currently interfaces directly with Aadhaar data for enforcement purposes.</li>
<li><strong>UIDAI's verification ecosystem:</strong> Aadhaar enrollment requires documentary proof of identity and address, but the robustness of document verification has been questioned. The UIDAI has introduced facial authentication and AI-based liveness detection to reduce impersonation, yet fraudulent enrollment using forged residency documents remains a documented gap.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Why was Acting CJ of Rajasthan HC not shifted, judge asks CJI
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Judiciary</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Judiciary; appointment and transfer of judges; collegium system; independence of judiciary

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Supreme Court judge Justice Sandeep Mehta wrote three letters to Chief Justice of India (CJI) Surya Kant questioning why he was designated as Acting Chief Justice of the Rajasthan High Court without being formally transferred or elevated, while the substantive post remained vacant. The letters highlight tensions within the judicial administration system regarding the collegium's transfer and appointment powers. The substantive Chief Justice of the Rajasthan HC, Justice Sanjeev Prakash Sharma, is scheduled to retire on September 26, leaving the position vacant. Designating an acting CJ without a proper transfer or recommendation process raises questions about transparency and the exercise of the collegium's powers under Articles 217 and 222 of the Constitution. The episode is significant for the ongoing debate on judicial accountability and the opacity of collegium decision-making.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Collegium System for HC appointments/transfers:</strong> Established by the Three Judges Cases — Second Judges Case (1993) and Third Judges Case (1998). The Supreme Court collegium (CJI + 4 senior-most SC judges) recommends appointments and transfers of High Court judges; the President of India appoints on that recommendation. The collegium's deliberations are not publicly disclosed, which has been a source of criticism.</li>
<li><strong>Article 217 — Appointment of HC Judges:</strong> Every HC judge is appointed by the President after consultation with the CJI, the Governor of the state, and the Chief Justice of the High Court. The Article also governs the appointment of acting judges.</li>
<li><strong>Article 222 — Transfer of HC Judges:</strong> The President may transfer a judge from one HC to another after consultation with the CJI. Transfers are used by the collegium to manage vacancies and perceived conflicts of interest, but have also been criticised as a tool of executive influence over the judiciary.</li>
<li><strong>Acting Chief Justice designation:</strong> When a High Court Chief Justice is on leave or when the post is vacant, the senior-most puisne judge of that HC acts as Chief Justice by convention. Designating a Supreme Court judge as Acting CJ of an HC (rather than a puisne HC judge) is an unusual administrative arrangement that departs from this convention.</li>
<li><strong>National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) and its striking down:</strong> Parliament amended the Constitution in 2014 (99th Constitutional Amendment) to replace the collegium with the NJAC, which would have included the Law Minister. The Supreme Court struck down the NJAC in 2015 (NJAC Case) as unconstitutional, reaffirming judicial primacy in appointments. The debate on collegium transparency continues, with the Supreme Court introducing a memorandum of procedure for limited disclosure of collegium resolutions.</li>
</ul>
</div>

  Meghalaya House passes resolution against uranium mining
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Federalism</span>

  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Industry</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Fifth and Sixth Schedules; tribal rights; federalism; state legislature resolutions
- GS Paper III → Environment → Mining regulation; tribal land rights; environmental clearance

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Meghalaya Legislative Assembly unanimously passed a resolution opposing uranium mining in the state, particularly in the South West Khasi Hills district where the Domiasiat deposit is located. Chief Minister Conrad Sangma moved the resolution, citing fears of radiation contamination, environmental damage, and threats to indigenous communities. Meghalaya's Domiasiat deposit holds an estimated 9.22 million tonnes of uranium ore, representing approximately 16% of India's total identified uranium reserves, making it one of the most significant mineral finds in the Northeast. The state has historically resisted the Atomic Minerals Directorate's (AMD) efforts to begin extraction. Anti-Uranium Day is observed on October 28 each year in solidarity with affected communities. MLA Spility Lyngdoh Langrin and other legislators have long been vocal advocates against mining. The resolution is non-binding on the Union Government, which retains control over atomic minerals under Entry 53 of the Union List.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Sixth Schedule of the Constitution:</strong> Provides for autonomous district councils (ADCs) in tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, and Mizoram. The Khasi Hills Autonomous District Council (KHADC) in Meghalaya has powers over customary law and land management, but mineral rights are reserved for the Union under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act.</li>
<li><strong>Atomic Minerals Directorate (AMD):</strong> A constituent unit of the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE), responsible for survey and exploration of atomic minerals (uranium, thorium, lithium, beryllium, etc.) in India. Uranium is a prescribed substance under the Atomic Energy Act 1962, meaning its extraction and processing can only be undertaken by the Government of India or an entity authorised by it.</li>
<li><strong>Atomic Energy Act 1962 and uranium:</strong> Section 3 of the Act vests ownership of all prescribed substances (including uranium) in the Central Government. This effectively preempts state legislative authority over uranium mining, placing it in the Union List (Entry 53: regulation and development of oilfields and mineral oil resources; mines and minerals).</li>
<li><strong>Domiasiat uranium deposit:</strong> Located in South West Khasi Hills, Meghalaya. Discovered by AMD in the 1980s and estimated at over 9 million tonnes of ore grading ~0.1% U₃O₈. Extraction has been stalled for decades due to tribal resistance, environmental concerns, and legal challenges by local communities and the state government.</li>
<li><strong>State legislature resolutions and Centre-State friction:</strong> A state assembly resolution is a political expression of intent; it does not create binding law. However, under cooperative federalism, the Centre typically engages with such resolutions. In practice, atomic energy policy has been insulated from state politics by the Atomic Energy Act's constitutional coverage under the Union List, making any state veto legally inoperative without statutory amendment.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Congress to hold march against Kaziranga ESZ reduction plan
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Protected areas; eco-sensitive zones; wildlife conservation; biodiversity
- GS Paper II → Governance → Centre-State relations; judicial oversight; wildlife governance

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Assam Pradesh Congress Committee announced a padayatra (foot march) on August 29 to protest the state government's proposal to reduce the Eco-Sensitive Zone (ESZ) around Kaziranga National Park from the recommended 10 kilometres to 1–3 kilometres. Kaziranga, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and home to about two-thirds of the world's one-horned rhinoceros population, is protected under the Wildlife Protection Act 1972. The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) had issued draft ESZ notifications recommending a 10 km buffer to prevent encroachment and human-wildlife conflict. Critics argue that the proposed reduction, which the Assam government supports to enable development in fringe areas, would irreversibly fragment the park's ecological corridor, threatening not only the rhino but also tigers, elephants, and swamp deer.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Eco-Sensitive Zone (ESZ):</strong> A buffer area declared by the MoEFCC under the Environment Protection Act 1986, surrounding national parks and wildlife sanctuaries. The Supreme Court's 2022 order mandated a minimum 1 km ESZ around all protected areas where no ESZ has been declared; the Centre has been issuing final notifications following this order, often contested by states and local populations.</li>
<li><strong>Kaziranga National Park:</strong> Located in Assam, Kaziranga was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985 and is a Project Tiger reserve. It hosts approximately 2,500+ Indian one-horned rhinoceroses (about 70% of the world population), 104 tigers, and 1,100+ elephants. Annual floods are integral to the ecology — they flush out invasive species — but also cause significant human-wildlife conflict.</li>
<li><strong>Wildlife Protection Act 1972:</strong> The principal legislation governing wildlife protection in India, establishing national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, conservation reserves, and community reserves. It prohibits hunting of scheduled animals and regulates activities within protected areas. Amendments in 2022 introduced provisions for CITES implementation and transboundary cooperation.</li>
<li><strong>T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1995–ongoing):</strong> The Supreme Court's landmark forest bench case that has addressed ESZ delineation, among other forest conservation issues. Court-appointed committees like the Central Empowered Committee (CEC) advise the Court on forest and wildlife matters, making judicial oversight a continuous feature of ESZ governance.</li>
<li><strong>Human-wildlife conflict and corridor protection:</strong> Ecological corridors connecting Kaziranga to the Karbi Anglong hills are critical for seasonal wildlife movement. Fragmentation of these corridors through unregulated development in the ESZ has been identified as the primary driver of wildlife deaths on NH-37 (now NH-715) passing through the park's southern boundary.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Anti-corruption mechanisms; CBI; ministerial accountability; tribal welfare fund governance
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Scheduled Tribes; welfare fund misappropriation; institutional accountability

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Karnataka Labour Minister B. Nagendra, facing CBI and Enforcement Directorate chargesheets in connection with the alleged misappropriation of funds from the Valmiki Scheduled Tribe Development Corporation, indicated he may resign from the Cabinet. Governor Thaawarchand Gehlot had earlier forwarded the chargesheets to the Chief Minister's office. The Valmiki ST Development Corporation, set up to channel welfare funds to the Valmiki (Scheduled Tribe) community, allegedly had crores diverted through fictitious accounts and shell entities under Nagendra's watch as the corporation's chairman. The CBI and ED registered cases under the Prevention of Corruption Act and the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) respectively. The episode raises questions about accountability mechanisms for state development corporations handling ST welfare funds and the Governor's role in forwarding Central investigation agency chargesheets to state governments.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Valmiki Scheduled Tribe Development Corporation:</strong> A state government undertaking in Karnataka established to provide financial assistance, scholarships, and livelihood support to members of the Valmiki community, classified as a Scheduled Tribe in Karnataka. ST Development Corporations across India are funded through a mix of state budget allocations and Central grants channelled through the National Scheduled Tribes Finance and Development Corporation (NSTFDC).</li>
<li><strong>Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (as amended 2018):</strong> Governs bribery and corruption offences by public servants in India. The 2018 amendment introduced the concept of "undue advantage" replacing older bribery definitions, and added protections for bona fide decisions by public servants. The CBI registers cases under this Act for corruption involving Central public servants or cases referred by state governments or courts.</li>
<li><strong>Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002 (PMLA):</strong> Empowers the ED to attach, seize, and confiscate proceeds of crime derived from scheduled offences (including corruption). <div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Since mid-March 2026, no new domestic 14.2-kg LPG cylinder connections have been issued by Oil Marketing Companies (OMCs) — IOC, BPCL, and HPCL — because of acute supply constraints caused by the Strait of Hormuz closure during the ongoing West Asia war. Before the war, India imported approximately 60% of its LPG requirement through the Strait of Hormuz; after the strait's effective closure, this channel dried up, reducing LPG availability to a fraction of pre-crisis levels. OMCs are managing the shortage by promoting smaller formats — 5-kg Free Trade LPG (FTL) cylinders and 10-kg composite cylinders — which can be refilled through alternate supply routes. Under-recoveries on subsidised LPG have reportedly tripled as OMCs absorb higher international procurement costs. The Ujjwala scheme's beneficiary households are among the most affected, as the new-connection freeze disproportionately impacts the rural poor who had not yet obtained connections.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY):</strong> Launched in 2016, PMUY provides free LPG connections to women from BPL households, aiming to transition rural families from biomass cooking fuels to clean LPG. As of 2024, over 10.35 crore connections were issued under PMUY. The scheme is pivotal for reducing indoor air pollution and improving women's health — indoor cooking smoke contributes to approximately 4 lakh deaths annually in India.</li>
<li><strong>Strait of Hormuz and India's LPG import dependence:</strong> The Strait of Hormuz — a 33-km-wide chokepoint between Oman and Iran — handles roughly 20% of global oil and 30% of global LNG trade. India imports LPG primarily from Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE; approximately 90% of this supply transits through Hormuz. Any disruption has immediate downstream effects on India's ₹90,000+ crore annual LPG subsidy budget and household energy access.</li>
<li><strong>LPG under-recovery and government subsidies:</strong> Under-recovery is the difference between the cost of supplying LPG and the price charged to consumers. The government pays this as a subsidy to OMCs through the DBTL (Direct Benefit Transfer for LPG) scheme — now called Pahal — which transfers the subsidy directly to consumers' Aadhaar-linked bank accounts. When international LPG prices spike (as during the Hormuz closure), under-recoveries surge, straining the Union Budget.</li>
<li><strong>India's strategic petroleum reserves (SPR):</strong> India has underground SPR facilities at Visakhapatnam, Mangaluru, and Padur with a combined storage capacity of 5.33 million tonnes (roughly 9 days of consumption). The SPR holds crude oil, not LPG, so it does not directly address the LPG shortage — though crude-derived naphtha cracking can produce LPG as a by-product. The Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Limited (ISPRL) manages these facilities under MoPNG.</li>
<li><strong>Free Trade LPG (FTL) and composite cylinders:</strong> FTL cylinders (5 kg) are sold at market prices without subsidy to commercial and retail customers. Composite cylinders (10 kg, made of fibre-reinforced polymer) are lighter and more portable than steel cylinders, making them suited for remote and hilly areas. Promoting FTL and composite cylinders during a shortage effectively shifts the subsidy burden from the household to the consumer, raising equity concerns for the poorest households.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India's Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) firmly rejected the concluding observations of the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD), which raised concerns about the treatment of Scheduled Tribes, Scheduled Castes, and Rohingya refugees in India. Spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal and Solicitor General Tushar Mehta, who led India's delegation, argued that the caste system does not constitute racial discrimination within the meaning of the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD), which forms CERD's mandate. India has consistently maintained this position, rejecting any conflation of caste-based social practices with race-based discrimination under international law. On Rohingya, India reiterated that it is not a signatory to the 1951 Refugee Convention and does not recognise Rohingya as refugees, treating their presence as an illegal immigration issue. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>CERD — Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination:</strong> A treaty body established under the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD, 1965). India ratified ICERD in 1968. CERD reviews periodic state reports and issues concluding observations — assessments and recommendations — that are not legally binding but carry political weight in international human rights discourse.</li>
<li><strong>Caste vs Race debate in international law:</strong> CERD has argued since its 2002 and 2007 sessions that discrimination based on descent (which includes caste) falls within ICERD's scope. India and several other Asian states have resisted this position, contending that caste is a social institution not equivalent to racial discrimination. The Durban Declaration (2001) referenced "descent" as a ground of discrimination, but India rejected paragraphs that explicitly linked caste to race.</li>
<li><strong>India's constitutional protections for SCs/STs:</strong> Articles 15, 16, 17 (abolition of untouchability), 46, 330, 332, 338, 338A, and the Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 and SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 form India's domestic legal architecture for protecting Scheduled Castes and Tribes. India argues these are more comprehensive than ICERD-mandated measures.</li>
<li><strong>Rohingya and India's non-signatory status to the 1951 Refugee Convention:</strong> India has neither signed the 1951 Refugee Convention nor enacted domestic refugee legislation, leaving Rohingya (and other asylum seekers) without a legal framework that distinguishes them from undocumented migrants. The Supreme Court in Mohammad Salimullah v. Union of India (2021) declined to stay deportations of Rohingya, noting the absence of a domestic refugee law. India's position is that national security considerations override humanitarian protection obligations not binding in domestic law.</li>
<li><strong>UN Human Rights Council (UNHRC) and India:</strong> India is an elected member of the UNHRC and actively participates in Universal Periodic Review (UPR) processes. However, India routinely rejects recommendations from Special Rapporteurs and treaty bodies that it considers as interfering in domestic affairs, particularly on caste, minority rights, and freedom of expression. This is consistent with India's broader principle of non-interference in domestic matters in international fora.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**

- GS Paper II → Governance → NITI Aayog; data transparency; ease of doing research

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
NITI Aayog's 'Ease of Doing R&D' report identifies a critical opacity at the heart of India's research funding system: there is no reliable, consolidated map of where research grants actually flow. Approximately 80% of Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF) funds are channelled through IITs, creating a concentration risk and leaving newer universities, state institutions, and private research labs with little access to Central research money. The report proposes a Unified Project Management System (UPMS) to track all Central R&D grants in real time using researcher identifiers (ORCID), institutional identifiers (ROR — Research Organisation Registry), and a Grant DOI (digital object identifier) system linked through Crossref's Grant Linking infrastructure. The goal is to create a transparent, internationally comparable research funding map that can reduce duplication, identify funding gaps, and attract international collaborations.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF):</strong> Established under the Anusandhan National Research Foundation Act 2023, ANRF is India's apex body for seeding, growing, and promoting R&D across universities, colleges, research institutions, and industry. Modelled loosely on the U.S. National Science Foundation, it has a target outlay of ₹50,000 crore over 5 years (2023–28), with the private sector expected to contribute the majority of funds.</li>
<li><strong>India's Gross Expenditure on R&D (GERD):</strong> India's GERD as a share of GDP has stagnated at approximately 0.64–0.7% for over a decade, well below the global average of ~1.8% and the target of 2% set in the Science, Technology and Innovation Policy (STIP) 2013 (draft 2020). China spends ~2.4% and the USA ~3.4% of GDP on R&D. ANRF was created specifically to address this chronic underfunding.</li>
<li><strong>ORCID (Open Researcher and Contributor ID):</strong> A globally used unique identifier for researchers, enabling attribution of research outputs, grants, and affiliations across institutions and career changes. ORCID integration is mandatory in research systems of the UK, EU, and US; NITI Aayog's recommendation to mandate ORCID in Indian grant systems would bring India in line with global best practice for research tracking.</li>
<li><strong>Research Organisation Registry (ROR) and Grant DOIs:</strong> ROR is an open registry of research organisations' persistent identifiers, enabling machine-readable institutional attribution in databases. Crossref's Grant Linking System assigns DOIs to research grants, making them citable, trackable, and linkable to publications and researchers. Together with ORCID, these form the backbone of open science infrastructure.</li>
<li><strong>IIT concentration in Central R&D funding:</strong> India has 23 IITs that collectively receive a disproportionate share of SERB, DST, DBT, and now ANRF funding, partly because their research capacity and grant-processing infrastructure is superior. The NITI Aayog report recommends explicit capacity-building grants for state universities and newer institutions to broaden the research base — mirroring the U.S. NSF's EPSCoR (Established Program to Stimulate Competitive Research) programme.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Health's 176th report (released August 7) documents the stark disparity in healthcare costs: average hospitalisation expenses in private hospitals stand at ₹50,508 compared to ₹6,631 in government facilities — a gap of nearly 8 times. The report identifies information asymmetry between hospitals and patients as the root cause of unchecked private-sector price escalation, and recommends a Diagnosis Related Group (DRG) system to standardise procedure costs nationally. The editorial argues that India's policy of incentivising private investment in healthcare through FDI liberalisation and tax concessions, without commensurate regulation, has created a dual system that is financially catastrophic for uninsured middle-class households. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Ayushman Bharat – PM Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY):</strong> Launched in 2018, AB-PMJAY provides health coverage of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary hospitalisation to approximately 50 crore beneficiaries (bottom 40% of India's population by SECC data). It is the world's largest government-funded health insurance scheme. Empanelled private hospitals must charge scheme rates for covered procedures, but implementation gaps — including upcoding, denial of admission, and extra charges — remain significant.</li>
<li><strong>Diagnosis Related Group (DRG) system:</strong> A payment system used in the U.S., EU, and Australia in which hospitals are paid a fixed amount per procedure/diagnosis regardless of actual costs incurred, incentivising cost efficiency. India's CGHS (Central Government Health Scheme) uses a partial DRG framework for reimbursements. The Standing Committee recommended a national DRG that would set price ceilings for all privately billed procedures, addressing information asymmetry.</li>
<li><strong>Private equity and healthcare consolidation:</strong> Global private equity firms (KKR, Blackstone, Temasek, IFC) have invested heavily in Indian hospital chains (Manipal, Fortis, Medanta, HCG). PE-backed consolidation typically prioritises investor returns (IRR of 20-30%), leading to revenue-maximising practices — longer stays, unnecessary tests, premium room allocation — that inflate bills. The Standing Committee recommended reviewing FDI norms in healthcare to add investor-return regulations.</li>
<li><strong>Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act 2010:</strong> Mandates registration and standardised minimum care norms for clinical establishments. However, fewer than 10 states have adopted the Act; most rely on state-specific regulations that are fragmented and weakly enforced. The Act empowers Central/State Councils to prescribe standards but lacks teeth for price regulation, a gap the DRG proposal would address.</li>
<li><strong>Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and India's commitments:</strong> SDG 3.8 targets UHC, including financial risk protection, for all. India's National Health Policy 2017 commits to progressive realisation of UHC. Out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure as a share of total health expenditure was 47% in India (2022), one of the highest globally — the high private cost documented by the committee directly contradicts India's UHC commitments.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Ramsar Convention on Wetlands (1971):</strong> An international treaty for wetland conservation, signed in Ramsar, Iran. India has 85 Ramsar sites (as of 2024), the highest count in Asia, covering 13.26 lakh hectares. Ramsar listing requires the government to maintain the ecological character of the site and prepare a management plan. However, listing does not automatically prevent urban encroachment without supporting domestic legislation and enforcement.</li>
<li><strong>Mission Amrit Sarovar:</strong> Launched in April 2022, this Central mission targets the restoration and rejuvenation of 75,000 water bodies (amrit sarova — 'nectar ponds') across India, one in every village, to improve water storage capacity, groundwater recharge, and rural livelihoods. Progress has been uneven; states with strong gram panchayat systems have done better.</li>
<li><strong>HYDRAA — Hyderabad Disaster Response and Assets Protection Agency:</strong> A Telangana state body established to protect government assets and restore encroached public lands including lakes, parks, and nalas in the Hyderabad metropolitan region. Its lake-restoration drive involves surveying revenue and survey records to identify original lake boundaries, demolishing illegal structures within the Full Tank Level (FTL), and restoring the bund.</li>
<li><strong>Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules 2017:</strong> Under the Environment Protection Act 1986, these rules prohibit drainage, dumping of solid waste, and construction within wetlands. A National Wetlands Authority is mandated to be set up; state governments are required to maintain an inventory of wetlands and notify buffer zones. Enforcement has been weak in most states.</li>
<li><strong>Urban heat islands and lake loss:</strong> Loss of urban water bodies directly reduces evapotranspiration cooling, intensifying urban heat islands. Studies on Bengaluru show that the city has lost over 79% of its lake area since 1973. Restoration of even a fraction of these water bodies can reduce ambient temperatures by 2–4°C in surrounding neighbourhoods — a significant co-benefit alongside biodiversity and flood management goals.</li>
</ul>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 19(1)(b) — Right to assemble:</strong> Guarantees the right of all citizens to assemble peaceably and without arms. This right is subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(3) in the interests of sovereignty, public order, or morality. The Supreme Court in Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar (2014) cautioned against arbitrary arrests in cases where the offence is less serious.</li>
<li><strong>Designated protest zones and constitutional validity:</strong> Several cities — Bengaluru (Freedom Park), Delhi (Jantar Mantar, Ramlila Maidan), Mumbai (Azad Maidan) — maintain designated protest zones. The Supreme Court in Re: Destruction of Public and Private Properties (2009) endorsed designated zones as a reasonable regulation. However, scholars argue that confining all protests to a single zone regardless of context, proximity to the object of protest, or crowd size is disproportionate.</li>

BNSS retains and in some cases expands provisions enabling preventive detention, police powers to disperse assemblies, and conditions on bail — provisions that critics argue could be misused to curb legitimate protests.</li>
<li><strong>Karnataka Regulation of Government Premises Bill 2026:</strong> A proposed state law to regulate the use of government and public property for assemblies and events. If enacted, it would give the executive broad discretion to grant or deny permission for protests, raising Article 19 concerns. Similar laws have been challenged in courts in other states on grounds of being overbroad and vague.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The opinion piece analyses discrepancies in the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls in Telangana and Karnataka, where approximately one-fifth of registered electors were deleted in both states. In Telangana, 3.38 crore electors were on rolls before SIR; in Karnataka, 5.54 crore. Post-SIR, around 1/5 of names were deleted in both. The author points out that the Election Commission's manual distinguishes between 'Shifted' voters (who have moved to another constituency but remain voters) and 'Absent' voters (who cannot be traced). However, field-level enumerators applied these categories arbitrarily, classifying shifted voters as absent and deleting them entirely, rather than transferring their registrations. This raises serious concerns about disenfranchisement of mobile populations — particularly migrant workers, students, and those in temporary accommodation — without due process.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of Electoral Rolls:</strong> An Election Commission of India (ECI) exercise to update voter lists by physically verifying existing registrations and adding new eligible voters. Unlike Summary Revisions (which allow deletions only on written application), SIRs involve door-to-door verification by Booth Level Officers (BLOs). If a voter is not found at the address, the BLO classifies them as 'shifted' or 'absent' and marks for deletion subject to due process.</li>
<li><strong>Representation of the People Act 1950 — voter registration:</strong> Under Section 16, a person may be removed from electoral rolls for being dead, having shifted out of the constituency, or being disqualified. Section 22 requires that before deletion, a notice must be issued and the voter given an opportunity to object. The opinion piece argues this due-process requirement was systematically bypassed during SIR in Telangana and Karnataka.</li>
<li><strong>Electoral Photo Identity Card (EPIC) and Aadhaar linkage:</strong> The government's electoral-Aadhaar linkage programme (Section 23 of RP Act, inserted 2022) was intended to de-duplicate rolls by identifying voters registered at multiple locations. Critics argued it could instead facilitate mass deletions of genuine voters — a concern the SIR controversy seems to validate.</li>
<li><strong>Migrant workers and voter disenfranchisement:</strong> India has an estimated 45-50 crore internal migrants, many of whom retain voter registrations at their home constituency while working elsewhere. They face a structural disenfranchisement — unable to vote in person at home while away and unable to easily transfer registration to their work location. The SIR problem compounds this: absent migrants get classified as 'absent' and deleted.</li>
<li><strong>Election Commission's accountability mechanisms:</strong> The ECI is a constitutional body under Article 324, empowered to superintend, direct, and control the preparation of electoral rolls. It functions independently of the executive but is not subject to legislative oversight in the same way as other institutions. Grievances about electoral roll errors must be filed with District Electoral Officers or challenged through election petitions — creating a practical accountability gap for systematic errors.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF) and Article 280:</strong> SDRF is constituted under Section 48 of the Disaster Management Act 2005. <li><strong>State Disaster Mitigation Fund (SDMF):</strong> Introduced by the 15th Finance Commission (FC-XV) for the first time, SDMF is separate from SDRF and is specifically for mitigation, preparedness, and capacity-building activities before a disaster occurs. FC-XVI has also been asked to recommend on climate finance and green transition — a first for a Finance Commission.</li>
<li><strong>India's heat risk geography:</strong> The India Meteorological Department (IMD) defines a heatwave as temperatures exceeding 40°C (plains) or 30°C (hills) and 4.5°C above normal. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) has mapped 57% of India's 766 districts as high/very-high heat risk. Rajasthan, Vidarbha, Telangana, and Odisha are perennial hotspots. The 2023 and 2024 heatwave seasons saw excess mortality estimated at 40,000–60,000 by independent researchers.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C):</strong> Established by MHA in 2018, I4C is the nodal agency for coordinating the fight against cybercrime in India. <li><strong>Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDPA):</strong> India's data protection law focuses on regulating how organisations collect, store, and use personal data. Both regions have high unemployment, poor education metrics, and weak policing — conditions that enabled the growth of fraud as a livelihood.</li>
<li><strong>Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 and cybercrime:</strong> BNS 2023 (replacing IPC) added Section 318 (cheating) and retained provisions for criminal conspiracy, fraud, and extortion. The Information Technology (Amendment) Bill 2023 is pending; a comprehensive cyber law rationalisation has been recommended by multiple committees but not yet enacted.</li>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Six months into the West Asia war that has effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, India is managing an acute energy crisis through a patchwork of substitutions. Hormuz — which before the war handled 15 million barrels per day (bpd) of oil — now sees only 0.25 million bpd transiting. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Strait of Hormuz — global strategic significance:</strong> A 33 km-wide chokepoint between Iran and Oman, through which approximately 20% of global oil supply and 30% of global LNG trade passes daily (pre-war: ~15–17 mn bpd). Iran has periodically threatened to close the strait as a geopolitical lever; its actual closure during the 2026 war represents an unprecedented disruption.</li>
<li><strong>India's Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR):</strong> Managed by the Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Limited (ISPRL) under MoPNG, India has three underground cavern facilities: Visakhapatnam (1.33 mn tonnes), Mangaluru (1.5 mn tonnes), and Padur (2.5 mn tonnes), totalling 5.33 mn tonnes — approximately 9 days of consumption. <p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Economy → Financial market regulation; SEBI; market infrastructure; cybersecurity
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → RegTech; SupTech; quantum-safe systems; IT governance

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
SEBI has introduced the IT Resilience Index (ITRI) to measure the technological robustness of Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs) — stock exchanges, clearing corporations, and depositories. The ITRI evaluates MIIs on nine parameters, with availability and security each weighted at 20%. An Early Warning System (EWS) flags declining resilience before systemic failures occur. The article benchmarks ITRI against comparable global regimes: MAS Singapore's Technology Risk Management Guidelines, UK FCA's operational resilience rules, the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), and ASIC Australia's CPG 234 framework. India's ITRI appears broadly aligned with global standards, with the addition of quantum-safe system readiness as a forward-looking parameter — distinguishing it from older regimes designed before the quantum computing threat was operationally relevant.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs):</strong> Under the SEBI Act 1992 and Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act 1956, MIIs include recognised stock exchanges (BSE, NSE), clearing corporations (NSCCL, ICCL), and depositories (NSDL, CDSL). They form the operational backbone of India's securities markets. A failure in any MII can disrupt trading, settlement, and custody for millions of market participants simultaneously.</li>
<li><strong>SEBI's cyber-resilience framework:</strong> SEBI issued a Circular on Cyber Security and Cyber Resilience for MIIs (2015, revised 2023) mandating incident response plans, vulnerability assessment, penetration testing, and business continuity plans. ITRI operationalises these requirements into a quantifiable index that SEBI can use for supervisory comparison and corrective action.</li>
<li><strong>Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), EU 2022:</strong> DORA applies to financial entities in the EU (banks, insurers, investment firms, exchanges) and their critical ICT third-party providers. It mandates ICT risk management frameworks, incident reporting, digital operational resilience testing, and oversight of third-party providers. India's ITRI draws inspiration from DORA but focuses specifically on market infrastructure rather than the entire financial sector.</li>
<li><strong>Quantum-safe cryptography:</strong> Classical encryption (RSA, ECC) is vulnerable to quantum computers running Shor's algorithm, which can factorise large primes — the basis of current public-key encryption — in polynomial time. The U.S. NIST finalised post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024. SEBI's inclusion of quantum-safe system readiness in ITRI places it ahead of most market regulators globally in proactive quantum risk management.</li>
<li><strong>RegTech and SupTech:</strong> RegTech (regulatory technology) refers to technology used by regulated entities to meet compliance requirements efficiently; SupTech (supervisory technology) refers to technology used by regulators for data collection, analysis, and supervision. ITRI is a SupTech tool — enabling SEBI to continuously monitor MII technology health without relying solely on self-reported disclosures, reducing regulatory information gaps.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**

Sophie Lainault, an Air France flight attendant, won a landmark French court ruling recognising her breast cancer as an occupational disease caused by decades of night-shift work. The ruling, the first of its kind in France and one of the first globally, establishes a legal precedent linking circadian rhythm disruption from chronic night shifts to hormone-sensitive cancers. Scientific evidence — including the WHO's IARC (International Agency for Research on Cancer) classification of night-shift work as a Group 2A probable carcinogen (2019) — supports the biological mechanism: suppression of melatonin by artificial light at night promotes oestrogen-driven cell proliferation. For India, where millions of women work in aviation, nursing, IT/BPO night shifts, and textile production, the ruling has significant implications for occupational health policy, labour law reform, and the Employees' State Insurance (ESI) framework for occupational diseases.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>IARC Classification of night-shift work (2019):</strong> The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) — a WHO body — classified night-shift work as a Group 2A "probable human carcinogen," citing mechanistic evidence (circadian disruption, melatonin suppression, immune dysregulation) and epidemiological associations with breast, prostate, and colorectal cancers. Group 2A means the evidence in humans is limited but the mechanism is well-established in animal studies.</li>
<li><strong>Employees' State Insurance (ESI) Act 1948 and occupational diseases:</strong> India's ESI scheme covers occupational diseases listed in Schedule III. Currently, Schedule III includes conditions like coal workers' pneumoconiosis, asbestosis, and silicosis — industrial diseases from mining and manufacturing. Cancer caused by night-shift work is not yet listed; the French ruling may provide impetus for India to expand Schedule III to include circadian-disruption-related diseases.</li>
<li><strong>Labour laws for women in night shifts — India:</strong> The Factories Act 1948 historically prohibited employment of women in factories between 7 PM and 6 AM. Several states have amended this prohibition to allow women in night shifts in IT, BPO, export-oriented units, and special economic zones — subject to employer-provided safety and transport facilities. The amendment enables economic participation but simultaneously exposes women to night-shift health risks now confirmed by European jurisprudence.</li>
<li><strong>Circadian rhythm and health — biological basis:</strong> The human circadian clock, regulated by the hypothalamic suprachiasmatic nucleus, synchronises to the 24-hour light-dark cycle. Disruption through artificial light at night suppresses pineal melatonin secretion, which has anti-tumour, anti-oxidant, and immune-modulating functions. Chronic disruption is associated with increased risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hormone-sensitive cancers — a growing area of clinical evidence.</li>
<li><strong>Occupational cancer recognition — global trends:</strong> Several European countries (UK, Denmark, Finland) have recognised breast cancer among flight crews and nurses as occupational disease, triggering compensation. Denmark became the first country (2008) to classify breast cancer in night-shift workers as an occupational disease eligible for workers' compensation. The French ruling is the first court judgment (as opposed to administrative policy) to make this recognition — a harder, longer-lasting legal standard.</li>
</ul>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → US-Canada trade relations; protectionism; USMCA; economic bilateralism
- GS Paper III → Economy → Global trade; tariff wars; WTO norms; supply chain disruption

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The U.S. imposed 50% tariffs on Canadian goods, invoking Depression-era trade law, with Canada retaliating with $20 billion in counter-tariffs effective September 8. USMCA (US-Mexico-Canada Agreement, successor to NAFTA) is up for review in 2026 — providing a natural deadline and negotiating window. Analysts Wendy Cutler and Christopher Sands (Johns Hopkins) argue that behind the escalatory rhetoric, both sides retain economic incentives for resolution: U.S. auto industry supply chains are deeply integrated with Canada, and January 1 auto tariffs would devastate North American auto manufacturing. Jared Kushner, who negotiated USMCA during Trump's first term, is reportedly involved in back-channel talks. For India, the US-Canada trade war has mixed implications: on the one hand it signals that the U.S. is willing to impose tariffs even on close allies, reinforcing concerns about U.S. trade policy unpredictability; on the other, disruption in North American supply chains may create market opportunities for Indian goods in the U.S.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>USMCA (United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, 2020):</strong> The successor to NAFTA (1994), USMCA modernised trade rules for digital trade, intellectual property, labour standards, and automotive rules of origin. It has a built-in review clause: the agreement is subject to a joint review in 2026, and either party can withdraw after giving 6 months' notice from 2026. This review window creates both risk and opportunity for renegotiation.</li>
<li><strong>Depression-era trade law — Section 232 and Smoot-Hawley parallels:</strong> The U.S. has invoked Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act 1962 (national security justification) and Section 301 of the Trade Act 1974 (unfair trade practices) for tariffs. "Depression-era trade law" likely refers to similar sweeping tariff authorities used in the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act — the protectionist law widely blamed for deepening the Great Depression by triggering a global tariff war. The comparison signals the stakes of the current US-Canada dispute.</li>
<li><strong>WTO dispute settlement and tariff responses:</strong> Canada and the EU have filed WTO Dispute Settlement Body (DSB) complaints against U.S. Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminium, with WTO panels ruling against the U.S. However, the U.S. has blocked the WTO Appellate Body (by refusing to confirm new members), making WTO rulings practically unenforceable. This institutional paralysis has made bilateral and plurilateral agreements (like USMCA) the primary arena for trade dispute resolution.</li>
<li><strong>North American auto supply chain integration:</strong> Auto manufacturing under NAFTA/USMCA is deeply integrated: a vehicle crosses the US-Canada or US-Mexico border multiple times during production. Ford, GM, and Chrysler have major Canadian assembly plants. A 50% tariff on Canadian automotive goods would add thousands of dollars to vehicle costs, making North American cars uncompetitive globally and domestically — a strong lobby against sustained tariff escalation.</li>
<li><strong>India-US trade and parallel concerns:</strong> India faces reciprocal tariff threats from the U.S. under the same protectionist framework applied to Canada. The India-U.S. trade deficit ($45 billion in 2023-24, favourable to India) and sectors like pharmaceuticals, IT services, and steel have been flagged by U.S. trade authorities. The US-Canada dispute's resolution or escalation will shape the broader environment for India's ongoing bilateral trade negotiations with the U.S.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Ukraine awards Musk top honour as it seeks to use Starlink more
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Technology</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Security</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 14 · The World</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Satellite communications; Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellations; dual-use space technology
- GS Paper II → International Relations → Technology in conflict; private sector role in national security

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy awarded Elon Musk the Order of Freedom — Ukraine's highest civilian honour — as Ukraine sought to deepen operational integration of SpaceX's Starlink satellite internet service into its military command and logistics networks. Starlink, a Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite constellation with over 6,000 satellites, has provided Ukraine with resilient battlefield communications in areas where terrestrial infrastructure was destroyed by Russian strikes. The award reflects how private technology companies — particularly those operating space infrastructure — have become strategic actors in modern conflicts, outside traditional arms-supply frameworks. For India, which is developing its own LEO constellation and has begun licensing OneWeb (in which Eutelsat and Bharti Enterprises hold stakes), the Ukraine experience offers lessons on satellite resilience, network architecture under adversarial conditions, and the governance questions of privately operated communications during conflict.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Starlink and LEO satellite constellations:</strong> SpaceX's Starlink is the world's largest LEO satellite constellation (~6,000+ active satellites as of 2026), providing broadband internet with latency of 20–40 ms — comparable to terrestrial networks. Unlike geostationary satellites (35,786 km altitude, 600 ms latency), LEO constellations at 550 km enable near-real-time communication, critical for military applications.</li>
<li><strong>Dual-use space technology — governance gap:</strong> Starlink terminals are commercially available civilian technology that Ukraine has repurposed for military use. This creates a governance grey zone: Musk has on at least one occasion (Crimea, 2023) restricted Starlink coverage in an active conflict zone, demonstrating that a private company can unilaterally influence the battlefield — without treaty obligations, parliamentary oversight, or democratic accountability.</li>
<li><strong>India's LEO satellite policy:</strong> India amended its space policy in 2022 to permit private entities to operate in the space sector. ISRO's IN-SPACe (Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre) licenses private companies. OneWeb (licensed in India through Bharti Airtel subsidiary), Amazon's Kuiper, and Elon Musk's Starlink (seeking DoT approval) are competing for India's satellite broadband market. Military and strategic applications of these systems are governed under the DoT/DoS framework.</li>
<li><strong>Satellite communications in modern warfare:</strong> The Russia-Ukraine war is the first major interstate conflict in which commercial LEO satellite communications played a decisive operational role. Russia attempted to jam and hack Starlink terminals (and briefly succeeded in disrupting ground stations) but SpaceX rolled out software-based frequency-hopping countermeasures rapidly — demonstrating the agility of commercial tech companies relative to traditional defence procurement cycles.</li>
<li><strong>India's military satellite communications:</strong> India's GSAT-7 (Navy) and GSAT-7A (Air Force) are dedicated military communication satellites in geostationary orbit. The Army's GSAT-7C was launched in 2023. DRDO and ISRO are developing indigenous software-defined radios and satellite communication terminals. The experience of Starlink in Ukraine has reinforced calls within the Indian defence establishment for LEO-based resilient communications to supplement geostationary assets.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Syndrela Das and Payas Jain inducted into TOPS Development Scheme
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 17 · Sport</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Sports policy; TOPS scheme; Khelo India; athlete welfare

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Table tennis prodigy Syndrela Das (17 years old), who won silver and bronze medals at the Asian Youth Championships, and Payas Jain were inducted into the Target Olympic Podium Scheme (TOPS) Development Group. The Mission Olympic Cell also approved international-event funding for star table tennis player Manika Batra. Both Syndrela and Payas are being groomed for the Asian Games squad and are expected to benefit from TOPS funding for coaching, equipment, foreign training camps, and tournament fees. TOPS is administered by the Sports Authority of India (SAI) under the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports and identifies athletes with potential for Olympic and Asian Games medals, providing them personalised support packages. The Development Group within TOPS covers emerging athletes while the Core Group covers those with a realistic shot at a medal at the upcoming Olympic cycle.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Target Olympic Podium Scheme (TOPS):</strong> Launched in 2014 by the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports (MYAS), TOPS provides elite athletes with direct financial support, access to world-class coaching, training equipment, and international competition funding. It has two tiers: Core Group (imminent medal prospects) and Development Group (promising athletes 6–8 years from peak). TOPS is managed by the Mission Olympic Cell (MOC), chaired by the SAI DG.</li>
<li><strong>Khelo India Programme:</strong> A broader ecosystem-building scheme covering identification and nurturing of talent from school level (Khelo India School Games, Khelo India University Games), infrastructure creation (Khelo India Centres), and support for athletes under 18 via the Khelo India Scholarship (₹5 lakh/year). TOPS targets the apex of this pyramid — Olympic-level athletes who have already been identified through Khelo India and state systems.</li>
<li><strong>Sports Authority of India (SAI):</strong> An autonomous body under MYAS established in 1984. SAI manages national training centres (NTC), National Centres of Excellence (NCOE), and sports hostels; implements Khelo India and TOPS; and provides coaching, scientific support, and nutrition assistance to elite athletes. It operates under the National Sports Development Code 2011.</li>
<li><strong>India's table tennis at the Asian level:</strong> India's table tennis programme has gained global visibility through Manika Batra (first Indian woman to reach the top 25 in the world; 2018 Commonwealth Games champion), Achanta Sharath Kamal (multiple CWG gold medallist, Paris 2024 Olympian), and G Sathiyan. The emergence of a 17-year-old at the Asian Youth Championship level signals depth in the talent pipeline.</li>
<li><strong>Athlete welfare and financial support — policy context:</strong> India's athletes have historically suffered from poor institutional support compared to China, Japan, and South Korea. The National Sports Policy 2001 (under revision to NSP 2021 draft) and the Sports Code 2011 established norms but implementation was weak. TOPS represents a paradigm shift — targeted, individualised funding based on performance potential rather than seniority or sport federation lobbying, modelled on Australia's AIS and UK's UK Sport podium programmes.</li>
</ul>
</div>
