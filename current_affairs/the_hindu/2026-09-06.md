**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-China bilateral; trans-boundary rivers; water disputes
- GS Paper III → Infrastructure → Hydropower; energy security; dam construction
- GS Paper I → Geography → Glacial dynamics; Brahmaputra river system; Himalayan ecology

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
China's plan to build the world's largest hydroelectric dam on the Yarlung Zangbo river — known as the Brahmaputra after it enters India — remains firmly on track despite a glacial collapse upstream in late August 2026. The project, valued at 1.2 trillion yuan, will have an installed capacity of 60 GW across five power stations and involves a 40-km tunnel through the Great Bend in Medog county, Tibet. India has repeatedly expressed concern about the dam's downstream impact on water flows, flood risks, and ecological disruption to the Northeast. China's Expert-Level Mechanism on Trans-border Rivers is scheduled to meet India later in September, offering a diplomatic channel but no binding framework to address Indian concerns. The glacial collapse, while dramatic, was characterized by Chinese officials as insufficient reason to halt construction. The dam's scale — nearly triple the Three Gorges Dam's output — gives it strategic significance beyond electricity generation, including potential for flow manipulation.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Yarlung Zangbo / Brahmaputra River:</strong> The river originates in Tibet as the Yarlung Zangbo, enters India through Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang, and becomes the Brahmaputra in Assam before flowing into Bangladesh. It is one of the few rivers that pre-dates the Himalayas (antecedent drainage) and carries enormous sediment loads, making it vital to the ecology and agriculture of Northeast India.</li>
<li><strong>Expert-Level Mechanism on Trans-border Rivers (India-China):</strong> A bilateral technical forum established to share hydrological data (flood season water levels and rainfall) on the Brahmaputra and Sutlej rivers. China suspended data sharing after the Doklam standoff in 2017 and resumed it in 2021. The mechanism has no binding treaty-level water-sharing arrangement, unlike the Indus Waters Treaty India has with Pakistan.</li>
<li><strong>Great Bend Hydropower Project:</strong> Located at the Great Bend (Metog/Medog) where the Yarlung Zangbo drops ~2,000 metres in a short stretch, creating massive energy potential. The project's 40 km tunnel would divert water to generate power; the 60 GW capacity would dwarf China's Three Gorges Dam (22.5 GW) and could give China operational control over downstream water flows into Assam.</li>
<li><strong>India's concerns — No Treaty Framework:</strong> Unlike the Mekong Commission or the Indus Waters Treaty, no legally binding trans-boundary water agreement exists between India and China for the Brahmaputra. India has consistently demanded such a framework. Downstream risks include altered sediment flows, artificial flooding if dam gates are opened, and seasonal water scarcity affecting agriculture in Assam and Bangladesh.</li>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs):</strong> The Himalayan region is acutely vulnerable to GLOFs, where glacial collapse causes sudden release of meltwater stored in glacial lakes, leading to catastrophic downstream flooding. The August 2026 collapse near the dam site highlights that mega-infrastructure in seismically and glaciologically active Himalayan terrain faces compounded risk, a fact that NDMA and ISRO have flagged in multiple reports for India's northeastern states.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Eight engineering students from PSNA College of Engineering and Technology, Dindigul, Tamil Nadu, were killed and several injured when their bus collided with an illegally parked lorry on National Highway 66 near Thrissur in Kerala. The accident highlights persistent failures in enforcing road safety norms on national highways: parking of heavy vehicles in non-designated areas at night remains a chronic hazard despite Motor Vehicles Amendment Act 2019 provisions. Road accidents are among India's leading causes of preventable death, with the government's National Road Safety Policy and Vision Zero framework setting targets that remain far from achieved. The tragedy underlines the need for better roadside infrastructure (designated truck lay-bys), stricter enforcement, and coordination between state traffic police and NHAI on National Highway segments.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019:</strong> Significantly increased penalties for traffic violations including drunk driving, over-speeding, and overloading. It also introduced provisions for road safety boards, good Samaritan protection, and electronic enforcement. Critics note enforcement remains patchy across states due to lack of capacity and political will.</li>
<li><strong>National Road Safety Policy (2010):</strong> India's overarching road safety framework, which set targets for reducing accident fatalities. India reports over 1.5 lakh road deaths annually — one of the highest globally — making road safety a key governance and public health concern for UPSC.</li>
<li><strong>National Highway 66 (formerly NH 17):</strong> A major National Highway running along India's western coast from Mumbai to Kanyakumari, passing through Kerala. Heavy commercial vehicle traffic on this highway is intense, and night-time illegal parking by lorry drivers at non-designated spots is a documented problem.</li>
<li><strong>NHAI and State Police Jurisdiction:</strong> The National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) manages highway infrastructure, while traffic enforcement on NHs is the responsibility of state police. This split jurisdiction often leads to accountability gaps in road safety enforcement.</li>
<li><strong>Vision Zero and Decade of Action:</strong> India committed to the UN Decade of Action for Road Safety (2021–2030) with the goal of halving road traffic deaths. The Supreme Court's Committee on Road Safety, formed in 2014, has repeatedly flagged systemic enforcement failures and slow implementation of road engineering improvements.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**UPSC Mains Classification**

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Delhi Police arrested Swatantra Bhardwaj, a self-styled social media influencer associated with the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP), in connection with the assault on Nishu Aazad's father at Jantar Mantar. The case illustrates the growing challenge posed by fringe political actors who use social media platforms to organise demonstrations and, in some cases, incite or carry out violence. India currently lacks a comprehensive digital intermediary accountability law specifically targeting influencers, though the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules 2021 create some obligations for platforms. The incident also raises questions about law enforcement's capacity to monitor and preempt violence linked to online mobilisation, and the blurring of the line between protected political speech and incitement.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021:</strong> Issued under the Information Technology Act 2000, these rules impose due diligence obligations on social media intermediaries (takedown timelines, grievance officers, traceability requirements for significant social media intermediaries with over 50 lakh users). They do not directly regulate individual influencers but place obligations on the platforms that host them.</li>
<li><strong>Jantar Mantar as a Protest Site:</strong> Jantar Mantar in New Delhi has historically been a legally designated protest venue. The Supreme Court curtailed its use in 2018, directing that protests there must not disrupt public order. Subsequent protests have required police permission, creating a regulatory and rights tension around peaceful assembly under Article 19(1)(b).</li>
<li><strong>Fringe Political Organisations:</strong> India has seen a proliferation of small, social-media-driven political entities with unclear ideological moorings. These groups often operate outside established party structures, raising accountability gaps — they may not be regulated under the Representation of the People Act 1951 as recognised political parties.</li>
<li><strong>Section 151 CrPC / BNSS Preventive Arrest:</strong> Police use preventive detention provisions to arrest persons likely to commit cognisable offences. Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023 (which replaced CrPC), similar provisions continue, allowing short-term detention to prevent public order breaches.</li>
<li><strong>Accountability of Social Media Influencers:</strong> ASCI (Advertising Standards Council of India) guidelines require influencers to disclose paid partnerships, but there is no statutory framework governing their political activities or incitement. The Press Council of India's mandate does not extend to digital influencers, creating a regulatory gap flagged by parliamentary committees.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Voter lists shrink by 15.7% in 17 States and U.T.s after SIR</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 1 · Front Page</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Electoral rolls; Election Commission of India; Representation of People Act; democratic rights
- GS Paper II → Governance → Transparency and accountability; e-governance in electoral administration

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Election Commission of India's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls has resulted in a 15.7% contraction in voter lists across 17 states and Union Territories, with the total electorate falling from 35.5 crore to 29.93 crore in the affected states. The deletions — 6.15 crore entries — comprise 4.52 crore voters marked "absent" and 1.11 crore recorded as deceased, with the remainder deleted on other grounds. The scale of deletion is stark in Delhi (−35.89%) and Maharashtra (−17.08%), raising opposition concerns about disenfranchisement, particularly of migrant workers and the urban poor who may not be present during SIR field visits. The INDIA bloc, led by CPI(ML) Liberation's Dipankar Bhattacharya, has demanded a unified opposition response and a special meeting to challenge the SIR process. The controversy touches on fundamental questions of universal adult franchise, the constitutional mandate of the ECI, and the adequacy of procedural safeguards during roll revision.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of Electoral Rolls:</strong> Under Section 21 of the Representation of the People Act 1950, the Election Commission of India is empowered to revise electoral rolls. SIR involves door-to-door verification by Booth Level Officers (BLOs) to update, add, or delete entries. It is distinct from routine Summary Revision and is triggered when the ECI determines that the rolls require a thorough clean-up.</li>
<li><strong>Representation of the People Act, 1950 and 1951:</strong> The RPA 1950 governs preparation and publication of electoral rolls, while RPA 1951 governs the conduct of elections. Section 16 of the 1950 Act specifies disqualifications for registration (non-citizen, unsound mind, corrupt practice). Deletion of a voter requires an opportunity to be heard under Rule 20 of the Registration of Electors Rules 1960.</li>
<li><strong>Universal Adult Franchise — Article 326:</strong> The Constitution guarantees the right to vote to every citizen above 18 years (lowered from 21 by the 61st Amendment, 1988) who is not disqualified. Mass deletions without adequate notice may be challenged as violating Article 326 and Articles 14 and 21 (equality and right to life with dignity).</li>
<li><strong>ECI's Constitutional Status:</strong> The Election Commission of India is a constitutional body under Article 324, vested with superintendence, direction, and control of elections. It operates independently of the executive, but its decisions on roll revision are subject to judicial review by the Supreme Court and High Courts.</li>
<li><strong>Migrant Workers and Electoral Exclusion:</strong> India's internal migration (estimated at 45–50 crore by Economic Survey 2017) creates structural vulnerability in electoral rolls: migrants registered at their home address may be marked "absent" during field verification in their native constituency while living elsewhere. The concept of "remote voting" for migrants was proposed by ECI but not yet implemented as of mid-2026, leaving this gap unaddressed.</li>
</ul>
</div>

- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Artificial intelligence; data centres
- GS Paper II → Governance → State industrial policy; public-private partnership

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) announced plans to establish a massive AI data centre in Hyderabad through its subsidiary HyperVault, with an investment of ₹70,000 crore and a capacity of 1 gigawatt — making it one of the largest data centre projects in Asia. Telangana Chief Minister A. Revanth Reddy confirmed the deal, expected to create 7,000 direct jobs, with a commissioning deadline of June 2, 2028 (Telangana Formation Day). The investment reflects the global surge in AI compute demand driven by large language models and generative AI requiring enormous energy and cooling infrastructure. India's data centre market has attracted significant FDI, with states competing on land, power, and connectivity advantages. The project also raises policy questions on energy consumption, water usage, and the need for a national Data Centre Policy framework.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Data Centre Policy and Infrastructure Status:</strong> India's Data Centre Policy 2020 draft sought to grant infrastructure status to data centres, enabling priority power allocation and cheaper financing. The 2022 Union Budget recognised data centres as infrastructure, facilitating easier land acquisition and long-term finance. A 1 GW facility would be among the world's largest hyperscale data centres.</li>
<li><strong>India AI Mission (2024):</strong> The Government of India launched the India AI Mission with a ₹10,300 crore outlay over five years, targeting AI compute infrastructure, public datasets, application development, and safety research. Private investment like TCS HyperVault complements government-funded GPU compute clusters being set up under this mission for public-good AI applications.</li>
<li><strong>Telangana Formation and Industrial Policy:</strong> Telangana was carved out of Andhra Pradesh on June 2, 2014 under the AP Reorganisation Act 2014. The state has aggressively courted IT and pharma investment, leveraging Hyderabad's established ecosystem. The June 2, 2028 deadline is politically symbolic as the state's 14th formation anniversary.</li>
<li><strong>Energy and Water Footprint of AI Data Centres:</strong> A 1 GW data centre consumes electricity comparable to a medium-sized city and requires vast water volumes for cooling. This raises sustainability questions in water-stressed Telangana and aligns with global concerns about AI's carbon footprint — relevant to GS3 environment themes and GS4 ethics of responsible technology deployment.</li>
<li><strong>Gig and Direct Employment in Tech Infrastructure:</strong> The 7,000 direct jobs figure represents high-skill employment in operations, maintenance, and management. However, data centres are capital-intensive rather than labour-intensive; critics note that ₹70,000 crore investment yielding only 7,000 direct jobs (₹10 crore per job) raises questions about employment intensity of AI infrastructure versus traditional manufacturing.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">T.N. govt. shortlists two sites for second airport for Chennai</h2>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Tamil Nadu government has shortlisted two sites for Chennai's proposed second international airport: Cheyyur in Chengalpattu district and Manellore near Gummidipoondi in Tiruvallur district. The earlier frontrunner at Parandur has been dropped following land acquisition concerns and agrarian opposition. The Airports Authority of India (AAI) is conducting a pre-feasibility study evaluating connectivity, land availability, environmental clearance feasibility, and proximity to the existing airport. A second Chennai airport has been under discussion for over a decade as the existing facility (capacity ~23 million passengers) faces growing congestion. The project connects to the National Civil Aviation Policy 2016 and the broader infrastructure expansion agenda, but land acquisition under the LARR Act 2013 and obtaining environmental clearances from MoEF&CC remain likely bottlenecks.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Airports Authority of India (AAI):</strong> Statutory body under the AAI Act 1994, under the Ministry of Civil Aviation. AAI manages 137 airports and conducts pre-feasibility and DPR studies for greenfield airport proposals before government approval. For greenfield airports involving private participation, the model concession agreement framework under the Greenfield Airports Policy applies.</li>
<li><strong>National Civil Aviation Policy 2016 (NCAP):</strong> Sets targets including 1 billion air trips per year by 2040, operationalising 100+ airports, and enhancing Tier-2/3 connectivity via Regional Connectivity Scheme (RCS-UDAN). Second airports in saturated metros are part of this vision to decongest existing infrastructure and support economic growth.</li>
<li><strong>Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR Act):</strong> Greenfield airport projects require large tracts of land. The LARR Act mandates Social Impact Assessment, consent of 70–80% of affected families (for PPP projects), and rehabilitation packages. Parandur's exclusion reflects the political and legal difficulty of acquiring fertile agricultural land under this framework.</li>
<li><strong>Parandur Controversy and Environmental Concerns:</strong> The Parandur site in Kancheepuram was opposed by farmers growing paddy and other crops on fertile land, and by environmentalists concerned about the proximity to the Chembarambakkam reservoir (a major drinking water source for Chennai). This illustrates the recurring infrastructure vs. agriculture/environment tension in India's development trajectory.</li>
<li><strong>Aviation Demand Projections:</strong> India is projected to become the world's third-largest civil aviation market by 2030. DGCA and ICAO data show India's domestic passenger traffic recovering strongly post-COVID. Chennai, as Tamil Nadu's capital and a major automotive, IT, and port hub, has significant aviation demand, making the second airport strategically important for the state's economic competitiveness.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">No closure yet in Gauri Lankesh, Kalburgi killings</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Governance → Rule of law; press freedom; criminal justice; judiciary
- GS Paper I → Society → Communalism; freedom of expression; rationalism

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Nine years after the targeted killing of journalist-activist Gauri Lankesh (September 5, 2017) and eleven years after scholar M.M. Kalburgi's assassination, justice remains elusive in both cases. All accused in the Gauri Lankesh case — investigated by a Special Investigation Team that identified links to right-wing extremist networks — remain on bail. Srikanth Pangarkar, an accused in the case, won a legislative assembly seat in Jalna (Maharashtra) in January 2026 elections while on bail. Kalburgi, a prominent Kannada scholar and former Hampi University Vice-Chancellor, was killed in August 2015; that case is effectively unsolved. The killings of Dabholkar (2013), Pansare (2015), Kalburgi (2015), and Lankesh (2017) are widely linked as part of a coordinated pattern targeting rationalists and dissenting voices, raising fundamental questions about press freedom, witness protection, and the pace of criminal justice in India.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Press Freedom in India — Constitutional and International Framework:</strong> Freedom of the press derives from Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech and expression); it is not a separate enumerated right. India ranked 159th in RSF World Press Freedom Index 2024. Targeted killings of journalists constitute extreme press freedom violations under the ICCPR (Article 19) and UNESCO's safety of journalists framework.</li>
<li><strong>SIT Investigation and the Sanatan Sanstha Connection:</strong> The Karnataka SIT probing the Gauri Lankesh killing identified accused persons with alleged links to the Sanatan Sanstha, a Hindu nationalist organisation. The SIT used digital forensics, call data records, and interstate witness testimony. MCOCA (Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act) was invoked in the interconnected Maharashtra cases involving Dabholkar and Pansare.</li>
<li><strong>Accused Persons Contesting Elections — Constitutional Issues:</strong> Section 8 of the RPA 1951 bars persons convicted of specified offences from contesting elections. Persons merely accused (under trial) are not disqualified until conviction. The Supreme Court in Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013) struck down the protection given to sitting legislators upon conviction, but pre-conviction disqualification remains legally complex.</li>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
This analysis by Varghese K. George examines the political controversy sparked by the Manusmriti vs. Quran debate — pitting Rahul Gandhi's critique of Manusmriti against Kanthapuram A.P. Aboobacker's defence of scriptural authority. The article identifies three broad approaches societies adopt toward ancient religious texts: outright rejection (rationalist position), selective reinterpretation to align with contemporary values (reformist position), and agnostic acceptance without attempting to reconcile contradictions (pragmatist position). **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Manusmriti and Its Contested Legacy:</strong> Manusmriti (Laws of Manu, c. 2nd century BCE–3rd century CE) is an ancient Dharmashastra text that codified social norms including caste hierarchy and gender roles. B.R. Ambedkar famously burned copies of Manusmriti on December 25, 1927 (Manusmriti Dahan Day) as an act of protest against caste discrimination. Modern debates on Manusmriti engage questions of constitutional morality vs. religious tradition.</li>
<li><strong>Indian Secularism — Sarva Dharma Sambhava:</strong> India's secularism (introduced into the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment 1976) is not strict separation of church and state (as in France) but "equal respect for all religions" (sarva dharma sambhava) or "equidistance from all religions." The state can intervene in religious practices that violate fundamental rights (Articles 17, 25(2)) — such as untouchability or practices "repugnant to public order, morality, or health."</li>
<li><strong>Personal Laws and Constitutional Morality:</strong> India has separate personal laws (Hindu Marriage Act, Muslim Personal Law) based on religious scriptures. The Supreme Court in Navtej Singh Johar (2018) and Joseph Shine (2018) invoked "constitutional morality" as superior to "popular morality" or scriptural morality. Shabnam Hashmi v. Union of India (2014) and Shah Bano (1985) are landmarks in the tension between religious personal law and constitutional rights.</li>
<li><strong>Religious Reform and Article 25:</strong> Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion. However, Article 25(2)(b) enables the state to make laws for "social welfare and reform" and to "throw open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus." This provision has been used to regulate temple entry, untouchability, and practices deemed oppressive.</li>
<li><strong>Kanthapuram Controversy — Gender and Religious Authority:</strong> Kanthapuram A.P. Aboobacker (Grand Mufti of India) has issued several circulars restricting women's participation in public religious events. His position illustrates how religious authority figures exercise normative control over community members in ways that may conflict with constitutional rights (Article 15 — non-discrimination on grounds of sex, Article 21 — right to dignity). The state's response to such intra-religious normative conflicts is a key secularism question.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">What is 'One Nation, One Time'?</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>

<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 8 · FAQ</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → NavIC; time synchronisation; satellite navigation; space technology
- GS Paper II → Governance → Legal Metrology; standardisation; regulatory reform

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Ministry of Consumer Affairs notified the Legal Metrology (Indian Standard Time) Rules 2026 on August 27, introducing "One Nation, One Time" — a mandate for all electronic devices, telecom networks, and digital services in India to synchronise to Indian Standard Time (IST, UTC+5:30) as disseminated by CSIR-NPL (National Physical Laboratory) via the NavIC satellite navigation system. A 180-day compliance window was given. The White Rabbit Technology IST Dissemination Network was commissioned in Bengaluru in July 2026 to provide nanosecond-level time accuracy across India. The policy eliminates India's dependence on GPS (American) for time synchronisation, strengthening digital sovereignty. Time standardisation is foundational for financial transactions, telecommunications, power grids, and critical infrastructure.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation):</strong> India's regional satellite navigation system, developed by ISRO. It consists of 7 satellites providing positioning accuracy of less than 20 metres over India and up to 1,500 km beyond its borders. NavIC provides two services: Standard Positioning Service (civil) and Restricted Service (encrypted, for military and government). It also provides time dissemination, making India independent of US GPS for timing. ISRO completed the NavIC constellation upgrade with NVS-01 satellite in 2023.</li>
<li><strong>CSIR-NPL (National Physical Laboratory):</strong> India's national metrology laboratory under CSIR, the custodian of Indian Standard Time and physical measurement standards. It maintains atomic clocks that define IST (UTC+5:30, no daylight saving). NPL's time signals are the legal reference for all Indian time synchronisation. The White Rabbit Technology network allows NPL to disseminate IST with nanosecond accuracy across India's critical infrastructure.</li>
<li><strong>Legal Metrology Act, 2009:</strong> Governs weights, measures, and measurement standards in India. The Act replaced the Standards of Weights and Measures Act 1976. Rules under the Act cover packaged commodities, weighing instruments, and now time measurement through the 2026 IST Rules. Violations of Legal Metrology rules attract fines and imprisonment under the Act.</li>
<li><strong>White Rabbit Technology:</strong> Developed at CERN (European Organization for Nuclear Research), White Rabbit is a sub-nanosecond synchronisation protocol built on standard Ethernet. It enables timing accuracy of <1 nanosecond and frequency accuracy of ~10 picoseconds over fibre networks. India's IST Dissemination Network uses this technology to distribute precise time from NPL to critical infrastructure nodes including banks, telecom providers, and power grids.</li>
<li><strong>Digital Sovereignty and GPS Dependence:</strong> GPS (Global Positioning System) is owned by the US Department of Defense and can be selectively denied or degraded in conflict situations. India's critical infrastructure (financial trading systems, telecom networks, power grids) previously relied heavily on GPS for time synchronisation — a strategic vulnerability. The One Nation, One Time initiative eliminates this dependence for domestic applications, aligning with the Atmanirbhar Bharat vision for technological self-reliance.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Why is BRICS exploring cross-border payments?</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">International Relations</span>
<span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Economy</span>
<span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">AI</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 8 · FAQ</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → BRICS; de-dollarisation; multilateral financial institutions; India as Chair 2026
- GS Paper III → Economy → International payments; CBDC; SWIFT; currency internationalisation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Ahead of the 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi (India holding the 2026 chair), the bloc is exploring alternatives to SWIFT for cross-border payments. SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication), a Belgium-based cooperative used by 11,000+ financial institutions in 200+ countries, is seen as a tool of Western financial coercion after Russia was excluded from it following the Ukraine invasion. Key alternatives under discussion include Project Nexus (BIS-led, linking central bank fast payment systems of six nations including India's RBI, going live 2027), mBridge (originally a BIS-China-Thailand-Hong Kong-UAE project using CBDCs; BIS left in October 2024; China's digital yuan dominates), and India's own proposal for CBDC linkage for trade and tourism payments. Trump's November 2024 threat of 100% tariffs on BRICS nations moving away from the dollar has added urgency but also caution to BRICS de-dollarisation efforts.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>SWIFT and Western Financial Sanctions:</strong> SWIFT is a messaging network that enables cross-border financial transactions between banks. Excluding a country from SWIFT — as done with Iran (2012, 2018) and Russia (2022) — effectively cuts it off from the global dollar-based financial system. This has incentivised BRICS nations to develop SWIFT-independent payment channels, driven particularly by Russia and China but also attracting India's interest in reducing dollar dependency.</li>
<li><strong>Project Nexus (BIS):</strong> The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) Innovation Hub's Project Nexus connects national fast payment systems (India's UPI, Singapore's PayNow, Malaysia's DuitNow, Philippines' InstaPay, Thailand's PromptPay) through a multilateral hub. The RBI is a participant. Nexus is designed for retail cross-border payments and is expected to go live in 2027. Unlike mBridge, it uses existing fiat currencies rather than CBDCs.</li>
<li><strong>mBridge — Wholesale CBDC Platform:</strong> A multi-CBDC platform originally co-developed by BIS, PBOC (China), HKMA, Bank of Thailand, and Central Bank of UAE for wholesale cross-border transactions. BIS withdrew from mBridge in October 2024, citing concerns about the platform's potential to facilitate sanctions evasion. China's e-CNY (digital yuan) dominates the platform. India has not joined mBridge, maintaining caution about China-dominated infrastructure.</li>
<li><strong>De-dollarisation and Its Limits:</strong> The US dollar accounts for ~58% of global foreign exchange reserves (IMF, 2024) and ~44% of SWIFT payment volumes. De-dollarisation is a structural long-term trend but is limited by network effects (contracts denominated in USD), deep US financial markets, and dollar-denominated global commodity pricing (oil, gold). Trump's tariff threat illustrates the political cost of challenging dollar dominance, making India's approach cautious — bilateral CBDC linkage rather than broad SWIFT replacement.</li>
<li><strong>India as BRICS Chair 2026:</strong> India assumed the BRICS chair in January 2026 and is hosting the 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi. As chair, India sets the agenda. India's priorities reportedly include practical payment connectivity (Project Nexus type) rather than confrontational de-dollarisation, reflecting its non-alignment tradition and its strong US economic relationship (India-US bilateral trade ~$190 billion annually). BRICS Clear — a proposed clearing system mentioned at the Kazan 2024 summit — was not featured in the Rio 2025 declaration, suggesting limited momentum.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Why is the Centre opposed to totalisers?</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
<span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 8 · FAQ</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Polity → Electronic Voting Machines; electoral integrity; Representation of People Act; Election Commission
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → EVM technology; totalisers; voter privacy; electoral engineering

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6kb 0;">
A Supreme Court Bench led by CJI Surya Kant (Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana) reviewed a PIL on totalisers on September 1, 2026. A totaliser is a device that clusters the vote counts from 14 EVMs before displaying results, so that voting patterns from individual booths cannot be identified — preventing post-election targeting of voters. BEL (Bengaluru) and ECIL (Hyderabad) developed totalisers; the ECI has supported their use since 2008. However, the Centre has opposed totalisers since 2017 (a bench led by Justice Dipak Misra cited data breach risk). In October 2025, a ministers' group led by Rajnath Singh decided there is "no intimidation at large scale" and maintained opposition to totalisers. A Law Ministry affidavit conveyed this to the SC. The case is a live debate about voter privacy, booth-level data, and the balance between transparency and anonymity in Indian elections.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) — BEL and ECIL:</strong> India's EVMs are manufactured by two public sector units: Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL, Bengaluru) and Electronics Corporation of India Limited (ECIL, Hyderabad). EVMs are standalone, non-networked machines with a one-time programmable microcontroller — they cannot be hacked remotely. The Voter-Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) was added post-2013 to allow voters to verify their vote.</li>
<li><strong>Totaliser Mechanism:</strong> A totaliser is an additional hardware device that aggregates vote counts from multiple EVMs (up to 14) before displaying constituency-level results. Without a totaliser, results can be displayed booth-by-booth, enabling political parties to identify which booths voted for which candidate — creating potential for voter intimidation or targeting of communities that voted against the local strongman. Totalisers prevent this by making the granular booth data invisible in the final count.</li>
<li><strong>ECI's Support and Centre's Opposition:</strong> The Election Commission of India recommended totalisers in 2008 after requesting BEL/ECIL to develop them. However, successive Central governments (including UPA and NDA) have opposed totalisers, with the 2017 opposition citing risk of data breach during the totaliser aggregation process. Critics allege the real reason is that booth-level data is valuable for political parties' micro-targeting strategies.</li>
<li><strong>Voter Privacy — Constitutional Dimension:</strong> The secrecy of the ballot is a foundational democratic principle. While Article 19(1)(a) does not explicitly protect voting secrecy, the Representation of People Act 1951 (Section 128) imposes obligations on election officials to maintain vote secrecy. Booth-level data analysis that enables identifying how a village voted arguably violates the spirit of ballot secrecy even if the individual vote is technically secret.</li>
<li><strong>PIL and Supreme Court's Role in Electoral Reforms:</strong> The Supreme Court has been an active driver of electoral reforms through PIL jurisdiction. Landmark cases include Association for Democratic Reforms (2002) mandating disclosure of candidate assets/criminal records, PUCL v. Union of India (2013) recognising NOTA (None of the Above) as a voter right, and Lily Thomas (2013) on disqualification of convicted legislators. The totaliser PIL continues this tradition of citizen-led electoral reform advocacy through courts.</li>
</ul>
</div>

The 26th SCO Summit was held in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan on August 31–September 1, 2026, marking 25 years since SCO's founding on June 15, 2001 (evolved from the Shanghai Five). **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>SCO — Structure and Mandate:</strong> The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation was founded June 15, 2001 in Shanghai, evolving from the Shanghai Five (China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan) with the addition of Uzbekistan. India and Pakistan joined as full members in 2017; Iran joined in 2023; Belarus in 2024. SCO focuses on regional security (counter-terrorism through RATS — Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure), economic cooperation, and cultural exchange. The SCO Charter governs its functioning; its Secretariat is in Beijing.</li>
<li><strong>India's SCO Membership — Strategic Calculations:</strong> India joined SCO despite reservations about China's dominance, viewing membership as a platform for Eurasian engagement, counter-terrorism cooperation (particularly on Afghanistan), and connectivity via the North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC). However, India has blocked China-Pakistan-driven connectivity projects (CPEC being part of BRI which India opposes as it passes through PoK) within SCO forums.</li>
<li><strong>Operation Sindoor (May 2025):</strong> India conducted military strikes on terrorist infrastructure in Pakistan and Pakistan-Occupied Kashmir in May 2025, following a major terrorist attack on Indian civilians. This significantly worsened India-Pakistan relations. India-Pakistan continuing within the same SCO framework creates diplomatic tension, as Pakistan chairs the next SCO summit — raising questions about India's attendance and the SCO's ability to function as a coherent body with two members in near-conflict.</li>
<li><strong>SCO and the US-Iran War:</strong> The Bishkek Declaration's condemnation of US-Israel strikes on Iran (which killed Khamenei on February 28, 2026) represents SCO positioning as a counterweight to Western-led international order. Iran's SCO membership since 2023 makes this condemnation institutionally significant. India's position — given its US partnership and Iran relations — requires careful calibration; India typically abstains or offers nuanced positions on such declarations.</li>
<li><strong>SCO Development Bank:</strong> The announcement of an SCO Development Bank represents an attempt to create a Eurasian development financing institution parallel to the World Bank and ADB. Russia and China have been the primary drivers. India's participation will depend on governance structures ensuring non-Chinese dominance — a pattern seen in India's selective engagement with the AIIB (where India joined) vs. the NDB (where India is a founding member). The bank's mandate and capitalisation structure were not finalised at the Bishkek summit.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Power beyond the pulpit: Kanthapuram A.P. Aboobacker</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>

- GS Paper II → Governance → Secularism; state-religion interface; Muslim personal law; minority rights
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Yemen diplomacy; consular protection

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Kanthapuram A.P. Aboobacker Musliyar (age 89) is the Grand Mufti of India and head of the Jamia Markaz educational complex near Kozhikode, Kerala. He exercises significant religious authority over Sunni Muslim communities in South India through Samastha Kerala Jamiyyathul Ulama (though a 1989 split led to a separate Sunni faction). He has drawn controversy for circular letters restricting women's presence in public during Islamic events. He gained positive attention in 2025 for intervening to save Nimisha Priya, a Kerala nurse sentenced to death in Yemen for murdering a Yemeni national, by facilitating negotiations with the victim's family for blood money (diya) payment. He was also questioned by CBI in connection with the mysterious disappearance of Islamic scholar Chekannur Moulavi in 1993, though he was not accused.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Samastha Kerala Jamiyyathul Ulama:</strong> One of the largest Muslim religious organisations in Kerala, representing the Sunni Barelvi tradition. Founded in 1926, it has significant influence over Islamic education, mosque management, and community affairs. The 1989 split created competing factions; Kanthapuram leads one; the other is Sunni Yuvajana Sangham-aligned. Both factions exercise considerable influence over Muslim community life in Kerala.</li>
<li><strong>Diya (Blood Money) in Islamic Law:</strong> Under Islamic Sharia criminal law practiced in Yemen and other Gulf states, the family of a murder victim can accept diya (blood money compensation) in lieu of qisas (equivalent retribution/execution). Payment of diya by the convicted person's family to the victim's family can stay the execution. Kanthapuram's intervention in the Nimisha Priya case involved facilitating the diya payment and family negotiations — a form of religious diplomacy that the Indian state often leverages through established religious intermediaries.</li>
<li><strong>Women's Participation in Islamic Public Life — Scholarly Debate:</strong> Different Islamic scholars hold divergent views on women's presence in public religious events, mosques, and festivals. Kanthapuram's restrictive circulars represent one conservative scholarly position within Sunni Islam; other South Asian Islamic scholars (Deobandi and Barelvi both have diverse internal opinions) and global bodies like Al-Azhar in Egypt take more permissive positions. This internal pluralism within Islamic scholarship is relevant to understanding minority community dynamics in India.</li>
<li><strong>Amman Message (2004):</strong> A fatwa-like declaration by King Abdullah II of Jordan and endorsed by Islamic scholars worldwide, including Kanthapuram, that defined who is a Muslim, prohibited takfir (declaring another Muslim a non-believer or apostate), and provided a framework for intra-Islamic tolerance. Kanthapuram's endorsement reflects his mainstream Sunni positioning against extremist ideologies.</li>
<li><strong>Consular Protection for Overseas Indians:</strong> India has obligations under the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963) to provide consular assistance to Indian nationals abroad facing criminal charges. The Nimisha Priya case highlighted how India's consular assistance — working through Indian embassies in Sana'a and through religious intermediaries — can supplement formal diplomatic channels, especially in states like Yemen with weak central governance during conflict periods.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Javelin (FGM-148) is a "fire-and-forget" anti-tank guided missile (ATGM) jointly developed by Raytheon Technologies and Lockheed Martin through the Javelin Joint Venture (JJV). A Memorandum of Understanding was signed between JJV and Tata Advanced Systems Limited (TASL) for co-production in India: sub-assembly kits from Lockheed Martin (Alabama) and guidance electronics from Raytheon (Arizona) are final-assembled at TASL's Indian facility. The Javelin has a 99% operational readiness rate and 94% engagement success rate, and has been combat-proven in Iraq (2003), Afghanistan, and Ukraine. India is also developing its own third-generation MPATGM (Man-Portable Anti-Tank Guided Missile) through DRDO. The Javelin's India production follows the US Ambassador's announcement on August 28, illustrating defence manufacturing cooperation under the US-India Major Defence Partnership.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>FGM-148 Javelin — Technical Profile:</strong> The Javelin uses infrared imaging seeker technology with "fire-and-forget" capability — the operator can take cover immediately after launch. It has two attack modes: direct (front) and top-attack (striking tank roof armour, which is thinner). Range: 65–4,750 metres. Warhead: tandem HEAT (High Explosive Anti-Tank). Weight (with CLU): ~22 kg. It was first used in combat in Iraq in 2003 and has been extensively deployed in Ukraine since 2022.</li>
<li><strong>Make in India in Defence:</strong> India's Make in India defence policy (launched 2014) targets raising domestic defence production to ₹1.75 lakh crore by 2025 and increasing defence exports. The Javelin co-production MoU with TASL fits the "Make in India" framework where India receives technology transfer and local manufacturing content. Defence FDI limit was raised to 74% (automatic route) and 100% (government route) in 2020.</li>
<li><strong>DRDO's MPATGM (Third Generation):</strong> India's Defence Research and Development Organisation is developing the Man-Portable Anti-Tank Guided Missile (MPATGM) — a fire-and-forget ATGM to replace aging Milan missiles. The MPATGM uses IIR (Imaging Infra-Red) seeker similar to Javelin. Development has faced delays; the system had not been inducted as of 2026. The Javelin co-production creates a strategic hedge — accessing proven technology while MPATGM development continues.</li>
<li><strong>US-India Defence Partnership:</strong> India is a "Major Defence Partner" of the US (a status conferred in 2016, above what is offered to most allies) and has signed foundational defence agreements: LEMOA (2016), COMCASA (2018), and BECA (2020). These enable information sharing, interoperability, and access to advanced defence technology. The Javelin MoU is consistent with the US-India initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology (iCET) and the Defence Industrial Cooperation Roadmap.</li>
<li><strong>Tata Advanced Systems Limited (TASL):</strong> TASL is Tata Group's aerospace and defence manufacturing subsidiary, which has made in India C-17 aerostructures for Boeing, assembled Sikorsky S-92 helicopters, and produces defence electronics. Its partnership with Lockheed Martin JJV for Javelin assembly makes it a key node in India's private defence industrial base — an ecosystem the government is actively building as a strategic alternative to DRDO/OFB-dominated defence production.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Striped hyenas help keep India clean, but remain misunderstood</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 10 · Science</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Wildlife conservation; biodiversity; WPA 1972; scavengers; ecosystem services
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Conservation science; pesticide threats; wildlife policy

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Striped hyenas (Hyaena hyaena), India's only hyena species, play a critical ecological role as scavengers — consuming carrion, controlling disease spread, and recycling nutrients. Globally, 5,000–14,000 striped hyenas survive, with India supporting an estimated 1,000–3,000. Yet there is no official census and no National Action Plan for their conservation, despite their Schedule I protection under the Wildlife Protection Act 1972. The primary threat is inadvertent poisoning by profinil (a common pesticide used on poultry carcasses to kill pests) — which also kills hyenas and other scavengers including vultures. Tamil Nadu imposes a ₹14 lakh penalty for killing hyenas, but cultural stigma — associating hyenas with grave robbery and black magic — drives human-animal conflict and persecution. Conservation attention has overwhelmingly focused on tigers and elephants, leaving scavengers like hyenas in a policy vacuum.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 — Schedule I:</strong> Animals listed in Schedule I of the WPA 1972 receive the highest protection: hunting, capturing, trapping, or trading them is a cognisable, non-bailable offence with imprisonment up to 7 years. Schedule I includes tigers, lions, snow leopards, elephants, rhinoceroses, and striped hyenas. However, Schedule I listing does not automatically trigger active conservation programmes or surveys — resources flow to "charismatic megafauna" (tigers, elephants) through designated projects.</li>
<li><strong>Ecosystem Services of Scavengers:</strong> Scavengers like hyenas, vultures, and jackals provide the "ecological cleaning" service of consuming carcasses, which prevents pathogen spread (anthrax, brucellosis, foot-and-mouth disease can persist in carcasses for days or months without scavengers). The economic value of vulture scavenging alone in India has been estimated at ₹1,000 crore+ annually in disease prevention and carcass disposal cost savings.</li>
<li><strong>Profinil (Profenofos) Pesticide Threat:</strong> Profinil is an organophosphate pesticide used widely in agriculture and poultry farming. When applied to livestock carcasses to kill flies and maggots, it creates a lethal poison bait for scavengers. India's National Action Plan for Vultures (2020) identified poisoning as the primary threat to vulture recovery; the same threat applies to hyenas, jackals, and other scavengers. EU and several countries have banned organophosphate uses that create secondary poisoning risks.</li>
<li><strong>Human-Wildlife Conflict — Cultural Dimensions:</strong> Striped hyenas face persecution rooted in cultural beliefs associating them with witchcraft, grave-robbing, and bad omens in parts of India and West Asia. Unlike tiger or elephant conflict (which involves economic damage), hyena conflict is often driven by fear and superstition — requiring different conservation communication strategies that address cultural attitudes rather than just economic compensation.</li>
<li><strong>Absence of National Action Plan — Policy Gap:</strong> India has National Action Plans for project animals (tigers, elephants, snow leopards, vultures, rhinos, bustards) but none for hyenas despite their Schedule I status. This reflects the "charismatic megafauna bias" in conservation policy — resources and attention concentrate on species with high public visibility and flagship status. IUCN categorises striped hyenas as Near Threatened globally.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
BODH (Bhartiya Oriented Design for High-performance), launched in September 2025, is a training programme to help Indian scientists in government research institutions develop leadership, management, and communication skills alongside their technical expertise. Participating institutions include ICAR-IARI (agriculture), CSIR-IMMT (minerals and materials), IIT-Hyderabad, and CSIR-CSIO (scientific instruments). **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India's Research Ecosystem — CSIR, ICAR, ICMR, DBT:</strong> India has an extensive network of government research institutions: CSIR (38 labs across scientific domains), ICAR (100+ institutes for agriculture), ICMR (26 institutes for biomedical research), and DBT (Department of Biotechnology). These institutions employ thousands of scientists but have been criticised for bureaucratic culture, hierarchical management, and insufficient industry linkage, limiting translational research output.</li>

This contrasts with leading US and European research universities where graduate programmes include research management training. The gap affects lab productivity, postdoc/student welfare, and knowledge translation.</li>
<li><strong>NITI Aayog Science and Technology Vision:</strong> NITI Aayog's Science & Technology Innovation Policy (STIP 2020, revised) emphasises building India's research capacity with targets for R&D expenditure (currently ~0.7% of GDP vs. 2–4% in advanced nations) and researcher density. Programmes like BODH that address the human capital dimension of research capacity — beyond just funding — are aligned with STIP's goals.</li>
<li><strong>PRABODH Module — Mentoring in Research:</strong> The PRABODH module within BODH focuses on deeper, sustained mentoring relationships between senior and early-career scientists. Research on mentoring in STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics) consistently shows that quality mentoring is the single most important factor in early-career research productivity and retention, particularly for women and first-generation researchers from non-elite institutions.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">Dragonflies are bellwethers of earth's ecosystems</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
<span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>
<span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 10 · Science</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Biodiversity; indicator species; wetland ecosystems; pesticide threats
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Entomology; biological indicators; conservation science

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India hosts approximately 600 species of dragonflies and damselflies (Order Odonata) — about 20% of the world's known species — making it one of the most biodiverse countries for this ancient insect group (300 million-year evolutionary lineage). Dragonflies are sensitive indicators of freshwater ecosystem health, as they require clean water for larval development and are directly affected by pesticide contamination (particularly profinil), water pollution, and wetland degradation. The blue hawklet (Hylaeothemis apicalis) is among threatened Indian species. A PNAS study (112(11)) highlighted that dragonflies possess 16+ types of visual opsin genes — the highest of any animal — enabling complex colour vision. As wetlands face increasing pressure from agricultural encroachment and pollution, monitoring dragonfly populations provides an accessible, low-cost early warning system for ecosystem decline.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Order Odonata — Evolutionary Significance:</strong> Dragonflies (suborder Anisoptera) and damselflies (suborder Zygoptera) belong to Order Odonata, one of the oldest winged insect orders — fossils date to 325 million years. Their persistence through five mass extinctions demonstrates evolutionary resilience. Their sensitivity to current environmental changes (wetland loss, pesticides) makes them paradoxically ancient yet modern indicators of ecological stress.</li>
<li><strong>Indicator Species in Biodiversity Assessment:</strong> An indicator species is one whose presence, abundance, or condition reflects the state of an ecosystem. Dragonflies serve as indicators for freshwater quality because their larvae are aquatic and acutely sensitive to dissolved oxygen levels, temperature, pH, and chemical pollutants. The Ramsar Convention on Wetlands uses macroinvertebrate (including Odonata larvae) surveys for wetland health assessment.</li>
<li><strong>Ramsar Sites and Wetland Conservation in India:</strong> India has 85 Ramsar sites (wetlands of international importance) — more than any country. These sites face threats from agricultural runoff (pesticides, fertilizers), encroachment, and water extraction. Monitoring Odonata populations at Ramsar sites provides a cost-effective biodiversity health check. The National Wetland Conservation Programme (NWCP) funds such monitoring through MoEF&CC.</li>
<li><strong>Profinil (Organophosphate) as a Systemic Threat:</strong> As noted in the hyena article, profinil (profenofos) threatens multiple trophic levels. For Odonata, it acts as a systemic insecticide — when applied to crops or poultry environments, it contaminates water bodies through runoff, killing dragonfly larvae and disrupting the insect-based food web that supports fish, amphibians, and birds. A cascade effect depletes freshwater biodiversity.</li>
<li><strong>Visual Opsin Genes — Significance for Research:</strong> Opsin genes encode the photoreceptor proteins that enable colour vision. Dragonflies' possession of 16+ opsin gene types (compared to 3 in humans: red, green, blue) enables UV vision and compound colour processing. Research on dragonfly opsins has applications in understanding the evolution of sensory systems and in developing bio-inspired optical sensors for technology. This basic research dimension illustrates the value of biodiversity beyond direct ecological services.</li>
</ul>
</div>

India's crypto investment landscape is seeing rapid democratisation: ₹51,000 crore was invested in crypto assets in 2024–25, with a significant shift toward SIP-style (Systematic Investment Plan) periodic investments — mimicking the mutual fund SIP model that has been highly successful in drawing retail investors. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Cryptocurrency Regulation in India:</strong> India does not have a comprehensive crypto law. The Cryptocurrency and Regulation of Official Digital Currency Bill — long-awaited — has not been enacted. Virtual Digital Assets (VDAs) are taxed at 30% on gains and 1% TDS on transactions (Finance Act 2022 provisions), which has constrained trading volumes but not eliminated the market. <li><strong>Tier-2/Tier-3 City Retail Investor Risk:</strong> First-generation retail investors in small cities typically have less financial literacy, less access to independent financial advice, and less institutional support (no broker networks, SEBI-registered advisors) than metro investors. Their entry into high-risk crypto assets via SIPs replicates the financial inclusion journey of equity mutual funds in the 2010s but in an unregulated, higher-risk environment — raising significant consumer protection concerns.</li>
<li><strong>Global Regulatory Context — MiCA (EU):</strong> The EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA, 2023) is the world's first comprehensive crypto regulation, covering crypto asset issuance, exchange operation, stablecoin rules, and consumer protection. India's inaction stands in contrast to both MiCA (regulatory clarity) and the US SEC's enforcement-based approach. The RBI has repeatedly warned of crypto's risks to financial stability and monetary policy transmission.</li>
<li><strong>Virtual Digital Asset Tax (30% + 1% TDS):</strong> India's Finance Act 2022 introduced 30% flat tax on VDA gains (no set-off against losses from other sources or other VDA) and 1% TDS on VDA transactions above ₹10,000. This punitive tax structure — higher than equities (10% LTCG, 15% STCG) — aims to discourage speculative crypto investment without an outright ban. Critics argue it pushes crypto trading to offshore platforms, reducing tax compliance rather than investment volumes.</li>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">'U.S. LPG is not a silver bullet for India'</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
<span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Energy</span>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
James Rockall, CEO of the World LPG Association (WLGA), cautioned that while India currently sources 67% of its LPG from the United States (as confirmed by Petroleum Minister Hardeep Puri), over-reliance on a single supplier creates supply chain vulnerability. Rockall recommended that India expand its LPG procurement to Canada, Algeria, Argentina, Australia, and West Africa to build a diversified, resilient supply base. He also flagged the need to expand India's LPG storage infrastructure and to better manage freight costs through short-term shipping charters or vessel ownership. The advice is significant as term contracts (which India has with US suppliers) can constrain flexibility in short-term procurement adjustments. India's LPG story — from fuel-starved kitchens to universal Ujjwala Yojana coverage — is a policy success, but supply-side diversification remains a strategic priority for energy security.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY):</strong> Launched in 2016, PMUY provides free LPG connections to BPL (Below Poverty Line) households — primarily targeting women from SC/ST, backward classes, and poor families. By 2023, over 9.6 crore connections were given under PMUY. The scheme dramatically expanded LPG access in rural India, reducing indoor air pollution from biomass cooking — one of the top causes of premature death in India (4 lakh+ deaths annually from HAP). PMUY Phase II covers 1.6 crore additional connections.</li>
<li><strong>India's LPG Import Dependence:</strong> India is the world's second-largest LPG importer. Domestic LPG production (from refineries and gas fields) covers only about 40% of demand; the rest is imported. The US shale gas revolution made American LPG (propane/butane mix) competitively priced, leading to India's rapid shift to US sourcing. However, 67% dependence on one country creates supply disruption risk (geopolitical, weather, logistical).</li>
<li><strong>LPG Supply Chain — Storage and Freight:</strong> India's LPG storage infrastructure is under strain as demand has grown faster than storage capacity. Storage at import terminals (Mangalore, Kandla, Haldia, Vizag) and inland bottling plants determines the buffer available during supply disruptions. Freight costs for LPG (carried in VLGCs — Very Large Gas Carriers) are volatile, especially when India's procurement is concentrated on long-haul US cargoes vs. shorter-haul Middle Eastern or African sources.</li>
<li><strong>Energy Security — Diversification Principle:</strong> The IEA (International Energy Agency) principle of energy security includes diversity of supply (multiple source countries), diversity of fuel types (energy mix), and strategic reserves. India's IEA-style Strategic Petroleum Reserve covers crude oil but not LPG. Diversifying LPG sources to Canada, Algeria, Argentina is consistent with the energy security doctrine India professes in policy documents.</li>
<li><strong>India-US LPG Trade and Bilateral Energy Relationship:</strong> The US became a significant LPG supplier to India after 2018 as India's LPG demand surged with PMUY expansion. This energy trade serves US interests (reducing trade deficit with India via commodity exports) and India's interests (diversifying from Middle Eastern dependence). However, any US LPG price advantage must be weighed against transit costs via longer shipping routes compared to Gulf sources.</li>
</ul>

It accounts for ~80% of India's domestic coal production. CIL's subsidiaries include SECL (Chhattisgarh), ECL (West Bengal), BCCL (Jharkhand), MCL (Odisha), NCL (Madhya Pradesh), WCL (Maharashtra), CMPDIL (technical), and NEC (Nagaland). Open-cast mining dominates (>90% of production) vs. underground mining.</li>
<li><strong>Monsoon Impact on Open-Cast Mining:</strong> Virtually all of CIL's open-cast mines are located in peninsular India (Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, West Bengal), which receives heavy monsoon rainfall from June–September. Waterlogging of mine pits, unstable slopes, and poor visibility halt large equipment operations. Overburden removal (stripping the soil and rock above coal seams) is particularly sensitive to wet conditions, making post-monsoon overburden catch-up critical for coal seam accessibility in the following quarters.</li>
<li><strong>Coal Gasification — National Mission:</strong> Coal gasification converts coal into syngas (CO + H₂) through controlled combustion with steam/oxygen. Syngas can be used for fertilizer production (ammonia from H₂), chemicals, and electricity. The government's National Coal Gasification Mission (2021) targets 100 MT of coal gasification by 2030. Talcher Fertilisers Ltd (a joint venture of NFL, CIL, GAIL, and FCIL) is building India's first coal gasification-based urea fertilizer plant in Talcher, Odisha — aiming to reduce India's dependence on natural gas-based urea imports.</li>
<li><strong>Coal's Role in India's Energy Mix:</strong> India generates ~70% of its electricity from coal-fired thermal plants (total installed capacity ~340 GW thermal, of which ~210 GW is coal). Despite rapid renewable energy expansion (solar: ~90 GW, wind: ~45 GW by 2026), coal remains essential for base load power, particularly in winter and pre-monsoon months when solar output is lower and demand is higher. India's NDC under the Paris Agreement targets 500 GW of non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030, but does not commit to coal phase-out.</li>
<li><strong>Environmental Consequences of Coal Dependence:</strong> India's coal mining and combustion contribute to: PM2.5 and SO₂ air pollution (causing ~1.4 lakh premature deaths annually, WHO estimates), fly ash disposal challenges (80% utilisation target under fly ash notification, partially achieved), land degradation from mine spoil dumps, and CO₂ emissions (~2.8 GT CO₂ equivalent annually from coal). India argues that per-capita emissions (2.4 tonnes CO₂/year) are far below OECD levels, justifying coal use for development — a central argument in climate negotiations.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
128 countries reached agreement on a definitional text for Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) in Geneva on September 6, 2026, under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) framework, with the Netherlands overseeing the talks. The agreed definition is a prerequisite for any binding treaty. The ICRC has warned that LAWS violate International Humanitarian Law (IHL) principles of distinction and proportionality — autonomous systems cannot reliably distinguish civilians from combatants or make proportionate harm assessments. However, the US, Russia, India, and Israel were identified as "key opponents of negotiations" for a binding LAWS ban. Brazil, Ireland, and Norway are leading advocates for a treaty. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), 1980:</strong> An umbrella treaty under the UN framework banning or restricting certain conventional weapons deemed excessively injurious or indiscriminate. Its five protocols cover non-detectable fragments (P.I), mines and booby-traps (P.II), incendiary weapons (P.III), blinding laser weapons (P.IV), and explosive remnants of war (P.V). LAWS discussions take place in a Group of Governmental Experts under CCW. Decisions under CCW require consensus — giving any state a veto over treaty text.</li>
<li><strong>Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) — "Killer Robots":</strong> LAWS are weapon systems that can select and engage targets without meaningful human control. The spectrum ranges from anti-drone systems (high autonomy, low strategic stakes) to fully autonomous battlefield weapons that decide to kill humans without real-time human authorisation. Current drone systems (Israeli Harpy, Loitering Munitions) already operate at the autonomy threshold, making definitional clarity for regulation urgent.</li>
<li><strong>India's Position on LAWS:</strong> India has not supported a binding ban, arguing that the pace of technological development makes premature regulation counterproductive and that any framework must accommodate defensive autonomous systems (anti-drone, base protection). India is developing autonomous drone swarms and counter-drone systems through DRDO; a binding LAWS ban could constrain this capability development, explaining India's opposition to strict treaty language.</li>
<li><strong>International Humanitarian Law (IHL) — Distinction and Proportionality:</strong> IHL (Geneva Conventions 1949, Additional Protocols 1977) requires parties to conflict to distinguish between combatants and civilians (distinction principle) and to avoid disproportionate civilian harm (proportionality principle). Critics of LAWS argue that autonomous systems cannot make these contextual, ethical judgments — particularly in complex urban battlefields — requiring human oversight (meaningful human control) for all lethal decisions.</li>
<li><strong>AI Governance and Weapons — Global Context:</strong> The November 2023 AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park produced the Bletchley Declaration on AI safety, signed by 28 countries including India. However, military AI is expressly outside the scope of most civilian AI governance frameworks. Autonomous weapons represent the most contested frontier of AI governance — where technological competition (US, China, Russia) intersects with IHL and global security architecture.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<li><strong>Climate Attribution Science:</strong> Climate attribution is the scientific field that quantifies how much human-induced climate change altered the probability or intensity of specific extreme weather events. <li><strong>Loss and Damage — UNFCCC COP28 (2023):</strong> At COP28 (Dubai, 2023), parties agreed to establish a Loss and Damage Fund to compensate developing nations for climate impacts they cannot adapt to. Climate attribution science is foundational to quantifying loss and damage — establishing that specific damages were caused by human-induced climate change (not natural variability). India, as a vulnerable developing nation, has a strong interest in this science for diplomatic positioning.</li>
<li><strong>India's Climate Research Capacity:</strong> India's climate research is distributed across IMD (Indian Meteorological Department), IITM Pune (Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology), NCMRWF (National Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasting), and IIT atmospheric science centres.
