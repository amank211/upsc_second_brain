**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Prime Minister Narendra Modi visited Tashkent on August 31, 2026, and elevated India-Uzbekistan ties to a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (CSP) in talks with President Shavkat Mirziyoyev. The two sides signed 11 agreements covering defence, connectivity, trade, and technology. A landmark Uranium Supply Framework was concluded — significant because Uzbekistan is among the world's top uranium producers — to support India's expanding civil nuclear programme. A UPI-UZQR payment interoperability pact was also signed, extending India's real-time digital payment network into Central Asia. Bilateral trade is targeted at $5 billion by 2030, up sharply from current levels. President Mirziyoyev conferred the Oliy Darajali Do'stlik Order — Uzbekistan's highest state civilian decoration — on PM Modi. Following the Tashkent visit, Modi departed for Bishkek (Kyrgyzstan) to attend the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) Heads of State Summit. The visit is seen as part of India's "Connect Central Asia" strategy and aligns with its pursuit of energy security and multimodal connectivity via the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC).
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (CSP):</strong> India uses tiered bilateral relationship designations — Strategic Partnership, Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, Special Strategic Partnership — to signal the depth of engagement. A CSP typically covers political, economic, defence, and people-to-people pillars. India has CSPs with countries including Russia, the UAE, and Australia.</li>
<li><strong>Uzbekistan's uranium reserves:</strong> Uzbekistan holds approximately 7% of global uranium reserves and is among the top ten producers globally. India's civil nuclear programme, anchored by the Nuclear Power Corporation of India (NPCIL), is expanding installed capacity to 22,480 MW by 2031-32 and requires assured uranium supply to fuel its Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWRs) and upcoming light-water reactors.</li>
<li><strong>UPI International Expansion:</strong> The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), governed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), has been extended to over 10 countries for cross-border remittance and merchant payments. The UPI-UZQR pact with Uzbekistan follows earlier integrations with UAE, Singapore, France, and Bhutan, and supports India's goal of making UPI a global digital public infrastructure standard.</li>
<li><strong>Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO):</strong> The SCO is a Eurasian political, economic, and security organisation with nine full members — China, India, Pakistan, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, and Iran (added 2023). India holds the Presidency of the SCO in 2023-24 cycle. The Heads of State Council is the apex body, meeting annually. Connectivity, counter-terrorism, and trade are its core agenda items.</li>
<li><strong>Connect Central Asia Policy:</strong> India's "Connect Central Asia" policy, articulated in 2012, seeks to deepen engagement with the five Central Asian republics (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan) through the India-Central Asia Dialogue mechanism and multimodal connectivity projects such as Chabahar Port (Iran) and the INSTC. Central Asia is critical for access to Afghan markets and as an overland corridor to Russia and Europe.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  788 dead, 2,500 missing in Nepal; glacial collapse triggers catastrophic floods
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper I → Geography → Himalayan glacial systems; glacial lake outburst floods; river basin geography
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Nepal bilateral relations; disaster diplomacy
- GS Paper III → Disaster Management → Early warning systems; transboundary disaster response; NDMA

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A glacial collapse in Rasuwa district, northern Nepal, triggered a Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF) that cascaded through the Bhotekoshi and Trishuli river systems, killing at least 788 people and leaving 2,502 missing as of August 31, 2026. The scale of the disaster — among the worst in Nepal's recorded history — is attributed to accelerated glacial melting in the Himalayas driven by climate change, combined with poorly regulated hydropower infrastructure in narrow gorges. Over 900 workers at hydropower projects along the affected river valleys are among the missing. Multiple bridges, roads, and settlements along the river corridors have been destroyed, severing connectivity with remote hill districts. Preservation of the dead presents a serious humanitarian challenge given the terrain. India, which shares the Koshi and Gandaki river basins with Nepal and is downstream of the Trishuli system via the Narayani/Gandak, has offered disaster relief. The event underscores the need for a bilateral transboundary GLOF early warning mechanism between India and Nepal.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF):</strong> A GLOF occurs when a glacial lake — formed by meltwater dammed by ice, moraine, or bedrock — suddenly releases its stored water. The Himalayas host over 9,000 glacial lakes; Nepal alone has ~3,600. Rising temperatures accelerate ice melt, enlarging these lakes and increasing outburst risk. GLOFs can generate flood waves 10–30 times larger than typical monsoon floods and travel at extremely high speed.</li>
<li><strong>Bhotekoshi and Trishuli River Systems:</strong> The Bhotekoshi originates in Tibet and joins the Trishuli in Nepal's Sindhupalchok/Rasuwa districts — a corridor with several run-of-river hydropower projects. The Trishuli flows into the Narayani River, which crosses into India as the Gandak (Bihar), a tributary of the Ganga. Transboundary flooding in these rivers has previously affected Bihar's embankments and floodplains.</li>
<li><strong>India-Nepal Disaster Diplomacy:</strong> India and Nepal have a 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship and a long history of bilateral water-sharing frameworks (Koshi Agreement 1954, Gandak Agreement 1959). The two countries have no formal GLOF early warning system, though ICIMOD (International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development, Kathmandu) operates regional alerts. India's National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) has protocols for rapid response to transboundary events.</li>
<li><strong>Himalayan Hydropower Vulnerability:</strong> Nepal's hydropower sector (installed capacity ~2,800 MW) is concentrated in steep gorges prone to GLOF and landslide dam outbursts. Run-of-river plants — which lack large reservoirs to buffer flood surges — are especially vulnerable. The 2021 Chamoli disaster in Uttarakhand (India) similarly killed over 200 workers at hydropower projects. Both nations' energy plans depend heavily on Himalayan hydropower despite this risk.</li>
<li><strong>Climate Change and Himalayan Glaciers:</strong> The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) projects that even at 1.5°C warming, the Hindu Kush-Himalaya region will lose one-third of its glacier volume by 2100; at 3°C it could lose two-thirds. The region supplies freshwater to over 240 million people in the mountains and 1.65 billion downstream. India's National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) includes a "National Mission for Sustaining the Himalayan Ecosystem" (NMSHE) to monitor glacial lakes and ecosystem services.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
RSS chief Mohan Bhagwat, addressing an event at Madison Square Garden in New York, stated that Hindutva's vision of Bharat is inclusive of Muslims and all communities, countering the perception of Hindutva as an exclusionary ideology. The speech, delivered to a diaspora audience in the United States, sought to reframe Hindutva as a civilisational identity rooted in Bharat's cultural ethos rather than a religion-based political project. This represents a recurring diplomatic strategy by senior RSS leaders — distinguishing between Hindutva as "a way of life" and political communalism — particularly before global audiences. The statement follows sustained international criticism over communal incidents in India, the CAA-NRC debate, and questions raised before UN human rights bodies about India's treatment of Muslim minorities. Bhagwat's assertion carries political salience ahead of the 2027 state elections cycle and amid scrutiny of India's constitutional secularism from the CERD committee.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS, est. 1925):</strong> Founded by K.B. Hedgewar at Nagpur, the RSS is the ideological parent of the Sangh Parivar — a family of organisations including the BJP, VHP, and Bajrang Dal. The RSS does not contest elections but exerts significant influence over BJP governance, cadre training, and social policy. Its chief (Sarsanghchalak) is Mohan Bhagwat (since 2009).</li>
<li><strong>Hindutva — Doctrine and Judicial Interpretation:</strong> Vinayak Damodar Savarkar coined "Hindutva" in his 1923 tract, defining it as a cultural-civilisational identity shared by all born on the Indian subcontinent. The Supreme Court in Manohar Joshi vs. Nitin Bhaurao Patil (1996) held that Hindutva per se is not a religion and its use in campaign speeches does not automatically constitute a "corrupt practice" under the Representation of People Act, 1951 — a ruling that has shaped electoral jurisprudence.</li>
<li><strong>Secularism in the Indian Constitution:</strong> "Secular" was inserted into the Preamble by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment (1976). Articles 25–28 guarantee freedom of religion. India practices "positive secularism" — the state can regulate religious affairs (especially social reform) unlike the strict church-state separation of Western models. This permits laws like the Hindu Code Bills while allowing Muslim personal law under the MPLB to continue.</li>
<li><strong>CERD and India's International Standing:</strong> The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD) reviewed India's record in 2026 for the first time since 2007, expressing "grave concern" over treatment of minorities, manual scavenging, and the NRC process. India ratified ICERD in 1968. The committee's recommendations are non-binding but carry reputational weight before international bodies.</li>
<li><strong>India's Muslim Population and Sachar Committee:</strong> Muslims constitute approximately 14.2% of India's population (2011 Census). The Sachar Committee Report (2006) documented significant socio-economic gaps — in education, employment, and political representation — between Muslims and the national average. The PM Vikas scheme (formerly PMJVK) targets minority welfare, including skill development and educational infrastructure for minority-concentrated areas.</li>
</ul>
</div>

- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Federalism; separation of powers; High Court/Supreme Court jurisdiction over executive appointments

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Devendra Nath Mahto, leading a Jan Chetna Yatra (public awareness march) across Jharkhand, is demanding systemic reform of the state's government recruitment examination process following a series of paper leaks and irregularities. The Jharkhand Public Service Commission (JPSC) and Jharkhand Staff Selection Commission (JSSC) have faced intense scrutiny after 22 recruitment examinations were cancelled in recent years amid allegations of large-scale paper leaks, impersonation, and corruption. Student organisations and unemployed youth have staged sustained protests. The Jharkhand High Court has stayed several recruitment processes, and the Supreme Court has issued notice for a CBI probe into the JSSC scandal. The yatra aims to build political pressure on the Hemant Soren-led JMM government to overhaul the commissions, implement strong anti-paper-leak measures, and expedite the backlog of pending recruitments that has left thousands of government posts vacant — affecting public service delivery in one of India's most underdeveloped states.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Public Service Commissions (Constitutional Basis):</strong> Article 315 of the Constitution mandates a Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) and State Public Service Commissions (SPSCs). Article 316 governs appointment of members, and Article 320 lists their functions — primarily conducting examinations for civil service appointments. State PSCs are constitutionally insulated from executive interference, yet repeated paper leaks indicate institutional failure at the state level.</li>
<li><strong>Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024:</strong> Parliament enacted this central legislation to address the epidemic of paper leaks in competitive examinations. It prescribes imprisonment up to 10 years and fines up to ₹1 crore for organised cheating networks. The Act covers central examinations (UPSC, SSC, NTA) but does not directly govern state PSC/SSC examinations, leaving a regulatory gap at the state level.</li>
<li><strong>Jharkhand's Socio-economic Context:</strong> Jharkhand, carved out of Bihar in 2000 under the States Reorganisation process (Jharkhand Reorganisation Act, 2000), is among India's most resource-rich but poverty-stricken states. Its HDI ranks among the lowest in India. High youth unemployment and a large tribal population (approximately 26.2% — 3rd highest ST share nationally) make government jobs a critical aspiration; recruitment delays and corruption therefore have outsized social consequences.</li>
<li><strong>Role of CBI in State Matters:</strong> The Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) is a central agency under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946. It can investigate cases in states only with the state government's consent (general consent) or on court direction. Several states have withdrawn general consent, limiting CBI's jurisdiction. However, courts — including the Supreme Court — can direct CBI investigation irrespective of state consent in matters of public interest.</li>
<li><strong>Jan Chetna Yatra as Political Tool:</strong> Padayatras and jan chetna yatras have been a recurring strategy in Indian politics to build grassroots support and pressure governments — from Chandrababu Naidu's 2022 padayatra in AP to Rahul Gandhi's Bharat Jodo Yatra. In state politics, such marches serve both organisational and electoral functions, mobilising support ahead of assembly elections. Jharkhand's next assembly election is due in late 2024 (already held) — so this 2026 yatra is likely pre-2029 groundwork.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  T.N. moves SC, seeks release of Cauvery water backlog from Karnataka
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Federalism</span>
  <span style="background:#E8F2E8;color:#1A5C2A;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Agriculture</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Interstate water disputes; Supreme Court jurisdiction; federalism; Inter-State River Water Disputes Act
- GS Paper III → Agriculture → Irrigation; Cauvery delta farming; water use efficiency

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Tamil Nadu has filed a petition before the Supreme Court seeking immediate release of 17.604 tmc ft (thousand million cubic feet) of Cauvery water from Karnataka, citing non-compliance with the Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA) directive. Karnataka has withheld the allocation citing below-average reservoir storage levels at KRS (Krishna Raja Sagar) and Kabini dams this monsoon season. Tamil Nadu argues that the water is critical for the standing samba (paddy) crop in the Cauvery delta districts of Thanjavur, Tiruvarur, Nagapattinam, and Pudukottai. The Supreme Court, which retains supervisory jurisdiction over the implementation of the Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal Final Award (2007), has been periodically hearing compliance matters. The dispute encapsulates the enduring tension between upstream storage rights and downstream agricultural dependency — a perennial flashpoint in India's interstate water politics that successive legal and institutional mechanisms have failed to durably resolve.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT) Award, 2007:</strong> The CWDT, constituted under the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956, delivered its final award in 2007 allocating 740 tmc ft annually among Karnataka (284.75 tmc ft), Tamil Nadu (419 tmc ft), Kerala (30 tmc ft), and Puducherry (7 tmc ft). Karnataka's monthly release schedule to Tamil Nadu is specified in the award. The Supreme Court modified the award in 2018, enhancing Karnataka's share slightly and reducing Tamil Nadu's by a small margin.</li>
<li><strong>Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA):</strong> Constituted in 2018 pursuant to a Supreme Court order, the CWMA replaced the earlier Cauvery River Authority (presided over by the PM) as the statutory body to implement the tribunal award and regulate water releases. It is chaired by a senior civil servant and includes representatives of all four basin states. Despite its mandate, non-compliance by Karnataka during deficit years has repeatedly required SC intervention.</li>
<li><strong>Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956:</strong> The Act empowers the Union Government to constitute a tribunal to adjudicate water sharing disputes between states on inter-state rivers. Article 262 of the Constitution bars the jurisdiction of courts (including the Supreme Court) on such disputes — but the SC has claimed supervisory jurisdiction over the implementation of awards, a position maintained through a series of Cauvery-related orders.</li>
<li><strong>KRS Dam and Cauvery's Hydrology:</strong> The Krishna Raja Sagar (KRS) reservoir in Karnataka (capacity: 49.45 tmc ft) is the key storage facility on the Cauvery. Its inflows depend on the South-West Monsoon in the Western Ghats catchment. Deficit rainfall in Karnataka's Kodagu (Coorg) district — Cauvery's headwaters — directly reduces KRS storage and triggers conflict with Tamil Nadu's delta irrigation demands, which are highly inelastic (paddy cultivation requires water within specific crop-calendar windows).</li>
<li><strong>Samba Paddy Season and Water Dependency:</strong> Samba is a long-duration paddy variety grown in Tamil Nadu's Cauvery delta from August to January, requiring consistent irrigation water. The delta is one of India's most productive rice-growing belts and feeds into the southern food economy. Shortage of Cauvery water during the samba season can cause significant crop loss, creating strong political pressure on state governments to pursue water claims aggressively through legal channels.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  HC name to stay, despite Kerala becoming Keralam
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Constitutional amendments; state renaming; judiciary; Parliament's role in changing court nomenclature
- GS Paper II → Federalism → Centre-state relations; legislative competence over High Courts

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Even as the state of Kerala has officially been renamed "Keralam" following a state legislative resolution and a Parliamentary amendment to the First Schedule of the Constitution, the High Court of Kerala will retain its current name until a separate parliamentary legislation — specifically an amendment to the High Court Names Act — is passed. Article 214 of the Constitution establishes High Courts for each state, and the naming of High Courts is tied to the state's name in the Constitution, but changing the court's official name requires Parliament to amend the specific statutory provision governing the High Court's name. The Kerala government has passed a resolution requesting both changes, but Parliament's legislative calendar has not yet accommodated the High Court Name Amendment Bill. This illustrates the distinction between state renaming (First Schedule amendment) and consequential changes to judicial institutions, which require separate, specific legislation — a nuanced constitutional point with relevance for UPSC polity questions.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>State Renaming Procedure:</strong> Under Article 3 of the Constitution, Parliament has the power to alter the name of a state by a simple majority. Before introducing such a Bill, the President must refer it to the affected state legislature for its views, though Parliament is not bound to accept those views. The First Schedule of the Constitution lists all state and UT names; its amendment is required to formally rename a state. Kerala's renaming to "Keralam" reflects the Malayalam pronunciation of the state's name.</li>
<li><strong>High Courts — Constitutional Basis:</strong> Article 214 mandates that "there shall be a High Court for each State." Article 215 confers on each High Court the status of a court of record. The names of High Courts are specified in the High Court Act/Order applicable to each state; changing a High Court's name requires a parliamentary enactment because it is a creature of statute, not just of the state legislature's will.</li>

However, courts have generally held that proceedings remain valid irrespective of transitional nomenclature changes, pending formal amendment.</li>
<li><strong>States' Reorganisation and Language:</strong> Kerala was formed on November 1, 1956, under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, on the basis of Malayalam-speaking areas (formerly Travancore-Cochin and parts of Madras State). **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 124(3) — Appointment of SC Judges:</strong> A person may be appointed as a Supreme Court judge if they: (a) have been a High Court judge for 5+ years; (b) have been an advocate of a High Court for 10+ years; or (c) are, in the President's opinion, a "distinguished jurist." The BCI's periodic controversies over recognition, enrolment, and inspection norms affect thousands of law graduates. The NALSAR dispute referenced by Justice Bhuyan relates to BCI's oversight powers versus institutional autonomy of NLUs.</li>

The UK's Supreme Court has included Lord Sumption (an academic-turned-barrister) and other scholars. In India, former SC judge V.R. Krishna Iyer advocated for academic judges, arguing that constitutional adjudication benefits from theoretical rigour that practice-oriented lawyers may lack.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A joint study by Manipal Centre for Natural Sciences and ISRO, using data from three Aditya-L1 payloads — SUIT (Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope), SoLEXS (Solar Low Energy X-ray Spectrometer), and HEL1OS (High Energy L1 Orbiting X-ray Spectrometer) — has identified pre-flare transients: subtle UV and X-ray signatures in the solar corona that precede major solar flare eruptions by several minutes to hours. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Aditya-L1 Mission:</strong> Launched by ISRO on September 2, 2023, aboard PSLV-C57, Aditya-L1 is India's first solar observation spacecraft. It is positioned at the Sun-Earth L1 (Lagrange Point 1) — approximately 1.5 million km from Earth — where it maintains an unobstructed view of the Sun at all times. It carries 7 scientific payloads studying the solar corona, photosphere, and chromosphere. L1 was reached on January 6, 2024, making Aditya-L1 the first Indian spacecraft to reach a Lagrange point.</li>
<li><strong>Solar Flares and Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs):</strong> Solar flares are sudden, intense bursts of radiation from the Sun's surface, classified by X-ray intensity (A, B, C, M, X — with X being most intense). CMEs are large expulsions of plasma and magnetic field from the solar corona. X-class flares and associated CMEs can cause geomagnetic storms on Earth (measured by the Kp index), triggering auroras, disrupting HF radio communications, damaging satellites, inducing currents in power grids, and affecting pipeline corrosion. The 1989 Quebec blackout (caused by a solar storm) affected 6 million people.</li>
<li><strong>Space Weather and Critical Infrastructure:</strong> India's NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation) satellite navigation system, INSAT communication satellites, and weather forecasting satellites (Kalpana-1, INSAT-3DR) are all vulnerable to solar weather events. As India launches more operational satellites — including the upcoming NISAR (joint with NASA), Chandrayaan-4, and Gaganyaan crewed mission — space weather forecasting becomes a critical element of mission safety and national infrastructure resilience.</li>
<li><strong>Lagrange Points (L1–L5):</strong> Lagrange points are positions in space where the gravitational forces of two large bodies (e.g., Sun and Earth) and the centrifugal force of a third smaller body balance, allowing the smaller body to maintain a stable or quasi-stable position relative to both. L1 (between Sun and Earth) is ideal for solar observation. L2 (on the opposite side of Earth from the Sun) is where JWST and Gaia are placed — ideal for astronomical observation away from solar interference. India's Aditya-L1 and NASA's SOHO and ACE are all stationed at L1.</li>
<li><strong>SUIT Payload and UV Solar Imaging:</strong> SUIT (Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope) on Aditya-L1 is the first telescope to image the solar photosphere and chromosphere in the near-UV range (200–400 nm). Its observations complement X-ray data from SoLEXS and HEL1OS to provide a multi-wavelength view of solar activity. UV emissions from the chromosphere are known to precede flare eruptions, making SUIT's data particularly valuable for identifying pre-flare signatures — the subject of this study.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Government of India notified the Legal Metrology (Indian Standard Time) Rules, 2026 on August 27, mandating that Indian Standard Time (IST) — maintained by CSIR-National Physical Laboratory (NPL) in New Delhi — must be the sole reference for all timekeeping devices, digital systems, broadcast equipment, and critical infrastructure within India. This is a significant step towards "time sovereignty" — ensuring India's critical infrastructure runs on domestically controlled time signals rather than foreign-operated satellites that could, in theory, be denied or degraded during conflict.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Indian Standard Time (IST) and CSIR-NPL:</strong> IST is UTC+5:30 (5 hours 30 minutes ahead of Coordinated Universal Time). It is maintained by CSIR-National Physical Laboratory (NPL), New Delhi, using a bank of caesium atomic clocks. NPL's time signals are broadcast over AIR (All India Radio) and through NTP (Network Time Protocol) servers. Despite NPL maintaining IST, most devices in India historically synchronised to GPS time or internet time servers operated by foreign entities — the new rules address this dependency.</li>
<li><strong>NavIC (Navigation with Indian Constellation):</strong> NavIC (formerly IRNSS — Indian Regional Navigation Satellite System) is India's indigenous satellite navigation system, consisting of 7 operational satellites providing positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) services primarily over India and surrounding regions (up to 1,500 km from borders). NavIC's civilian signal (L5 and S-band) provides accuracy of ~20 metres. Its timing signal is now being positioned as the primary source for IST synchronisation across critical infrastructure.</li>
<li><strong>Legal Metrology Act, 2009:</strong> The Legal Metrology Act, 2009, replaced the Standards of Weights and Measures Act, 1976. It governs weights and measures standards, pre-packaged commodities, and approved instruments for commercial transactions. Time is a legal metrology parameter — clocks used in financial transactions, transport logs, and broadcasting are subject to calibration requirements. The new rules add IST synchronisation as a mandatory requirement under this Act's framework.</li>
<li><strong>GPS Dependency Risk:</strong> GPS (Global Positioning System) is operated by the US Space Force. It provides both positioning and time signals (GPS Time, 19 nanosecond accuracy). India's mobile networks, internet infrastructure, financial settlement systems (RTGS, NEFT operate on precise time), and power grid synchronisation all rely on GPS time. In a conflict scenario, the US could deny or degrade GPS signals — as it did during the Kargil War in 1999, when it denied India access to precise GPS coordinates. The new IST mandate addresses this structural dependency.</li>
<li><strong>Atomic Clocks and Time Sovereignty:</strong> Atomic clocks measure time based on the quantum transition frequency of atoms (typically caesium-133 at 9,192,631,770 Hz — the SI definition of the second). CSIR-NPL maintains India's primary frequency standard. Countries that cannot maintain their own primary time standard depend on foreign atomic clock networks for critical infrastructure synchronisation — a form of digital sovereignty gap. The USA, Russia, China, EU, Japan, and now India are asserting "time sovereignty" through domestic atomic clock infrastructure linked to indigenous satellite systems.</li>
</ul>

  How a star with an extreme magnetic field could prove space is not empty
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>

</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Astrophysics; quantum electrodynamics; space telescopes; fundamental physics

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
NASA's Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE) satellite has, for the first time, observationally confirmed vacuum birefringence — a phenomenon predicted by Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) theory in the 1930s by Heisenberg and Euler. The observation was made by studying the magnetar 1E 1547.0-5408, whose magnetic field is approximately 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ Gauss (a quadrillion times stronger than Earth's magnetic field). X-rays from this magnetar, detected up to 80% polarised by IXPE, indicate that the vacuum of space near a magnetar is not truly empty — extreme magnetic fields cause virtual electron-positron pairs to momentarily appear and disappear (quantum fluctuations), bending light differently based on its polarisation direction. This effect — vacuum birefringence — demonstrates that the quantum vacuum has real physical properties. Published in Nature on August 5, 2026, the finding validates a cornerstone of QED theory that could not previously be tested on Earth with available magnetic field strengths.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Quantum Electrodynamics (QED):</strong> QED is the relativistic quantum field theory of electrodynamics — it describes how light and matter interact at the quantum level. Developed by Feynman, Schwinger, and Tomonaga (Nobel Prize 1965), QED is the most precisely tested theory in physics. The Heisenberg-Euler Lagrangian (1936) predicted that in extremely strong magnetic fields, the quantum vacuum would exhibit birefringence — different refractive indices for differently polarised light — a direct consequence of virtual particle-antiparticle pair creation and annihilation.</li>
<li><strong>Magnetars:</strong> Magnetars are a class of neutron stars with extraordinarily strong magnetic fields (10¹³–10¹⁵ Gauss, compared to Earth's 0.5 Gauss). They are the remnants of massive stars that exploded as supernovae. Magnetar 1E 1547.0-5408 (in the constellation Norma, ~9 kpc from Earth) is a well-studied soft gamma repeater. Magnetars' extreme conditions cannot be replicated in any Earth laboratory, making them natural "laboratories" for testing extreme-physics predictions like vacuum birefringence.</li>
<li><strong>IXPE (Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer):</strong> IXPE is a NASA-ASI (Italian Space Agency) joint mission launched in December 2021. It is the first space observatory dedicated to measuring X-ray polarisation — the directional orientation of X-ray photons from astrophysical sources. Polarisation measurements provide information about the geometry of magnetic fields, emission mechanisms, and relativistic effects near extreme objects. IXPE has previously studied black hole systems, neutron stars, and supernova remnants.</li>
<li><strong>Vacuum Birefringence — Significance:</strong> In normal vacuum, light travels at the same speed regardless of polarisation. Birefringence (differential refractive index for different polarisations) was previously observed only in certain crystals and materials with anisotropic atomic structures. Vacuum birefringence predicted by QED requires magnetic fields of ~10¹⁶ Gauss — impossible on Earth. Confirming it near magnetars validates the QED framework in a previously untestable regime and opens doors for testing other extreme-physics theories (like loop quantum gravity and string theory predictions about vacuum structure).</li>
<li><strong>Neutron Stars and UPSC Relevance:</strong> Neutron stars are the collapsed cores of massive stars that end their lives as supernovae. They have densities of ~10¹⁴ g/cm³ — one teaspoon weighs approximately 1 billion tons. Types include pulsars (rotating neutron stars emitting radio beams), millisecond pulsars, and magnetars. The study of neutron stars tests fundamental physics including general relativity, nuclear physics, and QED — all at extremes impossible to create on Earth, making them windows into the deepest laws of nature.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**UPSC Mains Classification**

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (Roman), NASA's next major space observatory named after the "Mother of Hubble" Nancy Grace Roman (1925–2018), is scheduled for launch aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket and deployment at Lagrange Point 2 (L2). With a budget of approximately $4 billion, Roman is designed as a wide-field successor to the Hubble Space Telescope and a complement to the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). Its primary scientific objectives are: mapping dark energy and dark matter distribution across the universe, discovering thousands of exoplanets through gravitational microlensing, and conducting wide-area infrared surveys of galaxy evolution. Roman's field of view is 100 times larger than Hubble's, allowing it to survey massive volumes of sky rapidly. The telescope will provide critical data on why the universe's expansion is accelerating — one of the deepest unsolved questions in cosmology — through precise measurements of Type Ia supernova distances and baryon acoustic oscillations.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Nancy Grace Roman (1925–2018):</strong> Often called the "Mother of Hubble," Nancy Grace Roman was NASA's first Chief of Astronomy and played a pivotal role in planning the Hubble Space Telescope mission. The telescope's naming reflects NASA's recent practice of honouring women scientists who have been overlooked historically. Previous telescopes named for women scientists include Vera Rubin Observatory (dark matter research) and the Henrietta Lacks memorial programmes.</li>
<li><strong>Dark Energy and Dark Matter:</strong> Dark energy (~68% of the universe's energy content) is a hypothetical form of energy causing the accelerating expansion of the universe, first confirmed by Perlmutter, Schmidt, and Riess (Nobel Prize 2011) through observations of Type Ia supernovae. Dark matter (~27%) is non-luminous matter inferred from gravitational effects on visible matter, galaxy rotation curves, and cosmic microwave background data. Together they constitute ~95% of the universe — Roman's mission is to characterise both by mapping cosmic structure at unprecedented scale.</li>
<li><strong>Gravitational Microlensing for Exoplanet Detection:</strong> Gravitational microlensing occurs when a massive object passes in front of a background star — the foreground object's gravity bends and amplifies the star's light, temporarily brightening it. If the foreground object has a planet, the planet creates a secondary brightening event. This technique — unlike Kepler's transit method — can detect Earth-sized planets in wide orbits and planets around distant stars throughout the Milky Way. Roman will survey the galactic bulge for microlensing events, potentially discovering thousands of free-floating planets.</li>
<li><strong>Lagrange Point 2 (L2):</strong> L2 is a gravitationally stable point on the Sun-Earth line, approximately 1.5 million km beyond Earth (opposite the Sun). It is ideal for space telescopes because the satellite orbits with Earth, maintaining a constant geometry relative to the Sun. Both JWST and the European Space Agency's Gaia, Herschel, and Planck telescopes operated at L2. Roman will join JWST at L2, enabling coordinated observations of the same sky regions in near-infrared (Roman) and mid-infrared/visible (JWST) wavelengths.</li>
<li><strong>Generation of Space Telescopes — Hubble → JWST → Roman:</strong> The progression reflects increasing capabilities: Hubble (1990, visible/UV, 2.4m mirror) mapped the universe's structure and confirmed dark energy; JWST (2021, infrared, 6.5m mirror) observes the first galaxies and exoplanet atmospheres; Roman (infrared, 2.4m mirror but 100× wider field) will survey the cosmic web at scale. Each generation addresses questions revealed by the previous one — Roman's dark energy mapping builds directly on JWST's detailed observations of early universe structure.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Naxalite Movement — Origin and Evolution:</strong> The Naxalite movement originated in 1967 at Naxalbari, West Bengal, as a peasant uprising led by Charu Majumdar and Kanu Sanyal inspired by Maoist ideology. Courts have consistently held that criticism of government policy, even strong criticism, is protected speech. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Medog Hydropower Project:</strong> China is constructing a mega-hydropower project at Motuo (Medog) County in Tibet's Yarlung Tsangpo Great Bend, where the Brahmaputra falls approximately 2,000 metres over a short distance, creating the potential for 60,000 MW of installed capacity — nearly double India's total installed hydropower and greater than Three Gorges Dam (22,500 MW). This combines earthquake risk with the GLOF risk from upstream glacial lakes — a "compound disaster" scenario.</li>

China suspended data sharing twice (post-Doklam 2017, post-Galwan 2020) before restoring it — illustrating how hydrological data has been used as a geopolitical lever. A permanent legally binding agreement (treaty) is absent.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The opinion article advocates for a national policy on reuse of treated wastewater, citing Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand as models where "Apna Jal" branded treated water is being supplied for non-potable uses (industrial cooling, irrigation, toilet flushing, construction) and, in some projects, after additional treatment for potable reuse. The article references the National Framework for Safe Reuse of Treated Wastewater (2022) — India's first dedicated policy framework for this sector — and argues that AMRUT (Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation) and Smart Cities Mission funding should be repurposed more aggressively towards Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) and treated water distribution networks. India currently treats only ~37% of its sewage; the rest flows untreated into rivers (a key contributor to the Ganga and Yamuna's pollution). Treated water reuse — particularly in water-stressed regions — can reduce freshwater extraction from rivers and groundwater, easing the double burden of pollution and scarcity.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Framework for Safe Reuse of Treated Wastewater (2022):</strong> Notified by the Ministry of Jal Shakti, this is India's first dedicated policy framework for treated wastewater reuse. It sets quality standards for different reuse categories (agriculture, industrial, groundwater recharge, environmental flows, and potable reuse after advanced treatment). States are encouraged to develop State Reuse Frameworks under this national umbrella. It aligns with the National Water Policy 2012's call for treating water as an economic good.</li>
<li><strong>Sewage Treatment Gap in India:</strong> India generates approximately 72,368 million litres per day (MLD) of sewage from Class I cities and Class II towns, but has installed treatment capacity of only ~26,869 MLD — a gap of ~45,000 MLD. Of installed capacity, actual utilisation is lower due to operational failures, power cuts, and improper O&M. This results in nearly 60-70% of India's urban sewage flowing untreated into rivers, lakes, and groundwater — the primary source of India's water body pollution crisis.</li>
<li><strong>AMRUT (Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation):</strong> AMRUT (2015–2022) and AMRUT 2.0 (2021–2026) focus on water supply, sewerage and septage, green spaces, and urban transport in cities above 1 lakh population. AMRUT 2.0 explicitly includes sewerage and septage management as eligible for central funding, with 100% tap water coverage and 100% sewage management as targets. However, the pace of STP construction and O&M quality under AMRUT has been criticised by CAG reports as inadequate relative to stated targets.</li>
<li><strong>Israel's Water Reuse Model:</strong> Israel is the world leader in treated wastewater reuse — it reuses approximately 87% of its treated wastewater, primarily for agricultural irrigation, making it nearly independent of freshwater for farming. The Dan Region Reclamation Project (Shafdan) is a model of large-scale indirect potable reuse through aquifer recharge. India's National Framework explicitly drew from Israeli and Singaporean models in designing its reuse standards and governance framework.</li>
<li><strong>Circular Water Economy:</strong> The concept of a circular water economy treats water as a resource to be reused rather than a waste product to be discharged. It integrates wastewater treatment, nutrient recovery (nitrogen and phosphorus from sewage sludge for fertiliser), energy recovery (biogas from anaerobic digestion), and water reuse into an integrated system. India's National Water Mission (under NAPCC) and Jal Jeevan Mission are beginning to incorporate circular economy principles, but implementation remains fragmented across different ministries and urban local bodies.</li>
</ul>

</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 10 · Text &amp; Context</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → OBC reservation; creamy layer; Supreme Court jurisprudence; DoPT guidelines
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Reservation policy; Indra Sawhney; 50% ceiling; backward class identification

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A Supreme Court bench is examining the "creamy layer" income test for OBC reservation following the March 11, 2026 judgment in Union of India vs. Rohith Nathan. The case raises a critical question: whether salary income of government employees should be included when calculating a family's income for creamy layer determination. A 1993 DoPT Office Memorandum (OM) and a subsequent 2004 clarification letter had excluded salary income from this test, reasoning that the creamy layer concept targets inherited economic advantage, not income from employment. However, the Centre is now arguing that the 2004 letter creates a "cascading effect" distorting appointments made during 2012–2026. The SC is constituting a new bench to resolve the issue, since the income threshold for the creamy layer (currently ₹8 lakh per annum for OBC central government reservation — though several states use different figures) and the definition of what counts as income determine whether the most economically advanced OBC families continue to benefit from reservation that was designed for the socially and educationally backward.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Creamy Layer — Origin in Indra Sawhney (1992):</strong> The Supreme Court's 9-judge bench in Indra Sawhney vs. Union of India (1992 — the "Mandal Case") upheld 27% OBC reservation but introduced the "creamy layer" principle: OBC individuals who have crossed a certain social and economic threshold should be excluded from reservations, since the benefit of reservation is meant for the genuinely backward, not those who have already achieved social advancement. The Court directed the government to identify and exclude the "forward sections" of OBC communities.</li>
<li><strong>DoPT OM of 1993 and Creamy Layer Criteria:</strong> The 1993 DoPT OM operationalised the creamy layer by listing categories of OBC individuals excluded from central government reservation: constitutional post-holders; Class I/II officers (and their children); those with gross annual income above the prescribed limit. The income threshold was set at ₹1 lakh in 1993, periodically revised upward — ₹2.5 lakh (2004), ₹4.5 lakh (2008), ₹6 lakh (2013), ₹8 lakh (2017). The question of whether salary income counts is procedurally set by the OM and its interpretation.</li>
<li><strong>PSU Equivalence Issue:</strong> A related question is whether employees of Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) — whose pay scales may equal or exceed Central government Group A officers — should be treated as equivalent for creamy layer purposes. If a PSU employee's income is excluded from the creamy layer test, their children may claim OBC reservation even when economically well-off. The SC's current case touches on whether PSU employment should be treated on par with government officer status for creamy layer exclusion.</li>
<li><strong>State Variations in Creamy Layer:</strong> While the central government uses ₹8 lakh as the income cut-off, states maintain their own creamy layer criteria for state government jobs and state quota reservations. Tamil Nadu, for instance, has no creamy layer for its state OBC list. This variation means the same OBC family may be excluded from central reservations (above ₹8 lakh threshold) but eligible for state reservations — a differential that becomes judicially complex when challenged.</li>
<li><strong>Sub-Categorisation Ruling (2024) and Creamy Layer:</strong> The 7-judge Constitution Bench ruling permitting sub-categorisation of SC/OBC reservations (August 2024, Punjab vs. Davinder Singh) opens the possibility of identifying the "most backward" within OBCs for priority benefits. This ruling implicitly depends on accurate income and social mobility data — which the creamy layer income test and the pending caste Census are designed to provide. The two issues are thus interlinked: creamy layer definition shapes who benefits from reservation; sub-categorisation determines which sub-groups get priority.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Meta has agreed to an $18 billion settlement with 29 US states over harms caused by Instagram and Facebook to teenagers — particularly mental health impacts linked to addictive algorithmic design. As part of the settlement, Meta has committed to: a 2-hour default daily usage limit for teens, a "Night Mode" restricting access between 12 AM and 6 AM, a "School Mode" blocking the platform from 8 AM to 3 PM, a non-algorithmic feed option (chronological order without recommendation engine), and removal of "like" counts visible to teens. A University of Newcastle study cited in the article found that Australia's social media ban for under-16s (enacted 2024) has been relatively ineffective, with teens using VPNs to circumvent the ban. This raises questions about the comparative efficacy of hard bans versus design-level interventions — a debate directly relevant to India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA) and proposed rules for children's data.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Algorithmic Addiction by Design:</strong> Social media platforms use recommendation algorithms — trained on engagement metrics (likes, shares, watch time) — to maximise time on platform. For teens, these algorithms preferentially surface emotionally stimulating content (outrage, social comparison, body image) because it drives higher engagement. Frances Haugen's 2021 Facebook whistleblower documents revealed that Meta's internal research showed Instagram worsened body image for 32% of teen girls — yet the company prioritised growth over harm mitigation. The concept of "persuasive technology" (BJ Fogg's Captology framework) underpins the addiction-by-design critique.</li>
<li><strong>Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA):</strong> India's DPDPA was enacted in August 2023, replacing the earlier proposed Personal Data Protection Bill. It restricts processing of personal data of children (defined as under 18) without verifiable parental consent; prohibits behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising to children; and requires data fiduciaries (including social media platforms) to comply with age-gating requirements. The DPDPA Rules are being finalised and will determine how platforms verify user age — a technically complex challenge.</li>
<li><strong>Australia's Social Media Ban (2024):</strong> Australia enacted laws in late 2024 prohibiting social media use for children under 16 — the world's most stringent age restriction. Platforms face fines up to AUD 50 million for systemic violations. The University of Newcastle study finding that teens circumvent the ban via VPNs reflects a persistent challenge: demand-side restrictions fail when the content is accessible through alternative technical means. This shifts the regulatory debate towards supply-side design obligations (algorithmic defaults, time limits, feed design) rather than age bans.</li>
<li><strong>Platform Liability and Section 230 (US) / IT Act Section 79 (India):</strong> In the US, Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act shields platforms from liability for user-generated content. The Meta settlement signals growing willingness of state attorneys general to use consumer protection laws to pursue platforms for design-level harms. In India, Section 79 of the IT Act provides safe harbour to intermediaries for third-party content, but the IT Rules 2021 impose "due diligence" obligations that, if violated, remove this immunity. The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules 2021 require platforms to address harmful content, including content harmful to children.</li>
<li><strong>Competitive Fairness and the YouTube/TikTok Clause:</strong> The contingency clause linking 30% of Meta's settlement to YouTube and TikTok compliance reflects a "level playing field" logic: unilateral safety measures by one platform may drive teens to less regulated alternatives. This market dynamic — where safety regulations raise costs for compliant players — is a standard economic argument against single-platform regulation and supports industry-wide binding standards. India's forthcoming DPDPA Rules and proposed Social Media Regulation framework will face the same challenge.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Iceland and the EU:</strong> Iceland is not an EU member but participates in the EEA (European Economic Area), which includes the EU's four freedoms (goods, services, capital, persons) without voting rights in EU institutions. EEA membership provides most of the economic benefits of EU membership — making the case for full membership less economically compelling for a prosperous small state like Iceland.</li>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<li><strong>Venezuela's Oil Reserves:</strong> Venezuela holds the world's largest proved crude oil reserves — approximately 303 billion barrels (OPEC, 2023), surpassing Saudi Arabia's ~267 billion barrels. She is a Chavista loyalist but is seen as more pragmatic on economic policy. The deal's 100-year term and US operational control provision suggests Venezuela received it under conditions of extreme negotiating weakness.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The explosive growth of AI and cloud data centres in the United States has triggered a significant political backlash that is influencing 21 congressional races across 18 states in the 2026 US midterm elections. In Ohio alone, the data centre boom has added $9.3 billion in grid capacity costs — a 174% increase — that is being passed on to electricity consumers. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Data Centre Energy Consumption:</strong> A large hyperscale data centre (e.g., those operated by Microsoft, Amazon AWS, Google, Meta) can consume 500–1,000 MW of electricity — comparable to a small nuclear power plant. Global data centres consume approximately 1-2% of world electricity; AI workloads are projected to increase this to 3-4% by 2030. The International Energy Agency (IEA) Data Centres and AI report (2024) flagged data centres as one of the fastest-growing sources of electricity demand globally, straining grids designed for stable industrial loads.</li>
<li><strong>AI's Water Footprint:</strong> AI training and inference require intensive cooling — large language models like GPT-4 consumed an estimated 700,000 litres of water during training. Data centres use either air cooling (high energy) or liquid cooling (water-intensive). Microsoft's data centres consumed 6.4 billion litres of water in 2022. In water-stressed regions (US Southwest, India's Deccan plateau), data centre water demands compete with agriculture and municipal supply. This is a growing ESG and regulatory concern for AI companies.</li>
<li><strong>India's Data Centre Industry:</strong> India's data centre market is among the world's fastest-growing — expected to reach 1,600 MW by 2026. Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad, and Pune are the primary hubs. The government's Data Centre Policy (2020) and National Digital Infrastructure Policy enable development of data centre parks. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA) 2023 allows cross-border data flows to "trusted countries" (to be notified), which will shape whether India becomes a preferred data centre location for regional operations. Power availability and grid reliability are key constraints.</li>
<li><strong>Grid Stress and Renewable Energy:</strong> Data centres require 24/7 "always-on" power, which conflicts with the intermittent nature of renewable energy (solar, wind). This drives demand for dispatchable power (natural gas, nuclear) that undermines decarbonisation targets. Big Tech companies have committed to 100% renewable energy matching (not 24/7 carbon-free energy), a weaker standard. Several US states are now requiring data centre developers to demonstrate renewable energy sourcing as a condition of grid connection — a model India's Ministry of Power is studying.</li>
<li><strong>National Data Governance Framework Policy (India, 2022):</strong> India's NDGF (draft) proposes establishing a non-personal data governance structure, enabling government agencies to access anonymised private sector datasets for public good purposes, and creating a data market. Combined with India's National Data Sharing and Accessibility Policy (NDSAP, 2012), which promotes open government data, and the DPDPA 2023 for personal data, these form India's emerging data governance architecture. India's IndiaAI Mission (₹10,000 crore, 2024) is building sovereign AI infrastructure including a compute facility with 10,000+ GPUs — which will face similar energy and water challenges as US hyperscale deployments.</li>
</ul>

In Saxony-Anhalt, one of Germany's eastern states and an AfD (Alternative für Deutschland) stronghold, anti-migrant sentiment is intensifying ahead of the September 6 state election. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Alternative für Deutschland (AfD):</strong> Founded in 2013 as an Eurosceptic party, AfD has shifted rightward into nativist, anti-immigration, and Islamophobic territory. It is classified as a "proven extremist" organisation by Germany's domestic intelligence service (BfV) in parts of the country. AfD consistently polls 25-35% in eastern German states (Saxony, Thuringia, Saxony-Anhalt, Brandenburg) — far above its western German support. Germany's constitution forbids banning parties unless they actively undermine the constitutional order — efforts to ban AfD have been debated but not pursued due to evidentiary standards.</li>
<li><strong>Germany's Immigration Debate:</strong> Germany accepted over 1 million Syrian refugees in 2015-16 (under Merkel's "Wir schaffen das" — "We can manage this"). The integration burden created political backlash: housing costs, school pressure, social service demands, and isolated incidents of crime attributed to migrants fuelled AfD's rise. Germany's new coalition government (since 2025) under Chancellor Friedrich Merz (CDU) has adopted stricter border controls and deportation enforcement — partially adopting AfD's agenda to contain its electoral spread.</li>
<li><strong>Skilled Migration and Europe's Labour Needs:</strong> Germany faces a severe demographic and labour shortage — requiring an estimated 400,000 skilled immigrants annually. Syrian, Afghan, and other migrants include doctors, engineers, IT professionals, and tradespeople. The German Skilled Immigration Act (2020, expanded 2023) created new visa pathways for non-EU skilled workers, including a "Chancenkarte" (Opportunity Card) points system modelled on Canadian immigration. The AfD's anti-migration stance directly contradicts Germany's long-term labour market needs — a tension that mainstream parties have failed to communicate effectively.</li>
<li><strong>East-West Division in Germany:</strong> Germany's East-West divide (Ost-West-Gefälle) persists 35 years after reunification (1990). Eastern states have lower wages, higher unemployment, fewer headquarters of major companies, and younger demographic exodus to western cities. GDP per capita in eastern states is ~70-75% of the western average. This economic lag creates fertile ground for populist parties — AfD and BSW (Sahra Wagenknecht's Alliance, a left-populist party) — that reject both mainstream conservative and social-democratic orthodoxy. The GDR's experience of collective identity under authoritarianism also shapes political culture differently from the West.</li>
<li><strong>India's Interests in European Migration Politics:</strong> India is a significant source of skilled migration to Germany — IT professionals (under the EU Blue Card and ICT visas) and healthcare workers (nurses under bilateral MoUs). Germany recognises India's BPO credentials for some professions. The far-right's hostility to immigration affects Indian expatriates' social safety. The Magdeburg Christmas market attack (2024, by a Saudi-born doctor with German citizenship) triggered a crackdown that affected all migrant communities including Indians, illustrating the collateral effects of anti-migration politics on legal, skilled immigrants.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Chennai's search for a second airport has hit a fresh impasse after the TVK government cancelled the previously selected Parandur site — where 1,802 acres had already been acquired — and repurposed the land for industrial development. The Parandur cancellation was driven by large-scale farmer protests and environmental concerns. New candidate sites being evaluated include Cheyyur and Gummidipoondi, but each faces constraints: IAF's Tambaram airspace controls create approach restrictions, Pulicat Lake's migratory bird corridors raise wildlife clearance hurdles, and local opposition is anticipated at any greenfield site. Meanwhile, Chennai's Kempegowda-style T5 Terminal expansion at Meenambakkam is proceeding as an interim measure. Chennai's air passenger traffic rank has fallen from 3rd to 5th nationally — behind Delhi, Mumbai, Hyderabad, and Bengaluru — reflecting infrastructure constraints that suppress demand. The broader issue of airport capacity planning, land acquisition under the LARR Act, and environmental clearance bottlenecks is directly relevant to UPSC infrastructure governance.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India's Aviation Sector Growth:</strong> India became the world's 3rd largest domestic aviation market (after US and China) in 2023, with annual passenger traffic exceeding 150 million. The government targets 220 airports operational by 2025 under UDAN (Ude Desh ka Aam Naagrik) regional connectivity scheme. Major airports (Chennai, Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Kolkata) are operated under AAI (Airports Authority of India) or PPP models. Capacity constraints at metro airports are a bottleneck for India's aviation ambitions.</li>
<li><strong>Land Acquisition and LARR Act, 2013:</strong> The Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (LARR) replaced the colonial Land Acquisition Act, 1894. It mandates: Social Impact Assessment for acquisitions above 50 acres; consent of 70% (private projects) or 80% (PPP) landowners; and comprehensive rehabilitation and resettlement package. These requirements have significantly raised the cost and complexity of large-scale land acquisition for airports, highways, and industrial corridors — a factor in Parandur's cancellation.</li>
<li><strong>Environmental Clearance for Airports:</strong> Greenfield airports require an Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) under the Environment Protection Act, 1986, and clearance from the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC). Projects near wetlands, bird sanctuaries, or biodiversity-rich areas (like Pulicat Lake — one of India's largest brackish water lagoons, home to flamingos and migratory birds) face additional scrutiny under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, and the Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) notifications. These clearances can add 3–5 years to project timelines.</li>
<li><strong>IAF Airspace and Civil Aviation Coordination:</strong> India's civil aviation airspace is shared with the Indian Air Force. Military airbases create "no-fly zones" and approach path restrictions around them — any new civilian airport must maintain safe separation from military flight paths. IAF's Tambaram base near Chennai constrains airport placement options in the southern quadrant of the city. The Civil Aviation Requirements (CAR) published by DGCA specify minimum separation distances between airports and obstacles including military bases.</li>
<li><strong>UDAN Scheme and Regional Connectivity:</strong> UDAN (Ude Desh ka Aam Naagrik), launched in 2016, subsidises operations on unserved and underserved routes, with the government supporting viability gap funding. It has connected 70+ cities and enabled first-time flyers from smaller towns. However, metro airport congestion limits the hub-and-spoke expansion of UDAN since smaller regional airports depend on connectivity to major hubs like Chennai. A second Chennai airport would relieve hub congestion and enable more regional connections.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Two devastating hotel fires in West Bengal within weeks have exposed catastrophic regulatory failure in fire safety enforcement. The Florence Mansion and Hotel Shikha Inn fires in Kolkata (August 19, 9 dead — including 7 Bangladeshi nationals) were caused by short-circuit in air conditioning, with plywood partitions blocking exits; neither hotel had a fire NOC. The Bideshini Hotel fire in Tarapith (August 17, 9 dead) occurred during a power cut when the back door was inaccessible. In both cases, the hotels operated without valid fire safety certificates; middlemen (touts) facilitated regulatory avoidance. A CAG audit cited in the article documented 591 fire incidents in West Bengal in 2019 alone, causing 528 deaths — with 68% shortfall in fire station coverage and 74% of fire operator posts vacant. The government closed 52 hotels post-Kolkata and 15 post-Tarapith, but critics including BJP leader Adhikari argue this is crisis management rather than systemic reform.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Fire NOC and Building Safety Regulation:</strong> A Fire No Objection Certificate (NOC) is mandatory under the National Building Code (NBC) of India (published by BIS, most recently updated 2016) for commercial establishments, hospitals, hotels, and multi-storey buildings. It certifies that fire exits, sprinklers, fire extinguishers, smoke detectors, and evacuation plans meet minimum standards. The NBC is a model code — states must adopt it through legislation. In West Bengal, the West Bengal Fire Services Act, 1950 governs fire safety; the State Fire Services is responsible for inspections and NOC issuance.</li>
<li><strong>Plywood Partition Hazard:</strong> A specific fire hazard in Indian budget hotels and markets is the use of plywood/wooden partitions to subdivide spaces and create additional rooms — maximising revenue but creating highly flammable building interiors without fire-compartmentalisation. Indian building codes require that load-bearing and non-load-bearing partitions in commercial/hospitality buildings meet fire resistance rating (FRR) standards — but enforcement is weak, particularly in older buildings and informal markets. The Uphaar Cinema fire (1997, Delhi — 59 deaths) was a landmark case that resulted in criminal convictions and exposed the same category of fire safety negligence.</li>
<li><strong>CAG Reports on Fire Safety:</strong> The Comptroller and Auditor General's performance audits on fire services in various states have consistently found: inadequate fire station coverage (NDMA norms vs. actual stations), critical equipment shortages, staff vacancies, and poor maintenance of fire engines. The 2021 CAG report on West Bengal found 591 fire incidents in 2019 causing 528 deaths — figures that indicate fire mortality as a significant preventable public health issue. CAG reports are tabled in Parliament/State Legislatures and are public documents used to hold governments accountable.</li>
<li><strong>NDMA Guidelines on Fire Safety:</strong> The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) has issued guidelines on Urban Flood Management, Industrial Disasters, and Fire Safety — the latter prescribing community-level fire prevention, hotel fire safety standards, and inter-agency coordination frameworks. The NDMA guidelines are advisory (not legally binding) but serve as a reference standard for state governments, ULBs (Urban Local Bodies), and the National Building Code compliance. Hotels serving tourist pilgrimage sites like Tarapith face elevated risk due to dense, informal accommodation building.li>
<li><strong>Bangladeshi Nationals and Cross-Border Tourism:</strong> India resumed Bangladeshi tourist visas in late June 2026 after approximately a two-year suspension (following Hasina's ouster and India-Bangladesh diplomatic cooling). The Kolkata hotel fire killing 7 Bangladeshi nationals illustrates the vulnerability of foreign tourists at poorly regulated budget accommodations — a diplomatic and safety governance issue. Kolkata, as the nearest major Indian city to Bangladesh, receives significant Bangladeshi tourist traffic for medical treatment, shopping, and religious tourism.</li>
</ul>
</div>
