<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Rescue efforts on a war footing in Nepal as disaster toll hits 675
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Disaster Management</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-Nepal bilateral relations; disaster diplomacy; humanitarian assistance
- GS Paper III → Disaster Management → Flash floods; glacial lake outburst floods; GLOF early-warning systems; climate change and mountain ecosystem fragility

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The death toll from Nepal's catastrophic August 26 flash flood crossed 675 by August 30, with at least 2,498 people still missing and over 7,514 rescued. The disaster originated from a high-altitude glacial collapse along the Nepal-Tibet border, sending a massive rock-ice avalanche — scientifically termed a "sturzstrom" — down the Bhotekoshi and Trishuli river corridors, devastating Rasuwa, Nuwakot, and Dhading districts. Over 900 workers at 11 hydropower projects remain unaccounted for, with rescuers struggling to reach those trapped in tunnels. India deployed a specialised tunnel rescue team and six forensic expert teams to help identify victims, while China sent five tunnel experts. Nepal PM Balendra Shah said 18,708 security personnel were mobilised. The scale of the disaster — with some scientists comparing it to the 2021 Chamoli sturzstrom in Uttarakhand — has reignited debate about the efficacy of early-warning systems in high-mountain environments and the broader role of climate change in accelerating Himalayan glacier melt, even as experts stop short of directly attributing this specific event to global warming.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>NDRRMA (National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority):</strong> Nepal's apex disaster management authority, constituted under the Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act, 2017. It coordinates rescue, relief and rehabilitation across all government tiers and is the primary agency releasing official casualty figures and missing-persons data.</li>
<li><strong>Sturzstrom:</strong> A German term for a massive, fast-moving long-runout rock avalanche in which a large volume of rock — often mixed with ice, snow and permafrost — collapses and travels at extraordinary speed over great distances. The 2021 Chamoli disaster in Uttarakhand, which killed over 200 people and destroyed two hydropower projects, is the closest Indian analogue; researchers found more than 25 million cubic metres of rock and ice broke loose from a high-altitude slope.</li>
<li><strong>India's Neighbourhood First Disaster Diplomacy:</strong> India's "Neighbourhood First" policy explicitly prioritises first-responder roles in South Asian disasters. Past examples include NDRF deployment after the 2015 Nepal earthquake and Operation Maitri. Tunnel rescue expertise is a specialised capability India has developed after Uttarkashi (2023) and similar incidents.</li>
<li><strong>Sendai Framework for DRR (2015–2030):</strong> Adopted at the Third UN World Conference on DRR in Sendai, Japan. India is a signatory and has a National DRR Plan aligned to the Framework's four priorities: understanding risk, strengthening governance, investing in resilience, and enhancing preparedness. Mountain community vulnerability and GLOF early-warning are specific gaps flagged in the Himalayan context.</li>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF):</strong> Sudden release of water from a glacial lake, often when an ice or moraine dam fails. Increasingly frequent due to Himalayan warming, which is occurring at 2–3 times the global average rate. The Bhotekoshi has flooded before (July 2025 GLOF), but this 2026 event's velocity and ferocity pointed to a sturzstrom origin rather than a simple GLOF — illustrating how climate-linked instability can produce multiple overlapping hazard types.</li>
</ul>
</div>

  T.N. to set up safe houses for vulnerable inter-caste couples
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → Honour killings; caste-based violence; protection of inter-caste couples; Supreme Court directives on social discrimination
- GS Paper II → Governance → State welfare mechanisms; protection homes; implementation of court orders

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Tamil Nadu government announced plans to establish a "safe house" in each district to shelter inter-caste and inter-religious couples facing threats of honour killings and caste-related violence. Social Justice Minister Vanni Arasu made the announcement in the State Assembly, citing the Supreme Court's 2018 ruling in Shakti Vahini vs. Union of India, which directed states to create protection mechanisms for couples choosing to marry against family wishes. Survivor-activist Kausalya Shankar — whose husband was killed in the 2016 Udumalpet honour killing — welcomed the decision as historic. Anti-caste activist Evidence Kathir cautioned that safe houses must not be used to pressure couples to end their marriages and must be placed under direct court monitoring. He also noted that Tamil Nadu had set up special complaint cells and a helpline as early as 2018, but these had largely stopped functioning. The initiative represents a state-level attempt to operationalise a directive that has remained unenforced across most Indian states for nearly eight years.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Shakti Vahini vs. Union of India (2018):</strong> A landmark Supreme Court judgment directing state governments to set up Special Cells to receive complaints from couples facing threats for marrying against family wishes, to appoint nodal officers in every district, and to constitute a Protection Home in each state. The court held that honour killings violate the fundamental rights to life (Article 21) and to choose a partner (Article 19).</li>
<li><strong>Honour Killing in India — Legal Framework:</strong> India has no standalone law against honour killings. Offenders are prosecuted under BNS provisions for murder, criminal conspiracy and abetment. The Law Commission (242nd Report, 2012) recommended specific legislation titled "Prevention of Crimes in the Name of Honour and Tradition Bill." Several states have tabled but not enacted such legislation.</li>
<li><strong>Khap Panchayats:</strong> Extra-constitutional caste-based community bodies, prevalent mainly in Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan, but not confined to the north — Tamil Nadu has its own variants. The Supreme Court in Shakti Vahini held that khap diktats mandating separation or death of couples have no legal sanction and amount to unlawful assembly at minimum.</li>
<li><strong>Article 17 of the Constitution:</strong> Abolishes "Untouchability" in any form. The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 operationalises it. Caste-based opposition to inter-caste marriages is legally connected to the same discriminatory social hierarchy that Article 17 seeks to dismantle, making honour killings a constitutional issue and not just a criminal one.</li>
<li><strong>Kausalya Shankar Case (2016):</strong> A high-profile Tamil Nadu honour killing in which Shankar, a Dalit youth from Palladam, was hacked to death in broad daylight in Udumalpet shortly after his marriage to Kausalya. Kausalya, who survived the attack, became a national symbol of anti-caste activism and has used her story to advocate for legal reform. The case led to a life sentence for the accused including her father, later enhanced.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  India and China yet to announce 2 more border meeting points along LAC
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">India-China Relations</span>
  <span style="background:#E8EEF6;color:#2858A0;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Security</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 3 · News</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India-China boundary dispute; LAC management; confidence-building measures; Special Representatives mechanism

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
India and China agreed at the 25th Special Representatives (SR) meeting on August 26 to establish two additional Border Personnel Meeting (BPM) points and General/Senior Military Commander-level meeting sites along the Line of Actual Control. The eight-point outcome document was released by both countries. India's NSA Ajit Doval represented India, and Wang Yi — Politburo member and Director of the Office of the Central Commission for Foreign Affairs — represented China. As of August 30, the specific locations for the new BPM points have not been officially announced, though government sources confirmed at least two rounds of local commander meetings have already taken place at Kepang La in Upper Siang district, Arunachal Pradesh. Currently five BPM points exist: Daulat Beg Oldie and Chushul in Ladakh, Nathu La in Sikkim, and Kibithu and Bum La in Arunachal Pradesh. The article is significant because it shows the post-Galwan normalisation process continues to deepen, with institutionalised military communication being expanded into sensitive eastern-sector areas.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Border Personnel Meeting (BPM) Mechanism:</strong> Established under the Agreement on Maintenance of Peace and Tranquility along the LAC (September 1993) and formalised through the Protocol on Procedures to be followed while dealing with border personnel meetings (2012). BPMs are flag meetings between local commanders at designated border points that serve as the first tier of conflict de-escalation, below the Corps Commander-level talks.</li>
<li><strong>Special Representatives Mechanism (2003):</strong> Created under the India-China Joint Declaration to negotiate the boundary question at a political level. It operates alongside military-level talks and is guided by the 2005 Agreement on Political Parameters and Guiding Principles for the Settlement of the India-China Boundary Question — which establishes the principle of "package settlement" covering all three sectors (western, middle, eastern).</li>
<li><strong>Wang Yi's Dual Role:</strong> Wang Yi simultaneously serves as China's Foreign Minister and Director of the Office of the Central Commission for Foreign Affairs (CCFA), which directly coordinates with the Politburo. This dual hat gives him authority over both diplomatic and strategic foreign-policy decisions — his presence as SR signals the highest-level Chinese political commitment to the boundary talks.</li>
<li><strong>Galwan Valley Clash (June 2020):</strong> A violent hand-to-hand confrontation between Indian and Chinese soldiers in the Galwan Valley, Ladakh, killing 20 Indian soldiers (and an unspecified number of Chinese soldiers). It led to the worst India-China military standoff since 1962 and froze diplomatic ties. The October 2024 patrolling agreement at Depsang and Demchok marked the formal end of that standoff, and subsequent rounds of SR talks have built on that.</li>
<li><strong>Kepang La (Upper Siang, Arunachal Pradesh):</strong> A high-altitude pass in the eastern sector close to the McMahon Line. Local commander meetings there — already underway before formal announcement — indicate that the confidence-building process has moved faster at the field level than at the political announcement level, a pattern consistent with how the BPM mechanism has evolved in the past.</li>
</ul>
</div>

</h2>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → International Relations → SCO; India-Central Asia relations; BRICS; UN Security Council elections; Chabahar port; INSTC; India-Iran relations
- GS Paper II → International Relations → India's neighbourhood policy; connectivity; foreign policy in a multipolar world

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
PM Narendra Modi arrived in Tashkent, Uzbekistan for a two-day visit before heading to Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan, to attend the 26th SCO Summit. India-Central Asia trade has grown from $500 million (2010) to over $2 billion (2025), but connectivity has been severely disrupted — the rupture with Pakistan since 2016 ended India's overland route; US sanctions effectively closed the Chabahar port option; and the US-Iran war has imperilled the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC). China's Belt and Road Initiative, meanwhile, has grown to $106 billion in China-Central Asia trade in 2025. The SCO Summit will also be Modi's first opportunity to meet Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian since the US-Iran war began — to discuss the Hormuz Strait blockade. India also wants Tajikistan to defer its UN Security Council non-permanent seat candidature for 2028-29 (both India and Tajikistan are competing for the Asian bloc's vote for the June 2027 UNSC election). The long-pending India-Central Asia Summit (not held since 2022) is also expected to get a date during this visit.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO):</strong> A Eurasian political, economic and security organisation founded in 2001. Current members include China, Russia, India (since 2017), Pakistan (since 2017), four Central Asian states (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan), Iran (2023) and Belarus (2024). The SCO focuses on counter-terrorism, security and regional connectivity — India has consistently pushed to keep the organisation free of bilateralism with Pakistan within SCO forums.</li>
<li><strong>International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC):</strong> A 7,200 km multi-modal route connecting India to Russia via Iran (Bandar Abbas/Chabahar) and the Caspian Sea, bypassing Pakistan and Afghanistan. India's investment in Chabahar port in Iran is the entry point into the INSTC. US secondary sanctions on Iran after the US-Iran war threaten to shut down this critical connectivity project.</li>
<li><strong>Chabahar Port:</strong> An Iranian deep-water port on the Gulf of Oman developed with Indian investment (India Ports Global Limited). India signed a 10-year contract in May 2024 to operate the Shahid Beheshti terminal. It provides India's only viable land-access route to Afghanistan and Central Asia bypassing Pakistan, making it strategically indispensable for India's Connect Central Asia policy.</li>
<li><strong>India-Central Asia Summit:</strong> A heads-of-government level forum India initiated in 2022 (first summit held virtually). It covers the 5 Central Asian states — Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan. The 2022 summit was the first and only edition so far; India's perceived disengagement has raised regional concern, which this visit aims to reverse.</li>
<li><strong>UNSC Non-Permanent Seat Election (2027):</strong> The UN General Assembly elects non-permanent members to the Security Council in contested elections by regional group. India and Tajikistan are both contenders for the Asian Group's non-permanent seat for 2028-29, to be elected in June 2027. India's strategy is to persuade Tajikistan to defer its candidacy, as a clean-slate arrangement — but the 2025 UNGA elections showed established powers sometimes lose (Germany lost to Portugal/Austria; Philippines lost to Kyrgyz Republic).</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Revanth seeks 4 more weeks for revision of electoral rolls — SIR controversy in Telangana
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Elections</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 2 · States</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Indian Polity → Election Commission of India; electoral rolls; voter enfranchisement; federalism and elections
- GS Paper II → Governance → Electoral integrity; Special Intensive Revision (SIR); role of Booth Level Officers

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Telangana Chief Minister A. Revanth Reddy wrote to Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar urging a four-week extension to the claims-and-objections period for the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, currently scheduled from August 17 to September 16 with final publication on October 19. The CM expressed alarm that nearly one-third of Telangana's 3.38 crore registered electors — about 92.86 lakh people — now require notices to prove their eligibility: 73.39 lakh entries are classified as Absent, Shifted, Dead or Duplicate (ASDD), and of the 2.65 crore enumeration forms digitised, 60.5 lakh are flagged for anomalies while another 32.36 lakh lack a link to the previous roll. The CM's concern is that legitimate voters could be deleted en masse. He also proposed safeguards including public reading of draft rolls. Separately, a BJP activist in Navi Mumbai was caught photocopying 3,400 SIR forms — containing voters' photographs and personal details — at a print shop, leading to the suspension of five Booth Level Officers who had handed over the forms.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Special Intensive Revision (SIR):</strong> A process initiated by the Election Commission of India (ECI) for a comprehensive review of voter rolls, involving door-to-door enumeration by Booth Level Officers (BLOs). It is more intensive than the Annual Summary Revision and is typically triggered when the ECI deems existing rolls require deep cleaning — but it is also criticized for creating scope for large-scale deletions.</li>
<li><strong>ASDD Classification (Absent, Shifted, Dead, Duplicate):</strong> A four-category framework used in electoral-roll revision to flag entries that may no longer be valid. Entries classified as ASDD are required to prove continued eligibility during the claims period. Critics argue the classification is over-broad and that "permanently shifted" is being used to delete voters who are still residents.</li>
<li><strong>Election Commission of India (ECI):</strong> A constitutional body under Article 324, which vests the superintendence, direction and control of elections in the Commission. The Chief Election Commissioner is appointed by the President and can be removed only by impeachment — like a Supreme Court judge. The Chief Electoral Officers in states implement ECI directives. Since the 2023 amendment to the Chief Election Commissioners and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, appointments are made by a committee of the PM, Leader of Opposition, and a Cabinet Minister.</li>
<li><strong>Booth Level Officers (BLOs):</strong> Government employees designated to maintain voter rolls at the polling-booth level. They conduct door-to-door verification during revisions, collect enumeration forms and process additions, deletions and corrections. BLOs are accountable to Electoral Registration Officers and are the foundational unit of electoral-roll administration.</li>
<li><strong>Representation of the People Act, 1950:</strong> Governs the preparation and revision of electoral rolls. Section 21 gives the ECI power to conduct special revisions. Courts have held that no voter can be deleted without following due process, including serving notice and providing an opportunity to respond — making the timeline for claims and objections a critical procedural protection.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Jarange-Patil begins indefinite strike for Maratha quota; warns of march to Mumbai
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Social Justice</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">OBC Reservation</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 4 · National News</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Social Justice → OBC reservation; Maratha quota agitation; sub-categorisation; Kunbi caste certificates
- GS Paper II → Polity → Constitutional provisions on reservations; 50% ceiling; state-level OBC lists

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Maratha quota activist Manoj Jarange-Patil resumed his indefinite hunger strike at Antarwali Sarati in Jalna district on August 30, demanding that the Maharashtra government immediately issue caste certificates to Marathas based on 58 lakh Kunbi records identified by the Shinde committee. His key demands include issuing Kunbi certificates using the Hyderabad Gazetteer and other historical records, abolishing caste scrutiny committees (which he argues exist only in Maharashtra), and ensuring no records are discarded. He warned that if demands are not met in Jalna, the agitation would move to Mumbai's Azad Maidan or Varsha Bungalow (the CM's residence). Jalna police served him a notice under Section 168 of the BNSS to maintain law and order. The Maratha agitation is part of a broader national debate on OBC sub-categorisation and the efficacy of the 50-per-cent ceiling on reservations, following the Supreme Court's August 2024 ruling allowing sub-categorisation within SC/ST communities.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Maratha Reservation — Legal Journey:</strong> Maharashtra legislated Maratha reservation (SEBC — Socially and Educationally Backward Classes) in 2018 (16% quota) and again in 2021 after the Supreme Court struck down the 2018 Act. In May 2021, a 5-judge Constitution Bench in Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil vs. Chief Minister, Maharashtra struck down the 2021 SEBC Act as violating the Indra Sawhney (Mandal) judgment's 50% ceiling and held that Marathas are not backward.</li>
<li><strong>Kunbi Records Strategy:</strong> Kunbi is an OBC farming community already listed in Maharashtra's OBC list. Activists argue that many Marathas, especially in Marathwada (the former Hyderabad State area), have Kunbi antecedents documented in colonial-era gazetteers and settlement records — and should therefore be entitled to OBC certificates. The Shinde committee identified ~58 lakh such records, making them the basis of Jarange-Patil's demand.</li>
<li><strong>Indra Sawhney vs. Union of India (1992):</strong> The landmark 9-judge Supreme Court bench judgment that upheld the Mandal Commission recommendations and the 27% OBC reservation, but capped total reservations at 50% (except in "extraordinary circumstances") and excluded the "creamy layer" from OBC reservation. It remains the foundational precedent for all reservation jurisprudence.</li>
<li><strong>Caste Scrutiny Committees:</strong> Bodies established in Maharashtra (but reportedly absent in other states) to verify the validity of caste certificates before extending reservation benefits. Jarange-Patil argues these committees are a bureaucratic bottleneck unique to Maharashtra that effectively denies certificates even after they are issued. He has demanded that verification be simplified to the tehsildar level.</li>
<li><strong>Sub-Categorisation (2024 Supreme Court Ruling):</strong> A 7-judge Constitution Bench in State of Punjab vs. Davinder Singh (August 2024) overruled the 2004 EV Chinnaiah judgment and held that states can sub-categorise SCs and STs to prioritise the most backward among them. While this ruling was for SC/STs, it has energised OBC sub-categorisation debates as well, including calls for Maratha-specific arrangements within the OBC list.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Naveen Patnaik urges Odisha BJP MPs to seek reversal of MMDR Amendment 2026

Former Odisha CM and BJP chief Naveen Patnaik wrote to Odisha's BJP MPs urging them to demand reversal of the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2026, passed in the Lok Sabha on August 13 with less than ten minutes of discussion. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 (MMDR Act):</strong> The principal legislation governing mining in India. Minerals other than atomic and hydrocarbons (governed by separate acts) fall under this. The Act has been amended multiple times — most significantly in 2015 (introducing auction of mineral blocks), 2021 (end of captive restrictions, composite licences) and now 2026. "Regulation of mines and mineral development" falls under Entry 54 of Union List, giving Parliament exclusive legislative competence.</li>
<li><strong>Centre-State Conflict on Mining Revenue:</strong> State governments earn royalties from mineral extraction under the MMDR Act — the rates are set by the Union government. Odisha's mineral wealth (iron ore, coal, bauxite, chromite) has made royalties a crucial component of its budget. MMDR amendments that change auction terms, royalty distribution or control mechanisms directly affect state revenues.</li>
<li><strong>Odisha's Mineral Contribution:</strong> Odisha holds approximately 44% of India's total iron ore reserves and significant reserves of coal, chromite, bauxite and manganese. Major public sector steel plants (SAIL Rourkela, TATA Steel) depend on Odisha's ore. The state's mining sector also provides significant employment and contributes over 20% of its GSDP.</li>
<li><strong>Schedule VII of the Constitution:</strong> Divides legislative powers between Union and State lists. Mining and mineral development falls under Entry 54 (Union List), but land (Entry 18, State List) and environment (Concurrent List) create a complex web of Centre-State jurisdiction in mining governance. State governments often leverage these overlapping jurisdictions — environment clearances, land acquisition — to assert influence over mining even where the Union has legislative supremacy.</li>
<li><strong>District Mineral Foundation (DMF):</strong> Established under the 2015 MMDR Amendment, DMFs are trusts set up in each mining-affected district to work for the interest and benefit of persons and areas affected by mining. States control DMF funds (at least 30% of royalty for mines leased before 2015). Any amendment affecting royalty distribution or auction proceeds directly alters DMF resources and states' ability to compensate affected communities.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Article 17 of the Constitution:</strong> Abolishes "Untouchability" and makes its practice in any form an offence punishable by law. It is one of the few fundamental rights directly enforceable against private individuals (not just the state). The term "Untouchability" is not defined in the Constitution but has been given meaning through the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955.</li>
<li><strong>Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (PCR Act):</strong> Enacted to give effect to Article 17, it penalises the practice of untouchability in access to public places, religious institutions, water sources, trade and commerce. Offences are cognisable and non-bailable. The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 (and its 2016 amendment) provides stronger protections including special courts and no anticipatory bail for accused.</li>
<li><strong>Shuddhikaran Ritual:</strong> A purification ceremony traditionally practised in some Hindu communities, often used as a tool of social exclusion — symbolically "purifying" a space that a person considered impure (on grounds of caste, religion, etc.) has visited. When applied to a Dalit leader's visit, it constitutes a direct act of untouchability under the PCR Act.</li>
<li><strong>Mallikarjun Kharge:</strong> President of the Indian National Congress and the Leader of the Opposition in the Rajya Sabha since December 2022 — the first Dalit to hold the post in nearly 50 years. He is also the longest-serving Dalit politician to become president of a major national party, and his identity is politically significant in debates around Dalit representation.</li>
<li><strong>Constitutional Morality vs. Social Morality:</strong> A distinction drawn by B.R. Ambedkar (and affirmed by the Supreme Court in Navtej Singh Johar vs. Union of India, 2018) — constitutional morality must prevail over social morality when they conflict. The Haldwani controversy directly tests this tension: social practice may persist even where the constitutional prohibition has existed for 75+ years.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  A quiet move that has kept Dehradun's vultures safe — relocating carcass dump 2.4 km from power lines
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Biodiversity</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 5 · News</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Biodiversity conservation; vulture population decline; electrocution as wildlife threat; human-wildlife interface
- GS Paper III → Environment → Species conservation; NSAID poisoning; community-based conservation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A decade-old study published in the Journal of Threatened Taxa documents a practical conservation intervention in Dehradun, Uttarakhand: moving a livestock carcass dump 2.4 km away from high-tension power infrastructure in April 2015 eliminated vulture electrocution deaths that had previously been killing dozens of birds annually. Between May 2011 and February 2014, 46 vulture carcasses attributable to electrocution were documented in the Sudhauwala area, with Himalayan griffon, Eurasian griffon and cinereous vulture most affected. After relocation of the dump, no mortality events were recorded during post-relocation monitoring. Vultures in India face multiple anthropogenic threats — diclofenac (a veterinary NSAID) poisoning that triggered their catastrophic population collapse in the 1990s, habitat loss, food scarcity — and electrocution from power infrastructure is the most underreported of these. The study suggests carcass-dump relocation is a low-cost, replicable conservation measure for other affected areas.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Vulture Population Collapse in South Asia:</strong> India's three Gyps vulture species — Oriental White-backed (Gyps bengalensis), Long-billed (Gyps indicus) and Slender-billed (Gyps tenuirostris) — suffered a 97-99% population crash in the 1990s-2000s. The cause was identified in 2004: diclofenac, a veterinary NSAID used to treat livestock, is fatally toxic to vultures that feed on carcasses of treated animals, causing visceral gout and kidney failure.</li>
<li><strong>Diclofenac Ban (2006):</strong> India banned the veterinary use of diclofenac in 2006, followed by Pakistan and Nepal. Ketoprofen, aceclofenac and nimesulide, which are also toxic to vultures, remain in use and pose continuing threats. Meloxicam is the approved safe alternative. The IUCN lists several South Asian Gyps species as Critically Endangered.</li>
<li><strong>Vulture Restaurants / Jatayu Conservation Breeding Centres:</strong> India established Vulture Conservation Breeding Centres (VCBCs) at Pinjore (Haryana), Bhopal and Rani (Assam). "Vulture restaurants" — controlled feeding stations where safe, diclofenac-free carcasses are provided — are a community-based conservation tool to supplement wild food supply. Rajasthan's Jatayu Conservation Breeding Centre is one of the largest.</li>
<li><strong>Wildlife Institute of India (WII):</strong> An autonomous body under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, based in Dehradun. WII conducts research on wildlife ecology and conservation and played a key role in identifying diclofenac as the cause of vulture deaths. The author of the Dehradun electrocution study is affiliated with WII.</li>
<li><strong>Electrocution of Wildlife:</strong> High-tension power lines and poorly insulated distribution infrastructure kill hundreds of endangered birds including vultures, eagles and storks globally. Solutions include retrofitting power lines with insulated/bird-diverting equipment, and as this study shows, relocating wildlife attractants away from high-risk infrastructure. India's National Action Plan for Vulture Conservation (revised 2020) lists electrocution among priority threats requiring mitigation.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) has proposed red hexagonal warning labels on packaged foods high in sugar, salt or saturated fat, responding to sustained pressure from the Supreme Court. However, nutrition advocate Arun Gupta (convenor of Nutrition Advocacy in Public Interest) has criticised a critical loophole: warnings would only be triggered when a product is high in two or more nutrients of concern — meaning a product high in only sugar (but not fat or salt) would carry no warning at all. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI):</strong> A statutory body under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. It sets standards for food articles, regulates their manufacture, storage, distribution, sale and import, and enforces compliance. The FSSAI's food labelling regulations are contained in the Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020.</li>

Countries like Chile, Mexico and Brazil have pioneered FOPL with positive public health outcomes. <li><strong>Bournvita Controversy (2023–2026):</strong> A food blogger's viral video in 2023 pointed to the high sugar content of Cadbury Bournvita and questioned its marketing as a children's health drink. The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) issued notices, and FSSAI ordered the product's sugar content to be revised. The 2026 action on health claims is the latest chapter in this ongoing regulatory action.</li>
<li><strong>ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines (2024):</strong> The Indian Council of Medical Research — National Institute of Nutrition updated India's dietary guidelines in 2024, providing specific recommended thresholds for total sugar, total fat and salt per 100g of food. These provide a scientific basis for defining HFSS foods. Aligning FSSAI's FOPL framework with these guidelines is a key demand of nutrition advocates.</li>
<li><strong>Supreme Court's Role in Food Regulation:</strong> The Supreme Court has taken suo motu interest in FOPL, directing FSSAI and the Union government to expedite the front-of-pack warning label system. This is consistent with the Court's broader health jurisprudence — framing access to safe, accurately labelled food as an aspect of the right to health under Article 21. The Court's intervention has been the key driver of FSSAI action in 2025–2026.</li>
</ul>

Justice S. <li><strong>Advocates Act, 1961:</strong> The primary legislation governing the legal profession in India. It established the Bar Council of India (BCI) and State Bar Councils. BCI vs. NLU governance tensions arise from NLUs operating outside BCI's affiliating framework.</li>

Pendency is concentrated in District and Sessions Courts. **Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Students of the National Law University Delhi (NLUD) boycotted their annual convocation ceremony in protest against Bar Council of India (BCI) directives that prohibited the University from employing foreign law faculty and from awarding dual degrees — combinations like LLB with BA/BBA that give law graduates broader interdisciplinary credentials. The BCI contends that only qualified advocates (i.e., those enrolled under the Advocates Act, 1961) may teach law subjects, which foreign nationals cannot be. NLUD students and faculty argue that BCI's restrictions undermine academic excellence, limit global integration of Indian legal education, and violate the autonomy of a statutory university. The dispute reflects a wider tension between BCI's statutory gatekeeping role and the aspirations of elite National Law Universities to adopt international best practices. The BCI has historically resisted moves that could dilute its control over the legal profession.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>National Law University Delhi (NLUD):</strong> Established by the National Law University Delhi Act, 2008 (Delhi legislation). It is an autonomous statutory university, distinct from other NLUs (which are established by state legislatures). It offers the BA LLB (Hons.), LLM, and PhD programmes. Its graduates sit CLAT as the common admission test but NLUD also conducts AILET (All India Law Entrance Test) separately.</li>
<li><strong>BCI's Authority over Legal Education:</strong> Under Sections 7(h) and (i) of the Advocates Act, 1961, the BCI has the power to "promote legal education and to lay down standards of such education" and to "recognize universities whose degree in law shall be a qualification for enrolment." This gives BCI leverage to deny recognition — effectively barring graduates from practising — if a university does not comply with its norms.</li>
<li><strong>Foreign Faculty Prohibition:</strong> BCI's Legal Education Rules (as amended) require law teachers to hold Indian law degrees and be eligible for enrolment as advocates in India. This effectively bars foreign nationals from being regular faculty in BCI-recognized law schools. Critics argue this disadvantages Indian legal education relative to global peers like Oxbridge and US law schools that actively recruit across borders.</li>
<li><strong>Dual Degrees in Law:</strong> Integrated dual-degree programmes (e.g., LLB + MBA, or LLB + LLM) combine legal training with another discipline. Some NLUs offer these; BCI has resisted where the combination was seen as diluting the primary law qualification. The debate mirrors global trends: in the US, JD-MBA programmes are common. India's NEP 2020 encourages multidisciplinary programmes, creating a policy tension with BCI conservatism.</li>
<li><strong>NLU-BCI Governance Tension:</strong> NLUs are established by state statutes and governed by their own acts and chancellors (typically the respective state Governor or Chief Justice). They operate with significant academic autonomy. BCI's claim to override NLU rules on faculty and curricula has been contested in courts. The Supreme Court has, in various cases, balanced BCI's statutory mandate with university autonomy under UGC regulations.</li>
</ul>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
A Parliamentary Standing Committee has recommended raising the OBC creamy layer income ceiling from the existing ₹8 lakh per annum to ₹15 lakh, arguing that the current threshold has not kept pace with inflation and has excluded a large section of the truly backward population from reservation benefits in central government jobs and educational institutions. The creamy layer concept was first introduced for OBCs by the Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney vs. Union of India (1992), which held that the more affluent among OBCs should be excluded from reservation benefits to ensure that the least advantaged benefited. The income ceiling has been revised periodically — from ₹1 lakh (1993) to ₹6 lakh (2013) and ₹8 lakh (2017) — but critics argue each revision has lagged inflation. The committee also recommended extending the creamy layer review to be conducted every five years and including cost-of-living indices, not just rural income data, in the calculation.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Creamy Layer Concept — Indra Sawhney vs. Union of India (1992):</strong> The Supreme Court's nine-judge bench upheld 27% reservation for OBCs in central government posts but introduced the "creamy layer" exclusion — individuals whose parents are in higher income/social strata cannot claim OBC reservation. The Court held that creamy layer determination should exclude those who have already advanced socially and economically, leaving benefits for those still backward. The ruling applies to OBCs in central services; states may set their own creamy layer norms for state reservations.</li>
<li><strong>Constitutional Basis — Articles 15(4) and 16(4):</strong> These provisions allow the state to make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes (Article 15(4)) and for reservations in state services for such classes (Article 16(4)). OBCs are identified by the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) — a constitutional body under Article 338B (inserted by the 102nd Amendment, 2018).</li>
<li><strong>Central OBC List and Income Ceiling:</strong> The Office Memorandum defining the creamy layer for central OBC reservations is issued by the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment. The income ceiling currently (as of 2026) stands at ₹8 lakh per annum (last revised in 2017). The calculation is based on combined income of parents from all sources excluding agricultural income. The 7th Pay Commission's salary revisions effectively absorbed many OBC government servants into the creamy layer — a key argument for raising the ceiling.</li>
<li><strong>National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC):</strong> A constitutional body established under Article 338B (102nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 2018), with the mandate to examine complaints regarding non-inclusion or inadequate representation of backward classes. Before the 102nd Amendment, NCBC was a statutory body. Its constitutionalization gave it powers akin to the National SC/ST Commission. The President of India, on the advice of the NCBC and the Union government, can add or remove communities from the Central OBC list.</li>
<li><strong>Sub-categorisation within OBCs — E.V. Chinnaiah to Pankaj Kumar (2024):</strong> In August 2024, a seven-judge Constitution Bench in Pankaj Kumar Tripathi vs. State of Punjab overruled the 2004 E.V. Chinnaiah ruling and held that states can sub-categorise OBCs within the 27% to give preferential treatment to the most backward sub-groups. This significantly changes the architecture of OBC reservations and intersects with creamy layer debates: a ₹15 lakh ceiling could reopen sub-categorisation to allow the most deprived OBC communities to access a larger share of benefits.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Can an accused person be denied access to their FIR?
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Polity</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 8 · FAQ</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper II → Polity → Fundamental Rights; Article 22; criminal procedure; rights of accused
- GS Paper II → Governance → Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS); police powers; transparency in criminal justice

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The FAQ explains the legal position on whether an accused can be denied a copy of the First Information Report (FIR) filed against them. Under the old Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC), and now under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) 2023 which replaced it, an accused has the right to receive a free copy of the FIR. The Supreme Court in Youth Bar Association of India vs. Union of India (2016) also directed that FIRs should be uploaded online within 24 hours of registration unless they concern sensitive areas (AFSPA regions, sexual offences). However, police sometimes deny FIR copies at the initial stage, citing ongoing investigation or the "looked-out notice" exception for cognisable offences. The article clarifies that while Section 154(2) BNSS (old Section 154(2) CrPC) entitles complainants to free copies, accused persons can get copies through the court — particularly at the chargesheet/trial stage — and through RTI if the matter is not sub judice. BNSS Section 173 also now mandates electronic service of documents to accused persons, strengthening procedural transparency.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>First Information Report (FIR) — Legal Basis:</strong> An FIR is a written document prepared by police upon receiving information about a cognisable offence. It sets the criminal law in motion and gives police power to arrest without a warrant. Registered under Section 173 of the BNSS, 2023 (formerly Section 154 CrPC, 1973), it must be read to the informant, signed, and a copy given to the informant free of charge.</li>
<li><strong>Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023:</strong> Replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC) from July 1, 2024. Key changes relevant to FIRs and accused rights: mandatory video recording of searches, time-bound investigations (chargesheet within 60–90 days), and electronic delivery of summons and documents. The BNSS retains most CrPC provisions on FIR access but adds digital transparency requirements.</li>
<li><strong>Youth Bar Association vs. Union of India (2016):</strong> The Supreme Court held that FIRs must be uploaded on the police station's or state police's website within 24 hours of registration — accessible to the accused and public — unless they relate to sensitive areas, offences against women and children, or where uploading would compromise an ongoing investigation. This set a nationwide standard for FIR transparency, though compliance has been uneven.</li>
<li><strong>Article 22 — Rights of Arrested Persons:</strong> Article 22(1) guarantees that no person arrested shall be detained in custody without being informed of the grounds of arrest, and they shall not be denied the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner. These rights intersect with FIR access: an accused cannot meaningfully exercise the right to a lawyer without knowing the charges — i.e., the FIR content. Courts have read FIR access as implicit in Article 22(1).</li>
<li><strong>Cognisable vs. Non-Cognisable Offences:</strong> A cognisable offence allows police to arrest without a magistrate's warrant; a non-cognisable offence requires prior judicial approval. FIRs are only registered for cognisable offences (Schedule I, BNSS). For non-cognisable offences, a Complaint Case is filed before a Magistrate. This distinction matters for FIR access: for cognisable offences the accused is entitled to a copy; for non-cognisable cases the procedural route differs.</li>
</ul>
</div>

Meta Platforms agreed to pay approximately $17.1 billion (£13.4 billion) to settle a UK class-action competition case brought by a consumer group on behalf of over 45 million UK Facebook users. The case alleged that Meta abused its dominant market position in social media by using personal data collected on Facebook to lock users in and deny them a fair choice — thereby reducing competition and consumer welfare. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Competition Law and Data Markets:</strong> Traditional competition law focuses on price effects. In data-driven markets (social media, search, e-commerce), the "product" is often free — users pay with their data. Regulators have shifted to analysing "zero-price markets" by measuring harm through reduced quality, less privacy, and foreclosed innovation rather than higher prices. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) and the EU's Digital Markets Act (DMA, 2023) are pioneering this framework.</li>
<li><strong>Digital Markets Act (EU, 2023):</strong> A landmark regulation imposing ex-ante obligations on "gatekeeper" platforms (those with very large user bases and market cap thresholds). Gatekeepers must allow interoperability, not self-preference their own services, and not combine personal data from different services without explicit consent. Meta, Google, Apple, Amazon, Microsoft and ByteDance have been designated gatekeepers. Violations attract fines up to 10% of global annual turnover.</li>
<li><strong>Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 — India:</strong> Extended CCI's jurisdiction to cover mergers by value (not just asset/turnover), strengthened investigation timelines, and introduced provisions for "settlement and commitments" (allowing companies to negotiate remedies with CCI). Crucially, it enables CCI to regulate data-driven anti-competitive practices — relevant because CCI has already investigated Google (paid ₹1,337 crore penalty in 2023 in the Android case) and is scrutinising Amazon and Flipkart.</li>
<li><strong>Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA):</strong> India's first comprehensive data protection law. It establishes the Data Protection Board, requires explicit consent for personal data processing, grants data principals the right to correction and erasure, and imposes penalties up to ₹250 crore per instance of violation. For Big Tech cases, the DPDPA and the CCI's competition framework create overlapping regulatory jurisdiction on how user data is collected and monetised.</li>
<li><strong>Meta's FTC Case (US):</strong> The FTC's antitrust suit against Meta (Federal Trade Commission vs. Meta Platforms, ongoing) challenges Meta's acquisitions of Instagram (2012, $1 billion) and WhatsApp (2014, $19 billion) as anti-competitive "buy or bury" acquisitions intended to neutralise competitive threats to Facebook rather than to integrate complementary products. The case tests whether platform acquisitions approved at the time (by a different FTC standard) can retroactively be challenged under Sherman Antitrust Act Section 2 monopolisation. A ruling against Meta could require divestiture of Instagram or WhatsApp.</li>
</ul>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Bhotekoshi floods: Why Himalayan rivers are becoming more dangerous
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Environment</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Disaster Management</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 9 · Profiles</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Environment → Climate change and extreme weather; Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs); Himalayan ecology
- GS Paper III → Disaster Management → Flood disaster risk; early warning systems; transboundary disaster cooperation

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The profile analyses why the Bhotekoshi (Bhote Koshi) river — a tributary of the Sapt Koshi originating in Tibet — has become increasingly prone to catastrophic flash floods. On August 29–30, 2026, extreme rainfall over the Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR) combined with glacial melt to trigger a flash flood that swept through Nepal's Sindhupalchok district and caused downstream flooding in Uttarakhand's Pithoragarh district. The article explains the mechanics of Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs): as temperatures rise, glacial lakes grow in size and the ice-dams or moraine-dams holding them back become structurally unstable. A triggered outburst releases stored water volumes that can exceed the river's bank capacity within minutes. The Bhotekoshi is particularly vulnerable because it flows through a steep gorge, amplifying flood velocity. India-Nepal-China transboundary data-sharing on glacier monitoring remains inadequate — a recurring gap in disaster preparedness. Early warning systems exist in Nepal's Koshi basin but real-time data from TAR-side glaciers is not shared by China.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs):</strong> Sudden release of water impounded by a glacial lake, caused by failure of the ice-dam, moraine-dam, or buried ice supporting the lake. GLOFs generate walls of water and debris moving at high velocity — often exceeding river channels many times over. The Hindu Kush Himalaya (HKH) region holds the largest concentration of glaciers outside the poles and is warming faster than the global average, increasing GLOF frequency. India has 7,500+ glaciers, concentrated in Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand.</li>
<li><strong>Bhotekoshi River and Sapt Koshi System:</strong> The Bhotekoshi (also called Bhote Koshi or Tamba Koshi) originates in Tibet and joins the Sapt Koshi in Nepal's Sindhupalchok district. The Koshi — a major left-bank tributary of the Ganga — drains parts of Tibet, Nepal, and Bihar. Its 2008 breach (Koshi floods, Bihar) displaced ~2.5 million people. The river system's upper reaches in Tibet are outside India-Nepal monitoring networks, making early warning for downstream India difficult.</li>
<li><strong>Transboundary Disaster Data Sharing — India-China gap:</strong> India and China have limited formal mechanisms for real-time hydrological data sharing on trans-boundary rivers. Under the 2002 Memorandum of Understanding on the provision of hydrological data, China shares flood-season data on the Sutlej and Brahmaputra (June–October). China suspended this data sharing in 2017 after Doklam and restored it partially in 2022. No similar arrangement exists for glacier monitoring data from TAR, leaving Nepal and India without advance warning of glacial lake formation or dam instability.</li>
<li><strong>National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) and GLOFs:</strong> NDMA released Guidelines on Management of Glacial Lake Outburst Floods in 2020. Key recommendations: satellite-based monitoring of glacial lakes (using ISRO's resources), community-based early warning in affected valleys, and multi-hazard approach combining earthquake and GLOF risk (the Himalayas are seismically active). The Space Applications Centre (SAC), Ahmedabad, has mapped over 5,000 glacial lakes in India.</li>
<li><strong>Climate Change and Himalayan Glaciers:</strong> The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2021–22) and the HKH Assessment (ICIMOD, 2019) project that even under 1.5°C warming, one-third of the HKH's glaciers will be lost by 2100; under 3°C, two-thirds. Glacial melt contributes to river flow in the short term (higher flows) but will cause "peak water" decline in decades — less seasonal water for 200 million+ people dependent on glacier-fed rivers in the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra basins.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">

<li><strong>Kurdish Question and NATO:</strong> Turkiye is a NATO member but has frequently clashed with Western allies over the Kurdish issue — most visibly by blocking Sweden and Finland's NATO membership applications (2022–2023) until those countries agreed to extradite PKK-linked individuals. A genuine Turkish-PKK peace settlement could ease these tensions and improve Turkey's strategic coherence within NATO at a time when the alliance is focused on Russia and the eastern flank.</li>
<li><strong>Implications for India:</strong> India has no direct stake in the Turkiye-PKK conflict but closely watches West Asian security dynamics. India-Turkiye relations are complex: both are members of the G20 but Turkiye has supported Pakistan on Kashmir. A stable, reconciled Turkiye would be a more predictable partner in multilateral forums (OIC, G20, UN). India's approach to counter-insurgency and peace processes — as studied by UPSC — draws on comparative examples including the Naga Peace Accord process and Sri Lanka's Tamil Eelam conflict.</li>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The article explains the discipline of climate attribution science — the field that estimates how much climate change increases the probability or intensity of a specific extreme weather event — and highlights its methodological limits. Using the Nepal floods and Uttarakhand cloudburst of August 2026 as examples, the article explains why attribution scientists cannot always produce timely results: attribution requires a long historical baseline of climate observations (often absent in data-sparse regions like the Himalayas), adequate climate model ensemble runs, and careful separation of land-use and anthropogenic warming signals. The World Weather Attribution (WWA) group — which produced the landmark study attributing the 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome — typically takes weeks to months to produce peer-reviewed results. The article highlights "rapid attribution" as a growing sub-field: preliminary probabilistic estimates published within days of a disaster. However, rapid attribution for complex topographic regions (mountain valleys, monsoon zones) is methodologically contentious because local factors (deforestation, glacier dynamics, land subsidence) interact with global warming signals in non-linear ways.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Climate Attribution Science:</strong> A branch of climate science that uses statistical and modelling approaches to estimate whether, and by how much, anthropogenic climate change altered the probability or magnitude of a specific weather event. The core methodology compares: (a) the actual observed climate (with human-caused warming) against (b) a "counterfactual" climate derived from models without anthropogenic forcing. If an event was, say, 5× more likely in the actual climate, that is the attribution statement. Key institutions: World Weather Attribution (WWA, Imperial College London / Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute), Lawrence Berkeley National Lab (US).</li>
<li><strong>Fraction of Attributable Risk (FAR):</strong> The main metric in attribution studies. FAR = (P_actual − P_counterfactual) / P_actual, where P is probability of the event. FAR of 0.9 means 90% of the event's probability is attributable to human-caused warming. This metric has been used in high-profile attribution studies — the 2022 Pakistan floods (FAR ~0.75 for extreme rainfall), 2021 European floods, and India's 2023 Sikkim GLOF.</li>
<li><strong>IPCC AR6 and Observed Extremes:</strong> The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (WG1, 2021) stated with high confidence that human influence has increased the frequency and intensity of hot extremes, heavy precipitation events, and marine heatwaves globally. The Report introduced the concept of "compound events" — simultaneous or successive hazards (e.g., drought followed by wildfire, or heavy rain on saturated soil) — as a particularly dangerous and underanalysed risk category. Himalayan compound events (GLOF + extreme rainfall) are precisely such events.</li>
<li><strong>Data Scarcity in Himalayan Attribution:</strong> Climate attribution requires dense, long observational records. The Himalayas are among the most data-sparse major mountain systems — few weather stations above 3,000m, short records (often post-1950), and significant spatial heterogeneity. This creates what scientists call the "Third Pole observation gap." India's ISRO and the Ministry of Earth Sciences have launched high-altitude AWS (Automatic Weather Station) networks to address this, but data gaps persist for real-time attribution.</li>
<li><strong>Loss and Damage — Attribution and Climate Finance:</strong> At COP27 (2022), nations agreed to establish a "Loss and Damage" fund for climate-vulnerable countries. Attribution science is critical to operationalising this fund — to determine which losses were caused by climate change and therefore merit compensation from historical emitters. The methodological limits highlighted in this article have direct implications: if attribution cannot conclusively establish climate causation for specific events in data-sparse regions, it creates legal and political obstacles to Loss and Damage claims by developing nations including India.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 style="background:#1E2D3D;color:#fff;padding:12px 18px;border-radius:7px 7px 0 0;margin:28px 0 0 0;font-size:1.05em;">
  Who owns art created by AI diffusion models — and can it be copyrighted?
</h2>
<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">
  <span style="background:#F0EBF6;color:#5B2C8D;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Science & Technology</span>
  <span style="background:#FFF0E6;color:#B84020;font-size:0.72em;font-weight:700;padding:2px 9px;border-radius:3px;margin-right:4px;">Governance</span>
  <span style="color:#8A8278;font-size:0.9em;margin-left:8px;">Page 10 · Science</span>
</p>

**UPSC Mains Classification**
- GS Paper III → Science & Technology → Artificial Intelligence; generative AI; diffusion models; intellectual property in AI
- GS Paper II → Governance → Digital regulation; copyright law; data protection; AI policy

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The article explains diffusion models — the AI architecture underlying image generators like Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, and Midjourney — and the unresolved legal question of copyright ownership in AI-generated works. Diffusion models are trained on billions of human-created images scraped from the internet, raising three distinct legal questions: (1) Does training on copyrighted images without permission constitute infringement? (2) Can the output — an AI-generated image — be copyrighted by the user who prompted it? (3) Who bears liability if the output closely resembles a copyrighted work? In the US, the Copyright Office has held that purely AI-generated content (without substantial human authorship) cannot be copyrighted, but works where a human exercised creative control over specific choices can be. Courts have split on the training-data question. In India, the Copyright Act, 1957 does not explicitly address AI authorship — the "author" must be a "person," and whether a juristic person (company owning the AI) can be the author of AI-generated work is unsettled. The article argues India needs an AI-copyright policy before litigation forces an ad-hoc judicial answer.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Diffusion Models — How They Work:</strong> A class of generative AI models that learn to create images by reversing a diffusion process: during training, noise is progressively added to images until they become random; the model learns to denoise step-by-step. During generation, starting from random noise, the model applies learned denoising to produce a coherent image matching a text prompt. Key models: Stable Diffusion (open-source, Stability AI), DALL-E (OpenAI), Midjourney (Midjourney Inc.), Imagen (Google). Unlike GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks), diffusion models produce higher quality, more diverse outputs.</li>
<li><strong>Training Data and Copyright — US Cases:</strong> Multiple lawsuits have been filed in the US: Getty Images vs. Stability AI (training on Getty's watermarked images), Andersen vs. Stability AI (class action by illustrators), and The New York Times vs. OpenAI (text, not images). In 2025, the US District Court for the Northern District of California dismissed some claims in the Andersen case, ruling that using copyrighted images for AI training may constitute "transformative use" under the fair use doctrine — but the cases are on appeal. No definitive US Supreme Court ruling exists yet on AI training data copyright.</li>
<li><strong>India's Copyright Act, 1957 — Gap on AI Authorship:</strong> Section 2(d) of the Copyright Act defines "author" for different works — for literary works it is the person who creates it; for computer-generated works it is "the person who causes the work to be created." The Explanation to Section 2(d) covers "computer-generated" works and vests authorship in the person who caused the work to be made. This could arguably cover AI-generated works if a human operator "caused" the generation. However, no court has definitively applied this to generative AI outputs. India's National IPR Policy (2016) and the Draft National AI Strategy (NITI Aayog) do not address AI copyright explicitly.</li>
<li><strong>EU AI Act (2024) and Copyright:</strong> The EU AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024, requires general-purpose AI (GPAI) models to comply with EU copyright law and publish summaries of training data. This is the first binding regulation addressing AI training data transparency. Separately, the EU Copyright Directive (2019) created a "text and data mining" exception allowing AI training on publicly available data for research purposes but not for commercial AI products without rightholder consent. This creates a framework India may reference in its own AI governance.</li>
<li><strong>India's AI Policy Context:</strong> India released its National Strategy for AI (NITI Aayog, 2018) and IndiaAI Mission (2024, ₹10,372 crore outlay). The Digital India Act (in draft, 2023–2025) proposes to replace the IT Act, 2000, and includes provisions on AI regulation — but copyright in AI outputs remains outside its current scope. The Ministry of Commerce's Copyright Office has been examining AI authorship through consultations. India's approach is expected to balance creative industry interests (Bollywood, music, digital art) against AI innovation promotion, similar to how the NEP 2020 balanced technology and traditional learning.</li>
</ul>
</div>

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
HDFC Bank's Managing Director and CEO Sashidhar Jagdishan announced his intention to step down at the completion of his current term, prompting the bank's board to initiate a formal succession process. Jagdishan, who became CEO in October 2020 succeeding Aditya Puri, oversaw the landmark merger of HDFC Ltd. The CEO transition comes at a critical time: HDFC Bank is in the midst of integrating the two balance sheets, managing a spike in NPA (non-performing asset) ratios at the merged entity, and navigating RBI's directive to reduce its loan-to-deposit ratio. HDFC Bank is the largest private sector bank in India by assets.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>HDFC Bank — Background and HDFC Merger:</strong> HDFC Bank was established in 1994, one of the first private sector banks under the post-liberalisation RBI licensing round. It consistently ranked among India's most profitable banks. HDFC Ltd. (Housing Development Finance Corporation), founded in 1977, was India's largest housing finance company and HDFC Bank's parent. The reverse merger — HDFC Ltd. merging into HDFC Bank — was completed in July 2023 creating a combined entity with ~₹25 lakh crore balance sheet, making it the fourth-largest bank in Asia by market capitalisation.</li>
<li><strong>RBI's Role in Bank CEO Appointments:</strong> Under Section 35B of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, the appointment, reappointment or termination of a bank's MD/CEO requires prior RBI approval. RBI applies "fit and proper" criteria (RBI Circular, 2019) — integrity, track record, financial soundness, and absence of conflicts of interest. RBI also caps private bank CEO terms at 15 years with the same bank and mandates that no individual serves as both Chairman and MD/CEO simultaneously (to separate governance from management).</li>
<li><strong>NPA (Non-Performing Asset) Framework:</strong> A bank loan becomes an NPA when interest or principal payments are overdue for more than 90 days. NPAs are classified into Sub-Standard, Doubtful, and Loss assets. RBI mandates provisioning against NPAs — banks must set aside a percentage of the loan value. Post-HDFC merger, the merged entity's NPA ratios elevated temporarily due to the integration of HDFC Ltd.'s legacy loan book. This is being closely watched by RBI as it affects systemic stability given HDFC Bank's size.</li>
<li><strong>Loan-to-Deposit Ratio (LDR) — RBI Concern:</strong> RBI has flagged high LDR at several private banks (loans significantly exceeding deposits), warning of liquidity risk. HDFC Bank's LDR spiked post-merger because HDFC Ltd. was funded largely through bonds/NCDs (not retail deposits), while bank regulation requires loan growth to be backed by stable deposits. RBI's informal directive to HDFC Bank to improve its LDR is driving a strategic shift: higher deposit mobilisation, slower loan growth, and potential asset sales — all of which the new CEO will need to manage.</li>
<li><strong>Aditya Puri's Succession Legacy (2020):</strong> Aditya Puri led HDFC Bank from its founding in 1994 to October 2020 — a record 26-year tenure at a single bank (exceptional under post-2019 RBI term-cap norms, which did not apply retrospectively). His succession in 2020 was cited as a model for managed CEO transitions in Indian banking. The current Jagdishan succession will be watched as a test of whether Indian private banks can institutionalise leadership beyond founder-CEOs.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;padding:4px 18px 6px 18px;background:#f4f6f8;border-radius:0 0 5px 5px;font-size:0.82em;">

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
Following the August 29–30, 2026 flash floods that damaged several hydroelectric projects in Nepal's Sindhupalchok and Kaski districts, Nepal's electricity authority requested emergency power imports from India. India's Power Grid Corporation facilitated the supply of 100 MW through existing cross-border interconnections, enabling Nepal to manage the shortfall in generation capacity caused by flood damage to run-of-river plants. The episode illustrates both the fragility of Nepal's flood-exposed hydroelectric infrastructure and the deepening India-Nepal energy relationship. **Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>India-Nepal Power Trade Framework:</strong> The Power Trade Agreement (PTA) between India and Nepal (2014, amended 2021) provides the framework for bilateral electricity trade. Nepal can export power to India through designated cross-border interconnections (Muzaffarpur-Dhalkebar, Gorakhpur-Butwal, and others). Nepal sells seasonal surplus (monsoon/post-monsoon hydropower) to India and imports during winter/dry seasons. In 2023, Nepal exported ~750 MW to India during peak hydro months.</li>
<li><strong>Nepal's Hydroelectric Potential vs. Developed Capacity:</strong> Nepal has a theoretical hydroelectric potential of ~83,000 MW and an economically feasible potential of ~42,000 MW — among the highest per capita in the world. Installed capacity (2025) is approximately 3,200 MW, meaning less than 8% of the potential is developed. Nepal's Vision 2030 targets 15,000 MW installed capacity. India is investing in several major Nepal hydro projects — Pancheshwar Multipurpose Project (5,040 MW), Arun-3 (900 MW, SJVN Ltd.) and others.</li>
<li><strong>Run-of-River (ROR) Hydroelectric Projects:</strong> Nepal's installed capacity is dominated by run-of-river projects — those that use the natural flow of a river without significant water storage (unlike large reservoir dams). ROR projects are environmentally less intrusive but are directly vulnerable to flash floods (which carry debris that damages turbines, penstocks and intakes) and to seasonal flow variability (low output in winter). The August 2026 flash floods represent a classic ROR vulnerability scenario.</li>

<li><strong>SAARC/BIMSTEC Regional Power Grid:</strong> A vision for a South Asia-wide synchronised power grid that would allow cross-border trade of electricity among all regional nations — enabling Bangladesh to buy Bhutanese hydropower routed through India, or Nepal to sell surplus to Sri Lanka via India. **UPSC Mains Classification**

**Summary**
<div style="background:#EDF4F2;border-left:4px solid #4A8A78;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:10px 0 6px 0;">
The Karnataka government's proposal to introduce toll fees on major arterial roads within Bengaluru — as a funding mechanism for infrastructure maintenance and expansion — has ignited a public debate on urban road financing. The proposal follows years of underfunding of the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP), which lacks the financial resources to maintain Bengaluru's ~13,000 km road network to acceptable standards. Proponents argue that user charges are the most sustainable and equitable funding model — those who use the infrastructure pay for it, reducing the burden on the city's general tax revenue. Critics counter that: (a) urban arterial roads are public goods that should not be tolled; (b) tolling will disproportionately burden lower-income commuters; (c) Bengaluru already has high income-tax payers who fund national and state governments without receiving commensurate urban services. The article contextualises this within the broader problem of urban fiscal gap — Indian cities collectively generate only about 1% of GDP in own tax revenue against international urban average of 3–4%.
</div>

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>BBMP — Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike:</strong> Bengaluru's municipal corporation, covering approximately 800 sq km and a population of ~13 million (making it one of India's largest urban local bodies). BBMP is responsible for road maintenance, solid waste management, storm-water drains, and local social services. Its annual budget (~₹10,000 crore in 2024-25) is inadequate for a global tech city's infrastructure needs. BBMP has been in a state of administrative dysfunction — elections have not been held since 2020, and it has been run by an administrator.</li>
<li><strong>Urban Fiscal Gap in India:</strong> The 15th Finance Commission recognised India's severe urban fiscal gap and earmarked grants for urban local bodies — ₹26,909 crore annually (2021–2026) for cities over 1 million population, tied to performance metrics including property tax reforms. However, Indian ULBs (Urban Local Bodies) remain heavily dependent on state grants and devolution, with weak own-revenue generation. Property tax — the primary own-tax source of cities — is grossly underlevied in most Indian cities due to political resistance to valuation revision.</li>
<li><strong>Congestion Pricing vs. Road Tolling:</strong> Two distinct models are being discussed in India. Congestion pricing charges vehicles for entering congested urban zones (akin to London's congestion charge) — revenue is reinvested in public transit. Road tolling charges a fee for using a specific road (BOT/BOOT model). Bengaluru's proposal is for road tolling. Singapore and London are global models for congestion pricing that successfully shifted commuters to public transport. Delhi-NCR's expressway tolling is the Indian model of road tolling for high-quality inter-city roads.</li>
<li><strong>PPP in Urban Infrastructure — BOT / TOT Models:</strong> Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) and Toll-Operate-Transfer (TOT) are PPP models for infrastructure financing. In BOT, a private player builds and operates a road for a concession period (recovering investment through tolls) and transfers it back. In TOT, an existing government asset is monetised — the government gives operational rights to a private player for a lump sum. NITI Aayog's National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP) has included urban road assets for TOT monetisation as a way to raise capital for new infrastructure.</li>
<li><strong>15th Finance Commission and Urban Bodies:</strong> The 15th Finance Commission (2021–2026) introduced performance-based grants for ULBs, linking transfers to property tax coverage, fire safety, and sanitation outcomes. It also recommended creating Metropolitan Governance Frameworks for million-plus cities (including Bengaluru). The Bengaluru Metropolitan Land Transport Authority (BMLTA) was set up to coordinate transport planning across the city-region — but fiscal powers for road development remain fragmented among BBMP, BMRCL (metro), BWSSB (water), BMTC (buses), and the state PWD.</li>
</ul>

Security forces have established permanent forward operating bases in the Karreguttalu Hills — a forested ridge along Telangana's Mulugu-Bhadradri Kothagudem district boundary that had been a Maoist stronghold for decades. The operation follows a multi-year campaign combining special forces (Greyhounds, CoBRA battalions) operations, intelligence-based targeted encounters, and road/infrastructure development to break the geographical sanctuary. Karreguttalu Hills — part of the larger Bastar-Abujhmad-Dandakaranya forest complex — was used by the CPI (Maoist) as a training and logistics base straddling the Telangana-Chhattisgarh border. The article notes that LWE-affected districts have declined from 90 in 2010 to under 20 in 2026 — one of the most significant counter-insurgency achievements in recent Indian history. The Telangana government has announced infrastructure development and tribal welfare schemes for the newly secured areas under the PVTG (Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups) mission and PM JANMAN scheme.

**Background Knowledge**
<div style="background:#F9F3E8;border-left:4px solid #B07030;border-radius:0 5px 5px 0;padding:12px 16px;margin:6px 0 16px 0;">
<ul>
<li><strong>Left Wing Extremism (LWE) — Background:</strong> LWE in India refers primarily to CPI (Maoist) — also called Naxalites — an armed Communist insurgency that emerged in the Naxalbari movement (West Bengal, 1967). The CPI (Maoist) operates in the "Red Corridor" — forested tribal districts of Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Maharashtra, and West Bengal. At its peak (~2010), LWE affected 90+ districts. The movement is designated as a terrorist organisation under UAPA. Its stated goal: overthrow of the state through armed struggle, in the name of tribal/peasant rights.</li>
<li><strong>SAMADHAN Strategy (2017):</strong> The Union government's comprehensive counter-LWE strategy launched by MHA in 2017. SAMADHAN is an acronym: Smart Leadership, Aggressive Strategy, Motivation and Training, Actionable Intelligence, Dashboard-based KPIs, Harnessing Technology, Action Plan for Each Theatre, No access to Financing. It combines security operations (anti-Maoist ops), governance delivery (infrastructure, education, health in LWE areas), and economic development. The strategy replaced earlier reactive approaches with a time-bound, performance-monitored framework.</li>
<li><strong>CoBRA Battalions and Greyhounds:</strong> CoBRA (Commando Battalion for Resolute Action) are specialised anti-LWE units of the CRPF (Central Reserve Police Force), trained in jungle warfare and guerrilla tactics. Greyhounds is Andhra Pradesh/Telangana's elite anti-Naxal police force — widely credited as the most effective state-level counter-Naxal unit in India, responsible for dismantling Maoist networks in AP's Nallamala forest and Telangana's Dandakaranya fringe. Their jungle-combat skills and intelligence networks have made them a model for other state police forces.</li>
<li><strong>PVTG Mission and PM JANMAN Scheme:</strong> Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) are 75 tribal communities identified by the government as the most marginalised — with declining populations, pre-agriculture economy, and geographic isolation. The PM-JANMAN (PM Janjati Adivasi Nyaya Maha Abhiyan, 2023) is a ₹24,000-crore scheme to provide infrastructure (housing, road connectivity, mobile medical units) specifically in PVTG habitations — many of which overlap with LWE-affected areas. The rationale: development fills the governance vacuum that Maoists exploit. Infrastructure in Karreguttalu Hills serves a dual purpose — connecting tribal communities and denying Maoists a sanctuary.</li>
<li><strong>Decline of LWE — Structural Factors:</strong> The reduction from 90 to under 20 LWE-affected districts (2010–2026) reflects multiple structural factors: death/surrender of top Maoist cadres in security operations; expansion of road, mobile phone, and banking connectivity into forest areas (reducing isolation that sustained Maoist recruitment); improved PVTG welfare reducing tribal alienation; intelligence-sharing frameworks (multi-state anti-LWE coordination through MHA's LWE Division); and loss of Maoist support from sympathetic intellectuals and urban networks following UAPA crackdowns. The residual Maoist presence is confined to the Abujhmad core in Bastar (Chhattisgarh) — India's most remote forest zone.</li>
</ul>
